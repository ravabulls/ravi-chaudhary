**Book:** An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations
**Author:** Adam Smith
**In one line:** A nation grows rich when people specialise and trade freely - and self-interested exchange, guided as if by an invisible hand, coordinates a whole economy without anyone in charge.

## Three things to remember

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="1 · Division of labour" icon="setting">
    Splitting work into specialised tasks multiplies output enormously - Smith's pin factory makes far more per worker than each working alone. Specialisation is limited by the size of the market.
  </Card>
  <Card title="2 · The invisible hand" icon="random">
    People chasing their own gain are led, as if by an invisible hand, to serve society. Prices and markets coordinate a complex economy with no central planner.
  </Card>
  <Card title="3 · Self-interest drives exchange" icon="heart">
    We get our dinner not from the butcher's kindness but from his regard for his own interest. Mutual benefit, not benevolence, is the engine of trade.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## The big idea

Written in 1776, this is the founding text of modern economics. Smith's core claim is that the wealth of a nation is not its hoard of gold but the productive output of its people - and that output rises fastest when individuals are free to specialise, exchange, and pursue their own advantage. Left to compete, self-interested actors are steered by prices toward outcomes that, unintentionally, benefit everyone. Its insights still frame how economists think about markets and policy.

## The key ideas

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Specialisation and the market" icon="setting">
    The division of labour drives productivity, but it is limited by the extent of the market - wider trade allows deeper specialisation, which in turn creates more wealth to trade.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Prices as coordination" icon="random">
    No one plans the economy, yet bread appears daily. Prices signal scarcity and demand, guiding effort and capital to where they are most valued.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Free trade over mercantilism" icon="approve-check">
    Smith attacks mercantilism, monopolies, and protective tariffs. Open competition and free trade, at home and between nations, raise everyone's wealth.
  </Card>
  <Card title="A limited but real government" icon="information">
    Markets need a state for defence, the administration of justice, and public works and institutions that private profit will not supply.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Suspicion of business" icon="warning">
    Smith is no cheerleader for merchants. People of the same trade, meeting together, tend to end in a conspiracy against the public - so he distrusts collusion and captured regulation.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Concern for workers" icon="open-book">
    A society cannot be flourishing and happy if most of its members are poor. Smith cared about the wellbeing of ordinary labourers, not only owners.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Follow the incentives.** When a market puzzles you, ask what self-interest each actor is really responding to - the answer usually explains the outcome better than good or bad intentions.

2. **Widen the market to specialise.** Growth often comes from reaching more buyers, which makes finer division of labour worthwhile. Look for where a bigger market unlocks it.

3. **Be wary of "for your protection."** Tariffs and monopolies are often dressed as public good but serve the protected few. Ask who actually benefits.

4. **Watch for conspiracy against the public.** Treat industry consensus and self-written rules with suspicion, exactly as Smith did.

5. **Reserve government for what markets skip.** Defence, justice, and shared infrastructure are legitimate public roles - draw the line there, not everywhere.

</Steps>

## Lines worth keeping

> *"It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest."*

> *Individuals seeking their own gain are led, as if by an invisible hand, to promote an end that was no part of their intention.*

> *People of the same trade seldom meet together, but the conversation ends in a conspiracy against the public.*

> *The division of labour is limited by the extent of the market.*
