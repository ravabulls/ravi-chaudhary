**Author:** Yuval Noah Harari

## Three things to remember

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="1 · A new human agenda" icon="rocket">
    For most of history the great enemies were famine, plague and war. Harari's opening claim is that these are now largely **manageable problems, not unavoidable fates**. So ambition turns upward: the 21st-century agenda becomes **immortality, bliss and divinity** - using biotech and AI to upgrade *Homo sapiens* into *Homo Deus*.
  </Card>

  </Card>

## The big idea

This is the forward-looking sequel to *Sapiens*. Where *Sapiens* explained how shared fictions - money, nations, gods, human rights - let strangers cooperate at scale, *Homo Deus* asks what those cooperating humans will do next. Harari's wager is that the projects which defined our past are becoming **engineering challenges rather than cosmic conditions**: famine yields to abundance, plague to medicine, war to political choice. When survival is roughly assumed, the species reaches for the old attributes of gods - endless life, controlled happiness, and creative power over life itself.

But the twist is that the same tools promising the upgrade also **corrode the belief system the modern world stands on**. Humanism placed the individual, with their inner feelings and free choices, at the centre of value and knowledge. Biotech and machine learning start to treat those feelings as **biochemical algorithms** that can be measured, predicted and hacked from the outside. Once an external system knows you better than you know yourself, "follow your heart" stops being liberating advice and becomes a data point.

Read the whole thing as **provocative forecasting, not prophecy**. Harari is explicit that these are possibilities meant to widen our sense of the options - scenarios to steer by, not fates to accept.

## The heart of it

<Steps>

1. **The old agenda is (mostly) solved.** Famine, plague and war ruled the human imagination for millennia and are now, in relative terms, under management - more people die from obesity than starvation, from old age than infectious disease, from suicide than soldiers, terrorists and criminals combined. Success frees up ambition and defines a new to-do list.

2. **The new agenda is godhood.** With survival assumed, the targets become **immortality** (treating death as a technical bug), **bliss** (engineering happiness biochemically rather than hoping for it), and **divinity** (acquiring god-like powers of creation and design via genetic engineering and AI). This is the "upgrade" that turns *sapiens* into *deus*.

3. **Humanism gets dethroned.** The upgrade needs a justification, and humanism - "trust your own feelings" - is the reigning one. But if feelings are just algorithms, they can be overridden by better algorithms. The authority that humanism located inside the individual begins migrating **outward, to data-processing systems** that judge more reliably than the human gut.

4. **Intelligence splits from consciousness.** Historically, only conscious beings could do intelligent work, so we assumed the two were the same thing. AI breaks that bundle: it delivers **superhuman intelligence with zero inner experience**. That raises an unsettling question - if the economy and the military want intelligence, and consciousness turns out to be optional, what exactly is the human for?

5. **Dataism arrives to fill the vacuum.** Into the space left by a fading humanism steps **Dataism**: a worldview that sees the whole universe as flows of data and rates every entity - a person, a company, an ideology - by its contribution to **information processing**. Where humanism said "listen to your feelings," Dataism says "listen to the algorithm," and may ultimately value the data flow over the individual human being.

</Steps>

<Aside type="tip" title="The through-line in one breath">
Solve survival, and the goal becomes godhood. Reach for godhood, and you rebuild humans as hackable algorithms. Once humans are hackable and intelligence no longer needs consciousness, the authority humanism kept inside the person leaks out to the data systems - and Dataism, not humanism, becomes the story that explains what matters.
</Aside>

## The key ideas

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Immortality, bliss, divinity" icon="star">
    The three new projects, reframed as **engineering targets**. Death becomes a solvable problem, happiness a biochemical setpoint to tune, creation a matter of design. Why it matters: it reveals ambition and religion did not disappear - they were redirected into the lab.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Organisms are algorithms" icon="random">
    Harari's core mechanical claim: feelings, desires and choices are **biochemical algorithms** shaped by evolution, not a mystical free will. Why it matters: if that is true, they can in principle be **computed, predicted and hacked** - the technical premise beneath every other argument in the book.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Intelligence without consciousness" icon="puzzle">
    A chess engine, a radiology model or a self-driving car can be brilliant while experiencing **nothing**. Why it matters: it dissolves the assumption that only feeling beings can do valuable work, and puts human labour and human authority both in play.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The 'useless class'" icon="down-caret">
    If algorithms absorb economic and military roles, huge numbers of people could become **not exploited but irrelevant** - lacking market or strategic value. Why it matters: it reframes the AI-and-jobs debate from unemployment to **meaning and political weight**, and warns of a small upgraded elite pulling decisively ahead.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Liberalism's sovereign self, challenged" icon="magnifier">
    Liberal democracy, free markets and consumer culture all assume **you know yourself best**. When external algorithms predict your vote, your purchase and your partner better than you do, that assumption - and the institutions resting on it - starts to erode.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Dataism" icon="approve-check">
    The candidate successor to humanism: reality as **data flows**, value measured by contribution to information processing. Why it matters: it is the worldview under which handing decisions to algorithms feels not like surrender but like **progress** - which is exactly what makes it powerful.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Shared fictions, still running" icon="open-book">
    The *Sapiens* engine carries over: money, nations and rights are **inter-subjective stories** that coordinate millions. Why it matters: it explains how a worldview like Dataism could take hold - not by proof, but by becoming the story enough people organise their lives around.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Forecasts as questions" icon="comment">
    Harari repeatedly frames scenarios as **"what would have to be true?"** rather than "this will happen." Why it matters: the value is in the steering, not the fortune-telling - the book is a map of forks, not a timetable.
  </Card>

## How the book unfolds

The argument moves in three broad arcs. **Part one** looks backward and outward: what makes humans different from other animals, and how shared fictions let *sapiens* conquer the planet - a compressed refresher on *Sapiens*. **Part two** examines humanism as the religion that has given the modern world meaning, tracing how "the human experience" became the ultimate authority in politics, economics, art and ethics. **Part three** turns to the future, where new science (organisms as algorithms) and new technology (AI, biotech) put humanism under pressure and open the door to **Dataism**. The tone shifts from history to open-ended speculation as it goes.

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Separate the two axes.** For any AI system, ask *how intelligent* and *how conscious* as **distinct questions**. Conflating them - assuming a smart system must "understand" or "want" - produces both hype and misplaced fear.

2. **Watch what a system optimises for.** Notice when a process quietly prioritises **smoother data flow or engagement** over an individual's actual interests. That gap is Dataism in miniature, visible in your own feeds today.

3. **Audit your algorithmic self-knowledge.** Track where recommendation engines already anticipate your choices better than you'd admit. Treat each one as a measure of **how much authority you have already outsourced** from feelings to algorithms.

4. **Test the "organisms are algorithms" premise.** Much of the book rests on it. Ask where you find it persuasive and where consciousness seems to resist reduction - your answer changes how seriously the conclusions land.

5. **Reframe the jobs question.** Move from "will automation take work?" to "**what gives people value and meaning** when intelligence no longer needs them?" That is the useless-class problem, and it is political before it is technical.

6. **Read forecasts as questions.** Convert each claim into "what would have to be true for this?" Harari offers scenarios to **steer away from**, not destinies to accept.

7. **Pair it with counterweights.** Hold it against thinkers who defend human specialness, embodied cognition, or the limits of current AI, so the provocation sharpens rather than settles your view.

</Steps>

## Worth knowing

A few standing critiques worth carrying alongside the notes. The book is **avowedly speculative** - Harari says so - which means its most striking claims are the least testable, and easy to mistake for predictions. Critics also flag a streak of **technological determinism**: the sense that data and algorithms will simply sweep humanism aside, with human agency, politics and resistance underweighted. The load-bearing premise that **"organisms are algorithms"** is philosophically contested - consciousness may not reduce so cleanly - and if it wobbles, several conclusions wobble with it. Finally, the sweep that makes the book exhilarating also **flattens detail**; treat the big arcs as provocations to investigate, not settled findings.
