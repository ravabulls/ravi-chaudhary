**Book:** Skin in the Game: Hidden Asymmetries in Daily Life
**Author:** Nassim Nicholas Taleb
**In one line:** Never trust advice from someone who pays no price for being wrong - symmetry of consequences is what keeps people honest and systems alive.

---

## Three things to remember

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="1 · Share the downside" icon="warning">
    Anyone who makes a decision should also carry its risk. When you keep the upside and pass the downside to others, you've broken the machinery of fairness and learning.
  </Card>
  <Card title="2 · The minority rules" icon="seti:default">
    A small, stubborn minority that refuses to bend can bend a flexible majority to its preference. This is how whole markets go kosher or halal without a vote.
  </Card>
  <Card title="3 · Survive first" icon="heart">
    Never confuse the average outcome across many people with your own path over time. Avoid anything carrying a chance of irreversible ruin, however small.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## The big idea

Fairness, learning, and functioning systems all run on one hidden mechanism: **symmetry of consequences**. When the person deciding also bears the risk, bad ideas get filtered out fast and words stay honest. When decision and consequence are split - pundits with no stake, bureaucrats untouched by failure, *interventionistas* who bomb distant countries - you get advice that costs its giver nothing and its receiver everything. Judge people and systems not by what they say but by **whether they are exposed to the outcome of their own choices**.

## The key ideas

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Asymmetry is the root of ruin" icon="error">
    Many social ills trace back to one pattern: keep the gains, transfer the losses. Skin in the game restores the symmetry that markets, ethics, and evolution depend on.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The Lindy effect" icon="approve-check">
    For non-perishable things - ideas, books, technologies - time is the best filter. What has survived long is likely to survive longer; age is evidence of robustness.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Via negativa" icon="close">
    You improve most systems by *removing* - harm, fragility, a bad advisor - not by adding. Knowing what to avoid beats knowing what to do.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Ergodicity and ruin" icon="random">
    A game's average payoff is meaningless if one bad round ends you. Survival is the precondition for every other gain, so refuse any exposure to blowup.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Rationality is survival" icon="rocket">
    What looks irrational may be what has kept a group alive for millennia. Rationality is not what sounds clever; it is what does not lead to extinction.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Soul in the game" icon="star">
    Virtue needs courage and real risk-taking. Artisans and entrepreneurs who stake themselves on their work have soul in the game, not just skin.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Ask who pays if this is wrong.** Before taking advice, find the giver's exposure. No downside for them means discount the advice.

2. **Prefer the tested to the shiny.** For durable choices, lean on what has already survived decades - apply the Lindy filter.

3. **Subtract before you add.** Fix problems by removing fragility and bad inputs first; reach for *via negativa* over new complexity.

4. **Refuse ruin at any odds.** Reject any bet with a real chance of a game-ending loss, no matter how attractive the average looks.

5. **Put yourself on the line.** Take positions where you share the consequence - that is what makes your judgment trustworthy to others.

</Steps>
