## The big idea

Orwell's short novella - subtitled *A Fairy Story* - is an allegory of the Russian Revolution and its betrayal under Stalin. Inspired by the dying vision of old Major, the farm animals expel the drunken farmer Mr Jones and found **"Animalism,"** a creed of equality codified in Seven Commandments painted on the barn wall. For a while it works: the harvest is gathered, the animals are fed, and they govern themselves. Then the pigs, the cleverest animals, begin to take the milk and apples "for their own good," and the slide begins.

The engine of the book is not a sudden seizure of power but a slow, deniable corruption. **Napoleon** (a Stalin figure) drives out his rival **Snowball** (a Trotsky figure) using dogs he raised in secret as a private police force, then rules alone. Each broken promise is explained away by the propagandist Squealer, each commandment quietly edited to fit the pigs' growing privileges. The animals work harder and eat less while the pigs move into the farmhouse.

Orwell's target is broader than one dictator. He is showing how any revolution can be hollowed out - how idealism, once it meets the appetite for power and the malleability of memory, curdles into the very tyranny it set out to destroy. The tragedy is that the animals let it happen, one reasonable-sounding compromise at a time.

## The heart of it

<Steps>

1. **The rebellion.** Old Major, a prize boar, tells the animals of a dream of a world without human masters and teaches them the anthem "Beasts of England." Soon after he dies, the underfed animals rise up, drive out Mr Jones, and rename the place Animal Farm. They paint the Seven Commandments of Animalism on the barn wall, ending with "All animals are equal."

2. **The commandments.** The pigs take charge of organising, having taught themselves to read. Almost at once they reserve the milk and windfall apples for themselves, and Squealer explains this is a scientific necessity, not greed. The first small privilege is accepted, and the principle of equality is already cracked.

3. **The power struggle.** Napoleon and Snowball clash over every decision, above all Snowball's plan to build a windmill for electricity. At the height of the debate Napoleon unleashes nine dogs he has secretly reared; they chase Snowball off the farm forever. Napoleon abolishes the animals' assembly and rules by decree, then claims the windmill was his idea all along.

4. **Propaganda and betrayal.** Snowball becomes the scapegoat blamed for every setback, including the windmill's collapse in a storm. Squealer rewrites the past nightly and edits the commandments - "No animal shall drink alcohol" gains "to excess," "No animal shall kill any other animal" gains "without cause." The loyal carthorse **Boxer**, whose mottoes are "I will work harder" and "Napoleon is always right," is worked until he collapses, then sold to the knacker for the price of a case of whisky.

5. **The pigs become the humans.** One by one the commandments vanish until a single line remains: *all animals are equal, but some are more equal than others.* The pigs learn to walk on two legs, carry whips, wear Mr Jones's clothes, and invite neighbouring farmers to dine. In the final scene the other animals look from pig to man and back, and can no longer tell which is which.

</Steps>

<Aside type="tip" title="The allegory map">
Old Major = Marx / Lenin, whose ideas launch the revolution. Napoleon = Stalin; Snowball = Trotsky, driven into exile. Squealer = the state propaganda machine; the dogs = the secret police. Mr Jones = the Tsar; the neighbouring farmers = the surrounding capitalist powers. The farm = the USSR, and the windmill = forced industrialisation and the Five-Year Plans. Boxer = the exploited working masses whose labour is used up and discarded.
</Aside>

## The key ideas

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Squealer and propaganda" icon="pencil">
    The regime's spokesman turns every betrayal into proof of loyalty and every hardship into a necessary sacrifice. Propaganda works not by convincing but by exhausting - repeat a claim confidently enough and resistance feels pointless.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Control of language" icon="document">
    The Seven Commandments are edited word by word until they endorse what they once forbade. Whoever controls the definitions controls what people can even think to object to. Vague, shifting words are a tool of power, not an accident.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Boxer's betrayal" icon="heart">
    The strongest, most devoted worker answers every crisis with more effort and never questions. His reward for a lifetime of loyalty is the knacker's van. Uncritical devotion is exactly the trait the powerful most love to harvest.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The corruption of ideals" icon="warning">
    Animalism begins as genuine liberation and ends as a slogan masking privilege. Ideals do not fail all at once; they are compromised in small, reasonable-sounding steps until nothing is left.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The cycle of tyranny" icon="rocket">
    The pigs do not build a new world - they step into the old master's shoes, whip in hand. Revolution changed the rulers' names but not the structure of domination, which reproduces itself.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The single scapegoat" icon="seti:notebook">
    The exiled Snowball is blamed for every failure, an ever-present enemy who can never defend himself. A permanent scapegoat lets the leadership escape all accountability.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Divide and distract" icon="star">
    While the animals argue, freeze, and starve, the pigs quietly accumulate comfort. Manufactured crises and rivalries keep the ruled too busy to notice who is actually winning.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## How the book unfolds

A dying boar's dream sparks a rebellion; the animals win their farm and write down their equality. The pigs organise, take small privileges, and fall into a power struggle that Napoleon wins by force. From there the arc is a steady descent - propaganda rewrites the past, the commandments are edited, Boxer is used up and discarded, and privilege hardens into a new ruling class. The circle closes when the pigs, now dressed as men and dining with humans, become impossible to tell apart from the tyrants the animals first overthrew.

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Watch what changes on the wall.** When the rules of an institution get quietly reworded, ask who benefits - the edit, not the announcement, is where power moves.

2. **Distrust the single scapegoat.** When every failure is blamed on one absent enemy, someone is using that enemy to dodge accountability.

3. **Notice private force behind public unity.** Loyal "guards" who answer only to the leader are how a movement's ideals get captured.

4. **Protect the record.** Tyranny needs a malleable past. Keep an independent memory of what was actually promised and done, so betrayals cannot be renamed as loyalty.

5. **Question "work harder" as the only answer.** When effort is always the fix and questions are never welcome, devotion is being exploited rather than rewarded.

6. **Beware small, reasonable exceptions.** The milk and apples came first; each compromise that "just makes sense" lowers the bar for the next one.

7. **Judge by outcomes, not slogans.** Equality on the wall means nothing if some eat at the table and others are sold to the knacker. Measure who actually gains.

</Steps>

## Worth knowing

Orwell wrote Animal Farm in 1943-44, while Britain and the Soviet Union were wartime allies - which made an open satire of Stalin politically awkward. Several publishers turned it down for fear of offending Moscow, and it did not appear until August 1945, after the war in Europe had ended. Orwell called it the first book in which he tried, with full consciousness, to fuse political purpose and artistic purpose into one whole. Its deceptively simple fable form is what let a sharp political attack read as a children's story - and outlast the regime it described.
