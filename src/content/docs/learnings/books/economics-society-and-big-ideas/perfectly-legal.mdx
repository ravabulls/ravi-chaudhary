## Three things to remember

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="1 · It's legal by design" icon="document">
    The scandal isn't cheating - it's the *rules themselves*. Those who can afford lawyers, accountants and lobbyists help write and exploit the code, so much of the tilt is **perfectly legal**.

## The big idea

A tax system in a democracy runs on **voluntary compliance** - people pay because they believe the deal is roughly fair. Johnston, an investigative reporter, argues that over decades a quiet, well-funded campaign reshaped the code so the largest incomes and corporations pay proportionally less, while wage-earners who cannot restructure their income pay more. Most of it is legal because the beneficiaries helped write the rules. The deeper cost is not any single loophole but the slow **corrosion of trust** in the fairness the whole system depends on.

## The key ideas

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Rules written by the winners" icon="pencil">
    Wealth buys lawyers, accountants and lobbyists who both **exploit and shape** the code. Effective tax burdens then drift downward onto ordinary earners who lack that access.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Complexity as camouflage" icon="magnifier">
    An intricate code is not an accident. It **obscures** who truly pays and who escapes, so the public cannot easily judge whether the system is fair.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The toolkit of escape" icon="setting">
    Deferral, tax shelters, offshoring, and **converting income into lower-taxed forms** let top earners shrink their real rate - while provisions like the Alternative Minimum Tax increasingly bite the middle class instead of the rich.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Enforcement aimed low" icon="error">
    An **underfunded IRS** finds it easier to audit simple returns than complex wealthy ones - so scrutiny falls hard on modest filers, such as those claiming the Earned Income Tax Credit.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Subsidies flow upward" icon="up-caret">
    "Corporate welfare" - targeted breaks and subsidies - channels public money **toward** large firms and the well-connected, the reverse of what most assume.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Fairness is the real asset" icon="heart">
    A system built on voluntary compliance depends on **perceived fairness**. Quietly rigging it in favour of the top erodes the trust that keeps everyone else paying.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Ask who wrote the rule.** When a provision seems arcane, ask *who benefits* and *who lobbied for it* - complexity usually points to an interest being served.

2. **Distinguish legal from fair.** "It's allowed" settles nothing. Judge a provision by its distribution of burden, not by whether anyone broke a law.

3. **Watch the effective rate, not the headline.** The posted bracket is theatre; what matters is the share actually paid after deferral, shelters and reclassified income.

4. **Follow the enforcement money.** Note *who gets audited*. Skewed scrutiny toward small filers is a sign of political capture, not neutral administration.

5. **Treat trust as the metric.** Weigh any tax change by whether it strengthens or corrodes public belief that the system is fair.

</Steps>

## Lines worth keeping

> *The most dangerous tax abuses are not the illegal ones - they are the ones made perfectly legal.*

> *Complexity is not a flaw in the code; it is where the advantages hide.*

> *When the people who benefit most also write the rules, fairness leaves quietly.*

> *A system that depends on voluntary compliance cannot survive the belief that only the little people pay.*
