## The big idea

In 2011 the supertanker *Brillante Virtuoso* was boarded and set ablaze in the pirate-infested Gulf of Aden. The story looked like piracy; the evidence pointed to a **deliberate scuttling for an insurance payout**. Campbell and Chellel, both Bloomberg journalists, use that single burning hull as a keyhole into an entire hidden system - **flags of convenience, shell-company ownership, and a high seas nobody effectively polices**. The deeper argument: the arteries of global trade are lined with so much deliberate opacity that a ship can be worth *more destroyed than afloat*, and proving otherwise is brutally hard.

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **See the ships behind the shelf.** Nearly everything you own crossed an ocean on a vessel you'll never see, run by an industry designed to stay invisible.

2. **Follow the flag.** Learn why "flags of convenience" and shell ownership exist - cheap registration and legal distance let real owners disappear behind paper.

3. **Understand how marine insurance polices risk.** Grasp that Lloyd's-style underwriting, not any government, is the main check on maritime behaviour - and why that check can be gamed.

4. **Ask who benefits when a ship is lost.** Treat a suspicious loss as a follow-the-money problem: cui bono, and who can *prove* what really happened?

5. **Read opacity as a warning sign.** Wherever oversight is thin and ownership is hidden, expect fraud - and sometimes worse - to move in.

</Steps>
