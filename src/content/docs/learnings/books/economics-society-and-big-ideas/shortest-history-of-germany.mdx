## The big idea

Hawes tells two thousand years at a sprint, but everything hangs on one claim: to understand Germany, find the **Elbe**. West of the river lies the older, Roman-touched Germany - the Rhineland, the cities along the *Limes*, the lands that traded with and looked toward the West. These were, for centuries, more urban, more commercial and more open. East of the Elbe lies ground that was Slavic before it was German, taken and settled from the Middle Ages onward by the **Teutonic Knights** and later organised by **Prussia** into a disciplined, land-owning, military society.

That, for Hawes, is the deep structure beneath the surface of events. "Germany" for most of its past was not a country at all but a loose imperial **patchwork**, and when a unified state finally arrived in **1871** it was built and led from the east, by Prussia. His boldest move is moral as well as geographic: the catastrophes of the twentieth century, he argues, flow less from some German national essence than from the **east-Elbian, Prussian** strain seizing control of the whole. Lose that east - as Germany effectively did after 1945 - and the country swings back toward its better, Western self.

## The heart of it

<Steps>

1. **Rome draws the first line.** The Rhine and the fortified *Limes* frontier marked the edge of the Roman world. The lands inside it grew towns, roads, trade and a Western orientation centuries before anything called "Germany" existed. Geography cut a groove the country would keep falling back into.

2. **A patchwork, not a nation.** Through the long life of the Holy Roman Empire, the German lands were a mosaic of principalities, bishoprics and free cities - hundreds of them, with no central state. Loyalty was local; "Germany" was a cultural and imperial idea, not a government.

3. **The east is colonised late.** From the high Middle Ages the Teutonic Knights and settlers pushed east of the Elbe into Slavic territory, building a frontier society of large estates, bound peasants and armed lords. Out of this world rose **Brandenburg-Prussia**, organised around its army.

4. **Prussia unifies on its own terms - 1871.** Under Bismarck, Prussia defeated Denmark, Austria and France in quick succession and proclaimed the German Empire in 1871. Unification was achieved not by liberal parliaments but by an eastern military monarchy, "by blood and iron."

5. **The Prussian capture and its catastrophes.** Hawes argues the new Germany inherited Prussia's militarism and authoritarian reflexes rather than the liberal, Western tradition of the Rhineland. He points to the electoral geography of the early 1930s - the Nazis' **strongest support in the Protestant Prussian east** - to argue that the fall was driven by that east, not by the Germans as a whole.

6. **1945: the return to the West.** Defeat, division, and then the permanent loss of the old Prussian east (to Poland and the Soviet sphere) stripped away the eastern pole. What remained in the West re-centred on the Rhineland. The Federal Republic, in Hawes's reading, is the "good," Western Germany finally freed of its eastern anchor.

</Steps>

<Aside type="caution" title="One strong, contested interpretation">
This is pointed, argument-driven popular history, not a neutral survey. Professional historians push back hard on its **determinism** - the idea that one river, drawn by Rome, can carry the weight of two millennia and explain Nazism. Read it as a bold, clarifying lens, not a settled verdict, and keep the counter-arguments in view.
</Aside>

## How the book unfolds

A brisk chronological sweep, always returning to the Elbe. It opens with **Rome** and the Rhine frontier, then moves through the medieval **Holy Roman Empire** as a patchwork of states and the eastward colonisation beyond the Elbe. It traces the rise of **Brandenburg-Prussia**, the Reformation's confessional split, and the long absence of any German nation-state. The pace quickens at **1871** and Bismarck's unification, then drives through the **Kaiserreich**, the First World War, Weimar, and the Nazi seizure of power - read throughout as a Prussian, eastern story. It closes on **1945**, division, the loss of the east, and the Federal Republic as a Western restoration, ending with a pointed reflection on where Germany should look next.

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Locate the Elbe first.** When a German event puzzles you, ask which side of the river it sits on - west (Roman, urban, liberal) or east (colonised, agrarian, militarist).

2. **Date the nation to 1871.** Treat "Germany" before Bismarck as a patchwork, not a country, and you will stop mistaking a cultural idea for a state.

3. **Separate "Prussia" from "the Germans."** Test how much of the dark twentieth century Hawes explains as *Prussian* ascendancy rather than national character - and where that distinction strains.

4. **Read 1945 as a return, not only a defeat.** See the Federal Republic as Germany swinging back to its Western centre of gravity once the eastern pole is gone.

5. **Watch the compass.** Carry away the book's central anxiety - Western anchoring versus the pull east - as a lens on Germany's choices today.

6. **Cross-check the electoral claim.** The Nazi-vote-and-the-east argument is load-bearing; note it, then weigh it against economic, religious and class explanations too.

7. **Hold the thesis lightly.** Keep the Elbe lens handy, but read at least one sceptical historian alongside it.

</Steps>

## Worth knowing

<Badge text="Context" variant="note" /> Hawes is a novelist and cultural historian writing popular history with a mission, not a dispassionate textbook. Academic reviewers have praised its verve while faulting its **geographic determinism**, its selective use of the electoral evidence, and the neatness with which a single fault line is made to explain Reformation, unification and Nazism alike. The value is in the provocation and the clarity of the frame - treat the certainty as an argument to interrogate, not a conclusion to accept.
