**Author:** Indian Institute of Banking & Finance (IIBF)
**In one line:** Buyer and seller in different countries don't trust each other — trade finance uses banks, documents and agreed rules to move goods and money safely across that gap.

## Three things to remember

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="1 · It's a trust problem" icon="warning">
    The seller wants payment before shipping; the buyer wants goods before paying. Every instrument exists to **bridge that gap** — a bank steps in so neither side has to trust the other.
  </Card>
  <Card title="2 · Documents are the goods" icon="document">
    Banks deal in **paper, not cargo**. Payment turns on whether the documents comply, not on what actually arrived — so accuracy of documents is everything.
  </Card>
  <Card title="3 · Rules make it work" icon="approve-check">
    Cross-border deals hold together because everyone follows the **same rule-books** — ICC's UCP, URC, URDG and INCOTERMS — plus each country's own regulator.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## The big idea

Two parties who have never met, in different legal systems, must exchange goods and money without either going first. Trade finance solves this by inserting **banks and standardised documents** between them: the seller ships against a document set, the bank pays against compliant documents, and internationally agreed rules decide what "compliant" means. The whole discipline is really about **allocating risk** — payment risk, delivery risk, country risk — and pricing the financing that lets trade happen anyway.

## The key ideas

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="The risk ladder" icon="list-format">
    Payment methods sit on a trust spectrum: **advance payment** (safest for seller), **letter of credit**, **documentary collection**, and **open account** (safest for buyer). Risk shifts from buyer to seller as you move down.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The letter of credit" icon="seti:lock">
    The LC is a bank's **conditional promise to pay** the seller against compliant documents — independent of the underlying contract. It converts the buyer's credit risk into a bank's, governed by **UCP 600**.
  </Card>
  <Card title="INCOTERMS 2020" icon="random">
    Standard three-letter rules (**FOB, CIF, EXW, DAP**) that fix exactly where cost, risk and responsibility pass from seller to buyer. They prevent disputes over "who pays for what, and until where."
  </Card>
  <Card title="The ICC rule-books" icon="open-book">
    **UCP 600** governs letters of credit, **URC** governs collections, **URDG** governs demand guarantees. Banks worldwide adopt these voluntarily, which is what makes cross-border settlement predictable.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Financing the trade" icon="rocket">
    **Pre-shipment** (packing credit) funds production; **post-shipment** finance funds the gap until payment. Add **bank guarantees, factoring and forfaiting** — and note the **LIBOR → ARR** reference-rate transition.
  </Card>
  <Card title="India's framework" icon="setting">
    Domestic trade finance runs inside **RBI** guidelines, the **Foreign Trade Policy**, **DGFT** licensing and **FEDAI** conventions — the regulatory layer over the international rules.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Start from the risk question.** Ask who trusts whom and how much — that alone points you to advance payment, LC, collection or open account.

2. **Pick the INCOTERM deliberately.** Agree where risk and cost pass *before* pricing the deal, so nothing is assumed.

3. **Check documents like a bank.** Read the LC terms first, then build the document set to match exactly — discrepancies, not the shipment, are what stop payment.

4. **Match the finance to the stage.** Use packing credit before shipment and post-shipment finance after; consider guarantees, factoring or forfaiting for larger risk.

5. **Stay inside the rule-books.** Cite UCP/URC/URDG and RBI/FTP requirements — compliance is what keeps the transaction enforceable.

</Steps>

## Lines worth keeping

> *In trade finance, banks deal in documents — not goods.*

> *An LC replaces the buyer's promise to pay with a bank's.*

> *INCOTERMS answer one question: up to what point is it the seller's problem?*

> *Move down the payment ladder and risk moves from buyer to seller.*

> *The rules are voluntary — which is exactly why everyone follows them.*
