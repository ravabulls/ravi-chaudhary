**Book:** Corporate Social Responsibility: A Very Short Introduction
**Author:** Jeremy Moon
**In one line:** CSR is how business takes responsibility for its social and environmental impacts *beyond the legal minimum* - a scholarly primer on an old idea that globalisation made urgent.

## Three things to remember

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="1 · Beyond the legal minimum" icon="approve-check">
    CSR is the set of ways firms take responsibility for their **social and environmental impacts**, going past what law requires. At heart it's about the relationship between **business and society**.
  </Card>
  <Card title="2 · Old idea, new scale" icon="open-book">
    CSR isn't new - paternalist industrialists and philanthropy run deep in its history. What changed is **scale**: bigger corporate power and globalised supply chains that reach beyond any one government.
  </Card>
  <Card title="3 · Driver or cover" icon="warning">
    The same practices - codes, reports, certification - can drive **real change** or supply a **greenwashing** cover. The honest question is always which one, and how you'd tell.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## The big idea

Corporations grew powerful enough, and globalisation stretched their supply chains far enough, that the old question - *what does business owe society?* - outran what any single government could regulate. CSR is the field that fills that gap: the voluntary responsibilities a firm accepts beyond the law. Moon treats it as neither hero nor villain but as a **balanced, contested practice** - genuinely capable of improving conditions, genuinely capable of being used as reputational cover - and increasingly tied to sustainability and to business's basic **legitimacy** in society.

## The key ideas

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Carroll's pyramid" icon="list-format">
    A common framing sorts corporate responsibilities into four layers: **economic**, **legal**, **ethical**, and **philanthropic** - from making a profit up to voluntary good works.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Friedman vs. stakeholders" icon="random">
    The classic debate: Friedman's view that *the social responsibility of business is to increase its profits* against the **stakeholder** view that firms owe duties to employees, communities, and the environment - not shareholders alone.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Policies and practices" icon="setting">
    CSR shows up as concrete instruments - **codes of conduct, reporting, certification**, and partnerships with NGOs and governments (a form of *"new governance"*), not just good intentions.
  </Card>
  <Card title="It varies by place" icon="earth">
    CSR is not one global template. It differs by **country and culture**, shaped by local histories of business, state, and civil society.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Separate law from responsibility.** Ask first what's legally required, then what the firm chooses to do *beyond* it - that gap is where CSR actually lives.

2. **Locate it on the pyramid.** Is a given claim economic, legal, ethical, or philanthropic? Naming the layer clarifies what's really being promised.

3. **Name the stakeholders.** List who bears the impact - workers, communities, environment - and check whether they appear anywhere in the firm's account.

4. **Test for greenwashing.** Look for verification: independent certification, audited reporting, outside partners. Unchecked self-report is the warning sign.

5. **Read for the local context.** Judge a practice against the country and culture it sits in, not a single global standard.

</Steps>

## Lines worth keeping

> *CSR is responsibility for social and environmental impact that begins where the law leaves off.*

> *The idea is old; globalisation is what forced it to grow up and professionalise.*

> *Friedman put profit first; the stakeholder view answers that a firm owes more than its shareholders.*

> *Every CSR instrument can drive change or disguise its absence - legitimacy hangs on which.*
