## The big idea

Technology — AI, platforms, on-demand work — is not a force that *happens to us*; it's a set of choices we make. O'Reilly argues the future is written by the **maps** we hold in our heads and the **rules** we choose to enforce. Point technology at the outcomes we actually want, and "WTF?" becomes wonder. Leave it on autopilot toward the wrong goal, and it becomes dismay. Either way, it's up to us.

## The key ideas

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Think in maps" icon="list-format">
    Every decision runs on a mental model of how the world works. Upgrade the map — of the economy, of tech, of work — and better choices follow almost on their own.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Platforms and their rules" icon="setting">
    Amazon, Google, and Uber are algorithmic systems governed by rules. Whoever writes the rules of a platform shapes the behaviour of everyone on it — that's the "WTF economy".
  </Card>
  <Card title="Beware the master algorithm" icon="warning">
    "Maximise shareholder value" acts like a master algorithm that treats people as costs to cut. A financial market chasing that single number behaves, O'Reilly warns, like a **rogue AI**.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Design for the outcome" icon="pencil">
    Systems produce what they're optimised for. Choose the outcome you actually want — universal prosperity, better work — and design the rules and incentives backward from it.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Create more value than you capture" icon="heart">
    O'Reilly's core business principle: a durable company adds more to the world than it takes out. Capture too much of the value you create and you starve the ecosystem you depend on.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Government as a platform" icon="rocket">
    Rules aren't fixed. Government, work, and markets are systems we can rewrite — "do more, not less" — to spread the gains of technology rather than concentrate them.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Name your map.** Before deciding, write down the model you're assuming about how this market or technology works — then ask what would prove it wrong.

2. **Ask "augment or replace?"** For any new tool, decide deliberately what you'll do with the freed-up capacity, rather than defaulting to cutting.

3. **Check the algorithm you're optimising.** Find the single number your team or system is really maximising, and ask whether it's the outcome you actually want.

4. **Design backward from the outcome.** Pick the future you want, then set the rules and incentives that make it the path of least resistance.

5. **Create more value than you capture.** Sanity-check any plan by asking whether it grows the whole ecosystem or just your slice of it.

</Steps>
