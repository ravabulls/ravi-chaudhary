## The big idea

Perlroth, a New York Times cybersecurity reporter, spent years mapping a hidden trade that most people never hear about. A **zero-day exploit** is a previously unknown software vulnerability, and a whole shadow market grew up to buy and sell them. The buyers with the deepest pockets were governments — above all the US and its NSA — who stockpiled flaws for spying and sabotage rather than warning vendors to patch them. The result is a **cyberweapons arms race** with a soft, sprawling attack surface and few of the rules that govern conventional weapons. Her core warning is a paradox: every flaw you keep secret to attack others is a flaw left open in your own hospitals, pipelines, and elections.

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Treat patching as strategy, not chores.** Unpatched known flaws cause most real-world breaches — apply updates fast and everywhere, especially on infrastructure.

2. **Reward disclosure over hoarding.** Bug-bounty programmes and responsible disclosure turn finders into allies instead of sellers on the grey market.

3. **Assume your suppliers are the way in.** The soft attack surface runs through vendors and connected devices — map dependencies and demand security from them.

4. **Push for rules and transparency.** As a citizen or leader, back norms that limit offensive stockpiling and require breach disclosure — the arms race won't self-regulate.

5. **Shrink what's exposed.** Fewer connected systems, strong basics, and network segmentation limit how far any single exploit can travel.

</Steps>
