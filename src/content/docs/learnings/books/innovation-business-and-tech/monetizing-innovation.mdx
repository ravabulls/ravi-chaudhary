## Three things to remember

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="1 · Price first, not last" icon="rocket">
    Most teams build the product, then bolt on a price at the end. Flip it: design the product *around* the price. Price is a design constraint, not an afterthought.
  </Card>
  <Card title="2 · Willingness to pay is the number" icon="approve-check">

## The big idea

Innovation projects rarely fail because the product is bad; they fail because nobody checked whether people would *pay* for it. The fix is to bring price forward and treat willingness to pay as your compass through the whole design. When you know early what customers value and what they will spend, you build the right features, pick the right pricing model, and walk into launch already knowing the product will earn its keep.

## The key ideas

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Four ways innovations fail" icon="list-format">
    **Feature shocks** cram in too much and over-engineer. **Minivations** underprice or under-market a real breakthrough. **Hidden gems** are opportunities nobody recognises. **Undead** products launch but nobody wanted them.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Ask willingness to pay early" icon="magnifier">
    Willingness to pay is knowable *before* you build. Talk to customers up front, in plain terms, so the answer can still shape the product rather than just explain the disappointment.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Segment by value and need" icon="seti:plan">
    Group customers by what they value and what they will pay, not by age or industry. Demographics are easy to collect and weak at predicting the wallet.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Pick the right pricing model" icon="setting">
    Subscription, freemium, per-use, bundling, good-better-best — the *how* of charging matters as much as the number. Match the model to how customers actually get and measure value.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Build a value story, don't discount" icon="open-book">
    Make a value-based business case and a clear story for why the price is fair. Reactive discounting trains customers to wait and quietly erases the margin you designed for.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Price for how people decide" icon="heart">
    Buying is psychological. How you frame, anchor, and package a price shifts what feels reasonable — so design the choice, not just the figure.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Have the pricing talk before you build.** Ask customers what the idea is worth to them while the design is still open enough to change.

2. **Rank features by willingness to pay.** Keep what people will pay for, cut what they won't — this alone kills feature shock.

3. **Segment by value, then differentiate.** Sort customers by what they value and use good-better-best or bundles to serve each tier.

4. **Choose a pricing model deliberately.** Decide whether subscription, per-use, freemium, or bundling best fits how value shows up over time.

5. **Write the value story before launch.** Put the business case and the reason-to-pay on paper so you never fall back on reflex discounts.

</Steps>

## Lines worth keeping

> *Design the product around the price — not the price around the product.*

> *Willingness to pay is the most important number in your business, and you can learn it before you build.*

> *A discount is a decision you failed to make earlier, priced.*

> *If customers won't pay for a feature, it isn't a feature — it's a cost.*

> *Segments are groups of wallets that behave alike, not groups of people who look alike.*
