**Book:** The Lean Startup: How Today's Entrepreneurs Use Continuous Innovation to Create Radically Successful Businesses
**Author:** Eric Ries
**In one line:** A startup is an experiment under extreme uncertainty, so stop guessing and start learning — build the smallest thing that tests your riskiest belief, measure what real customers do, and steer.

## Three things to remember

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="1 · Learning beats building" icon="rocket">
    The point of a startup is not to ship features, it is **validated learning** — hard evidence about what customers actually want. Any work that doesn't produce that evidence is waste.
  </Card>
  <Card title="2 · Build–Measure–Learn" icon="random">
    Turn an idea into a product, measure real behaviour, learn, repeat. Speed through the **whole loop** is the goal — the faster you turn it, the faster you find the truth.
  </Card>
  <Card title="3 · Pivot or persevere" icon="setting">
    Every so often, look the evidence in the eye and decide: keep going, or change direction. A **pivot** is not failure — it is a course correction with the ground already tested.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## The big idea

Most startups fail not because the product breaks, but because they build something nobody wants — perfectly. Ries reframes a startup as an institution built to run **experiments** under extreme uncertainty, where success is measured by learning rather than by output. The job is to find the shortest path to a sustainable business by testing your assumptions against real customers, fast and cheaply, before you spend everything building the wrong thing.

## The key ideas

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Minimum Viable Product" icon="star">
    The MVP is the **smallest thing** that starts the learning loop — just enough product to test your riskiest assumption with real customers. It can be embarrassing; its job is to teach, not to impress.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Validated learning" icon="approve-check">
    Progress is proof that you're building something people want. Replace hope and opinion with an experiment that either confirms or kills the assumption.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Vanity vs actionable metrics" icon="warning">
    Totals that only go up — page views, raw sign-ups — flatter you but guide nothing. **Actionable metrics** tie a change in behaviour to a change you made, so you can decide.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Innovation accounting" icon="list-format">
    A disciplined way to measure progress when you have no revenue yet: set a baseline, tune the engine toward it, then judge whether to pivot or persevere.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Engine of growth" icon="seti:powershell">
    Growth comes through one of three engines — **sticky** (retention), **viral** (customers bring customers), or **paid** (spend less to acquire than you earn). Know which one is yours.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Small batches & the Five Whys" icon="magnifier">
    Ship in **small batches** so problems surface early and cheaply. When something breaks, ask *why* five times to reach the root cause, not the symptom.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Name your riskiest assumption.** Write down the one belief that, if wrong, sinks the whole idea — the leap of faith to test first.

2. **Build the smallest test.** Design an MVP that checks that assumption with real customers, using the least work possible.

3. **Measure real behaviour.** Track what people actually do, not what they say — pick metrics that change your next decision.

4. **Go and see for yourself.** Watch real customers use it firsthand instead of trusting reports from a distance.

5. **Decide out loud: pivot or persevere.** Meet on a set cadence, look at the evidence, and either keep going or change direction deliberately.

</Steps>

## Lines worth keeping

> *"The only way to win is to learn faster than anyone else."*

> *A startup's real output is validated learning, not lines of code or a longer feature list.*

> *If you're not embarrassed by your first version, you shipped too late.*

> *Vanity metrics make you feel good; actionable metrics help you decide.*

> *Ask "why" five times, and a technical glitch usually turns out to be a human and process problem.*
