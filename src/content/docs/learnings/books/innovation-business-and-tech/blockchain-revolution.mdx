## Three things to remember

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="1 · A trust protocol" icon="approve-check">
    Blockchain is a distributed, tamper-resistant ledger. Trust is built into the **network**, not vested in a bank, platform, or middleman.
  </Card>
  <Card title="2 · Internet of value" icon="random">
    The first web moved *information*. This one lets anyone move and settle **value** - money, assets, identity - peer-to-peer, without a central clearer.
  </Card>
  <Card title="3 · Programmable trust" icon="setting">
    **Smart contracts** let agreements execute themselves when conditions are met - commerce that runs on code rather than paperwork and intermediaries.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

<Aside type="note">This is the authors' optimistic **2016 thesis**, not settled fact. Read it as a claim about what the technology *could* do.</Aside>

## The big idea

For centuries we've relied on trusted third parties - banks, governments, platforms - to establish trust between strangers. The Tapscotts argue blockchain replaces that with a **shared, replicated ledger** secured by cryptography and validated by consensus, so no single party owns or can quietly rewrite the record. Once records are **immutable** and verification is built into the network itself, whole layers of intermediaries become optional. That, they claim, reshapes money, business, and inclusion.

## The key ideas

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="How it works" icon="document">
    A ledger **shared and replicated** across many nodes, secured by cryptography and digital signatures, agreed through **consensus** (mining / proof-of-work), and kept **immutable** - plus smart contracts on top.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Seven design principles" icon="list-format">
    Networked integrity, distributed power, value as incentive, security, privacy, rights preserved, and inclusion - the properties a trustworthy blockchain should embody.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Reinventing finance" icon="seti:money">
    The **"Golden Eight"** functions - moving, storing, lending, trading, funding, investing, insuring, and accounting for value - could all be rebuilt on shared ledgers.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The firm & the ledger of things" icon="puzzle">
    New business models emerge as coordination costs fall; pairing blockchain with IoT creates a **"ledger of things"** where devices transact and record autonomously.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Prosperity & inclusion" icon="heart">
    For the world's **unbanked**, verifiable identity and portable reputation on a ledger could open access to finance, property, and services.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Challenges & risks" icon="warning">
    The authors are candid: energy use, governance, scale, regulation, and adoption are serious **implementation hurdles**, not footnotes.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Ask "who is the middleman?"** For any transaction or record, name the trusted third party - that's where a shared ledger might apply.

2. **Test against the seven principles.** Before trusting a blockchain claim, check integrity, security, privacy, and rights - buzzwords aren't proof.

3. **Look at the Golden Eight.** Map a finance process to moving / storing / lending / funding value to see where disintermediation actually bites.

4. **Weigh the risks honestly.** Pair every "could disrupt" with governance, energy, and adoption realities before betting on it.

</Steps>
