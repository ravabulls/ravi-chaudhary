## The big idea

Published for IBM's 2011 centennial, the book asks a simple question with a hundred-year answer: how does an institution stay relevant and alive for a full century while the technology around it turns over again and again? Rather than a straight chronology, the authors organise the story as three essays, each taking one theme and following it from the company's early tabulating machines through to the present.

The thread running through all three is that longevity is not luck or a single great product. It comes from a repeatable posture - pioneering hard science ahead of demand, managing for decades rather than quarters, anchoring the organisation in values people can name, and treating reinvention as the normal condition of survival. IBM's history becomes a case study in pairing bold technical bets with the unglamorous discipline of management and culture.

The wider argument is that information technology has been one of the great forces reshaping the past century - in science, in how corporations are run, and in how cities, industries and the planet function. The company's story is used as a lens on that larger transformation, ending with the idea that technology's next act is to make the systems people already depend on measurably smarter.

## The heart of it

The book's substance sits in its three essays. Each takes one theme and traces it across the century.

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Essay 1 · Pioneering the science of information" icon="laptop">
    The first essay follows information technology itself - from punch cards and tabulating machines that counted a census, to the mainframe era and the System/360, and on to modern computing, data and analytics. The argument is that IBM helped invent the very idea of processing information at scale, and kept advancing that frontier era after era.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Essay 2 · Reinventing the modern corporation" icon="list-format">
    The second essay is about the company as an organisation: professional management science, the rise of the multinational, a deliberate corporate culture and set of values, the "THINK" ethos, and repeated self-reinvention through crises. It treats management itself as an invention IBM helped pioneer and refine.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Essay 3 · Making the world work better" icon="puzzle">
    The third essay looks outward: using information technology and "smarter systems" to improve how cities, industries and the planet actually function - the "Smarter Planet" idea. Technology's purpose is framed as optimising the real-world systems people live inside, from traffic and energy to healthcare and supply chains.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

<Aside type="tip">
The through-line across all three essays is the same: reinvention paired with information technology in service of progress. The company endures because it keeps turning new science into new ways of working - for itself, for the corporation as an institution, and for the world's systems.
</Aside>

## The key ideas

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Punch cards and tabulating machines" icon="seti:db">
    IBM's roots lie in tabulating and punch-card equipment used to count and process data at scale, including census and business record-keeping. It matters because it established the founding idea that would run through everything after - machines that handle information.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The System/360 mainframe" icon="seti:default">
    The System/360 was a landmark family of compatible mainframe computers and one of the company's boldest bets. It matters because it reshaped business computing and showed how a single, risky reinvention could define an entire era.
  </Card>
  <Card title="THINK" icon="comment">
    "THINK," associated with Thomas J. Watson Sr., became shorthand for a culture of disciplined thought and pioneering research. It matters because it turned long-term science and learning into the company's identity rather than a slogan.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Deep Blue and Watson" icon="rocket">
    Milestones such as the Deep Blue chess machine and later the Watson question-answering system marked frontiers in computing and artificial intelligence. They matter as public proof that the habit of funding hard research kept paying off across generations.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Smarter Planet" icon="puzzle">
    The Smarter Planet idea holds that the world's systems can be made instrumented, interconnected and intelligent. It matters because it reframes technology's job as improving traffic, energy, healthcare and supply chains, not just building new devices.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Reinvention through crisis" icon="random">

  <Card title="Management as an invention" icon="setting">

## How the book unfolds

The book opens by framing the centennial and the puzzle of corporate longevity, then hands the story to three essays. The first traces the science of information from tabulating machines forward; the second follows the company as a self-reinventing modern corporation and culture; the third looks outward to technology in service of the world's systems and the Smarter Planet. Figures such as Thomas J. Watson Sr. and pivotal products and crises recur across the essays, so the same century is seen three times through three lenses rather than once in a line.

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Fund the frontier early.** Invest in hard research and technical bets ahead of clear demand - the lead built in one era is what carries a company into the next.

2. **Treat reinvention as routine.** Assume today's core business is tomorrow's legacy, and start the next act before a crisis forces it.

3. **Manage for decades.** Value professional management and long-term thinking as a moat, not overhead; patient structure compounds across generations.

4. **Anchor to nameable values.** Stand for something beyond the next quarter so the organisation has a reason to hold together through its worst years.

5. **Build a culture of thought.** Make disciplined thinking and learning - the spirit of "THINK" - part of identity, not a poster on the wall.

6. **Make existing systems smarter.** Look for value in instrumenting and connecting what already exists - logistics, energy, care - rather than only in brand-new products.

7. **Read history for posture, not recipes.** The lesson is a repeatable stance toward change, not a checklist to copy step by step.

</Steps>

## Worth knowing

<Badge text="Context" variant="note" /> This is a company-sponsored centennial history, written for IBM's hundredth anniversary. Its tone is celebratory and it emphasises the company's achievements and enduring ideas; read it as an authoritative, insider account of the themes rather than a critical outside audit.
