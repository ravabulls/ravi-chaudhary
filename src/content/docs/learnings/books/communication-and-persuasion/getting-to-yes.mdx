**Book:** Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In
**Author:** Roger Fisher & William Ury (Harvard Negotiation Project)
**In one line:** Stop haggling over positions - negotiate on the merits, so you reach wise agreements efficiently without wrecking the relationship.

## Three things to remember

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="1 · Negotiate on the merits" icon="approve-check">
    Positional bargaining - stake a stance, defend it, trade concessions - produces bad deals slowly and strains relationships. **Principled negotiation** is the alternative: judge the outcome by interests and fair standards, not by who is more stubborn.
  </Card>
  <Card title="2 · Interests, not positions" icon="magnifier">

  </Card>
  <Card title="3 · Know your BATNA" icon="star">

## The big idea

Most people negotiate by taking a **position** and defending it, conceding grudgingly until someone caves. Fisher and Ury call this *positional bargaining*, and it fails on three counts: it produces unwise agreements, it is inefficient, and it endangers the relationship. Going soft to keep the peace leaves you exploited; going hard to win leaves both sides exhausted and resentful. The usual advice - be tough, or be nice - just picks a losing side of a false choice.

Their answer, developed at the Harvard Negotiation Project, is **principled negotiation**, or negotiation *on the merits*. The idea: decide issues by their substance rather than through a haggling contest over what each side will and will not do. Be hard on the problem and soft on the people. Look for mutual gains wherever possible, and where interests conflict, insist the result rest on some fair standard independent of the will of either side.

The method rests on four principles - people, interests, options, criteria - plus one source of real-world power, the BATNA. Together they replace the tug-of-war of wills with a joint search for a wise outcome. The book is short, practical, and deliberately general: it is meant to work for a landlord and tenant, a diplomat and a rival, or a couple deciding where to live.

## The heart of it

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Separate the people from the problem" icon="puzzle">
    Negotiators are people first - with egos, emotions and misperceptions. Attack the **issue**, not each other. Handle perception, emotion and communication *directly and early*, so friction between people never contaminates the substance. Put yourselves side by side, facing the problem together.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Focus on interests, not positions" icon="open-book">
    Ask *why* and *why not* behind each stance to reach the real needs. Two sisters quarrel over one orange; one wants the **peel** for baking, the other the **fruit** to eat. Splitting it in half leaves both with less; naming the interests gives each all they need.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Invent options for mutual gain" icon="pencil">
    Before deciding, **brainstorm** many possibilities without judging them. The two enemies are the fixed-pie assumption and the hunt for a single right answer. Separate inventing from deciding, look for shared gains, and expand the pie before dividing it.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Insist on objective criteria" icon="list-format">
    When interests genuinely clash, don't let it become a contest of will. Anchor the outcome to a **fair standard** - market value, precedent, expert opinion, cost, law. Reason together over which standard applies, and stay open to reason; yield to principle, never to pressure.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Know your BATNA" icon="star">
    Your **Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement** is what you will do if you walk away. It is your true measure of any deal and your source of power. Improve it before you negotiate, know theirs, and never accept terms worse than it.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

<Aside type="tip">
The four principles run across the whole negotiation - each maps to a phase. **People** matters throughout; **interests** and **options** dominate the analysis and planning; **criteria** settles the bargaining. The **BATNA** sits underneath them all as your protection: it tells you when to walk. Used together they turn a fight over slices into a joint hunt for the best available deal.
</Aside>

## The key ideas

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Positional bargaining fails" icon="warning">
    Arguing over positions locks ego into stance, so retreat feels like losing face. It is slow, it breeds anger, and with more parties it gets worse. Recognising the pattern is the first step to leaving it.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Deal with emotions and perception" icon="heart">
    Acknowledge feelings as legitimate, let people **blow off steam** without reacting, and put yourself in their shoes. Look for a gesture that costs you little but means much to them. Perception *is* the problem as much as the facts.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The orange: interests behind positions" icon="open-book">
    The classic example - two people, one orange, one wants peel, one wants fruit. Opposed positions (*I want the orange*) hid **compatible interests**. Most negotiations have more shared or simply different interests than directly opposed ones.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Expand the pie, don't split it" icon="pencil">
    The fixed-pie mindset assumes your gain is my loss. Inventing options for **mutual gain** - dovetailing different interests, trading on what each values more - often creates value that plain compromise destroys.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Fair standards end the will-contest" icon="list-format">
    Deciding a used-car price by market value or a lease by precedent turns *my will against yours* into *what is fair here*. It protects the relationship and yields agreements each side can defend to their own constituents.
  </Card>
  <Card title="BATNA over a bottom line" icon="star">
    A rigid bottom line is arbitrary and can block a good deal; a clear BATNA is a real, comparable benchmark. It stops you accepting too little **and** stops you rejecting terms better than your alternatives.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Negotiation jujitsu" icon="random">
    When they attack or dig in, don't push back and don't defend. **Deflect** the force: reframe an attack as an attack on the shared problem, treat their position as one option, ask questions, and use silence. Redirect to interests and criteria.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Guard against dirty tricks" icon="seti:lock">
    Meet hardball tactics - deception, good-cop/bad-cop, escalating demands - by **naming the tactic** and negotiating the rules of the game itself, on the merits, before continuing on the substance.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## How the book unfolds

<Steps>

1. **The Problem** - why positional bargaining, soft or hard, gives poor results.

2. **The Method** - the four principles, one chapter each: people, interests, options, criteria.

3. **Yes, But...** - handling harder cases: when they are more powerful (use your BATNA), when they won't play (negotiation jujitsu), and when they fight dirty (dirty tricks).

4. **Closing** - the method restated, plus common questions on fairness, power and getting started.

</Steps>

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Name and improve your BATNA before you enter.** Know your best walk-away option and strengthen it - it sets the floor no agreement should fall below, and gives you calm confidence.

2. **Ask "why" behind every position.** Surface the real interests - theirs and yours - instead of trading demands. List them; the most powerful interests are basic human needs.

3. **Separate the people work from the substance.** Acknowledge emotions, check your perceptions, and attack the problem side by side rather than each other.

4. **Brainstorm options before deciding.** Put several possibilities for mutual gain on the table with judgment suspended, then choose - never invent and evaluate at once.

5. **Agree on a fair standard first.** Settle *which* objective criterion applies - market value, precedent, expert opinion - then let the answer follow from it, not from willpower.

6. **When attacked, reframe rather than counter-attack.** Treat the assault as an attack on the shared problem, ask questions instead of asserting, and steer back to interests and criteria.

7. **Name dirty tactics out loud.** If they use tricks, raise the tactic explicitly and negotiate the process before returning to substance.

</Steps>

## Worth knowing

<Aside type="caution">
The method assumes a good-faith counterpart and can feel thin against a party who simply wants to dominate - which is why the follow-up book *Getting Past No* (Ury) exists. It also leans on your BATNA to handle **power imbalances**: when your alternative is weak, principled negotiation informs but cannot manufacture leverage. Critics note real people are less rational, and cultural norms shape what counts as a "fair standard." Treat it as a strong default, not a guarantee.
</Aside>
