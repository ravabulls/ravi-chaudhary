## The big idea

Most people negotiate by staking out a **position** and defending it, trading concessions until someone caves. That produces poor agreements, drags on, and strains the relationship. Fisher & Ury's alternative — **principled negotiation**, from the Harvard Negotiation Project — is to negotiate *on the merits*: focus on the underlying interests and fair standards, not a contest of will. Behind opposed positions usually lie shared and compatible interests; find them and you can expand the pie instead of fighting over slices.

## The key ideas

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Separate people from the problem" icon="approve-check">
    Negotiators are people first — with egos, emotions and misperceptions. Deal with those *directly*, side by side, so the human friction doesn't contaminate the substance.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Focus on interests" icon="open-book">
    Two sisters fight over one orange; one wants the **peel** to bake, the other the **fruit** to eat. Split it 50/50 and both lose half. Ask *why* and both get all they need.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Invent options for mutual gain" icon="pencil">
    Brainstorm many possibilities **before** deciding. The enemies are the assumption of a fixed pie and the hunt for a single right answer — expand the pie first.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Insist on objective criteria" icon="list-format">
    Anchor the outcome to fair, independent standards — market value, precedent, expert opinion — not to who is more stubborn. Reason, and be open to reason.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Negotiation jujitsu" icon="random">
    When they attack or play hardball, don't push back. Deflect the force — reframe attacks as attacks on the problem, and redirect to interests and criteria.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Name your BATNA before you enter.** Know your best walk-away option and improve it — it sets the floor no agreement should fall below.

2. **Ask "why" behind every position.** Surface the real interests — theirs and yours — instead of trading demands.

3. **Generate options before deciding.** Put several possibilities on the table for mutual gain before you commit to any one.

4. **Agree on a fair standard first.** Settle *which* objective criterion applies, then let the answer follow from it — not from willpower.

5. **When attacked, reframe.** Don't counter-attack; treat the assault as an attack on the shared problem and steer back to interests.

</Steps>
