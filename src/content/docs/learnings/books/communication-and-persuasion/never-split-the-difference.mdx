## The big idea

Classic negotiation treats people as rational actors trading concessions. Voss — a former FBI lead international kidnapping negotiator — learned across life-and-death cases that **emotion drives every deal**. You cannot argue someone out of a position, but you can lower their guard by showing you understand it. **Tactical empathy** — recognising the counterpart's feelings and reflecting them back — is the lever. It costs nothing, and it turns an adversary into a collaborator who helps solve *your* problem.

## The key ideas

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Mirroring" icon="seti:default">
    Repeat the last few words they said, then go quiet. It keeps them talking, signals you're listening, and quietly draws out the information they didn't plan to give.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Labeling" icon="comment">
    Name the emotion out loud — *"It seems like…"*, *"It sounds like…"*. Naming a feeling acknowledges and softens it. Never *"I think you feel…"*; keep it neutral.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Accusation audit" icon="list-format">
    Say their worst objections *before they do*. Voicing every negative up front drains its power and makes your ask sound reasonable by contrast.
  </Card>
  <Card title="'No' starts the negotiation" icon="rocket">
    "No" isn't failure — it hands the other side control and safety, so they relax. Real bargaining begins *after* the first no, not after a forced yes.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Calibrated questions" icon="question">
    Open with **How** and **What** — never *why* (it accuses). *"How am I supposed to do that?"* makes them solve your problem while feeling in charge.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Hunt the Black Swans" icon="magnifier">
    Chase the hidden facts that change everything, and remember the person across the table may not be the real decision-maker.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Slow it down with the late-night DJ voice.** A warm, calm, downward-inflecting tone signals safety and de-escalates before a word of content lands.

2. **Mirror, then pause.** Echo their last three words and let the silence pull more out of them.

3. **Label the emotion in the room.** *"It sounds like this timeline worries you."* Acknowledge it before you argue anything.

4. **Run the accusation audit.** List their likely objections first so they can't be used against you.

5. **Ask a calibrated 'How' question.** Hand them your constraint to solve — and listen for *"That's right."*

</Steps>
