## The big idea

Most sales pitches fail because they are a **feature-dump built around the vendor's product** instead of a story built around the customer's *decision*. Reps walk buyers through a tour of capabilities, assuming that more features shown equals more value proven. But a buyer drowning in near-identical options doesn't need a bigger catalogue - they need help making a confident choice they won't be blamed for later. When every option looks plausible and the cost of a wrong call feels high, the safest move is to stall. That is why so many well-run deals end in silence.

Dunford's reframe is that the pitch is a piece of **decision-support**, and the customer's hardest competitor is usually themselves - the paralysis of "let's revisit next quarter." So a winning pitch lowers uncertainty. It says: here is the problem, here are the honest ways to solve it and where each breaks down, here is our distinct approach, and here is the value only we deliver. Positioning - the context you set for what you are and who you beat - is the foundation the whole story stands on.

Done this way, the pitch stops being a monologue about you and becomes a guided path to *yes*. You earn the right to recommend by first showing you understand the whole landscape, competitors included. Then differentiated value does the selling, and the buyer feels smart and safe choosing you.

## The heart of it

The recommended pitch has a clear two-part shape: set up the decision honestly, then land your unique value.

<Steps>

1. **Part 1 - Insight (set up the problem and the landscape).** Open not with your product but with a point of view that reframes how the buyer sees their problem. Then honestly map the real options for solving it: doing nothing, the status quo tool, and named competing approaches - and why each old way falls short as needs have changed. Naming alternatives you could have hidden is what earns trust and positions you as a guide, not a salesperson.

2. **Part 2 - Your differentiated value (the "perfect world").** Now introduce your distinct approach and the specific value only you can deliver. Paint what becomes possible when the buyer has the right solution - the "perfect world" they should hold out for - then show how your product uniquely gets them there. Features appear only in service of that value, never as a checklist.

3. **Prove it and de-risk the yes.** Back the value with demos, proof points, and evidence that reduce the perceived risk of choosing. The goal is a buyer who feels the decision is obvious and safe, not merely impressed.

4. **Equip the champion to retell it.** The deal is usually closed inside the account by a champion pitching your story to their peers and boss when you aren't in the room. Package the story so it is simple and repeatable enough for them to win that meeting for you.

</Steps>

<Aside type="tip">
Leading with the honest landscape feels counter-intuitive - why name competitors and the option to do nothing? Because the buyer is *already* weighing those alternatives silently. Surfacing them first shows you understand their real situation, disarms the suspicion that you are hiding something, and gives you the standing to say why your approach wins. The guide who maps the whole terrain earns the trust to recommend a path.
</Aside>

## How the book unfolds

A short, practical book. Dunford opens by diagnosing why typical pitches fail - the feature-dump and the ignored "no decision" competitor. She connects the pitch to **positioning** (the groundwork from her earlier book) as the context everything else depends on. She then builds the **two-part structure** - Insight, then differentiated value / the perfect world - and walks through crafting each part with real B2B examples. Later chapters cover proof, common mistakes, and how to arm the champion and roll the pitch out across a sales team. It reads as a field guide you can apply to a live deck immediately.

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Fix positioning first.** Be explicit about your market, your best-fit customer, and who you actually beat. The pitch inherits this frame, so get it right before writing a word.

2. **Name the real enemy.** Assume the default outcome is *no decision*. Ask what makes this choice feel risky for the buyer and their internal reputation, and design the pitch to remove that risk.

3. **Map the approaches honestly.** List every way a buyer could solve this - doing nothing, the status quo, named competitors, your way - and where the old ways fall short. Say it out loud in the pitch; don't hide the alternatives.

4. **Lead with an insight.** Craft the reframe that shifts how the buyer sees their problem, and open with it *before* introducing what you sell.

5. **Anchor on differentiated value.** For each strength, state the value only you deliver and the outcome it creates. Delete anything a competitor could claim; attach features to values, never the reverse.

6. **Paint the perfect world and prove it.** Show what becomes possible with the right solution, then back it with demos and proof points that de-risk the yes.

7. **Arm the champion.** Boil the story down to something an insider can retell accurately, and hand it over so they can build the confident case for yes when you're not there.

</Steps>

## Worth knowing

This is the natural sequel to Dunford's *Obviously Awesome*: positioning sets the strategic context, and *Sales Pitch* turns that context into the conversation a rep actually has. The advice targets modern **B2B** selling, where a buying committee faces many look-alike SaaS options and stalls under choice overload - so the emphasis on trust, honesty, and risk reduction is a direct answer to that market, not generic charisma coaching.
