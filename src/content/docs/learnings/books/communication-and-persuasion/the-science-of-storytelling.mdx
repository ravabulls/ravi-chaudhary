## The big idea

We don't experience the world directly; the brain builds a **controlled hallucination** - a story - out of sparse sense data. That model runs quietly until something *changes*, and change is what a narrative exploits: a moment of unexpected difference opens a curiosity gap the mind is compelled to close. But information alone doesn't hold us. What grips us is a **character** wrestling with a broken idea of how to be safe. Great stories are, at heart, a controlled assault on that flawed self - and Storr fuses cognitive science with craft to show why the techniques writers stumbled onto actually match how the brain works.

## The key ideas

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="The brain is a storyteller" icon="pencil">
    Perception is a model, not a recording. Because we're built to attend to **change**, an unexpected shift is the natural first beat - it hijacks attention and starts the *what happens next?* engine.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The sacred flaw" icon="star">
    Beneath the surface goal sits a **flawed theory of control** - a subconscious belief about the world. The plot's job is to pressure that flaw until the character must either change or break.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Curiosity is an information gap" icon="magnifier">
    We crave answers to open questions. Curiosity isn't sparked by mystery for its own sake but by a **specific gap** the reader now needs closed.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Cause and effect" icon="right-arrow">
    Readers want events to happen **because** of choices, not just after them. A chain of decision-and-consequence is what turns a sequence into a story.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Meaning and status" icon="approve-check">
    Characters are driven by **connection and status** - the pursuit of belonging and standing. We watch partly to learn how to be human, and how to rise among others.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Structure serves the psychology" icon="list-format">
    Shapes like a **change-driven five-act arc** aren't arbitrary rules; they're scaffolding that keeps delivering the change, cause, and character-testing the brain is hungry for.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Open on a change.** Start where something unexpectedly shifts, not with setup or background - give the brain the jolt that asks *what next?*

2. **Define the flaw first.** Before plotting, name the character's mistaken theory of control. What do they wrongly believe keeps them safe?

3. **Attack the flaw with the plot.** Design events that pressure that belief, forcing the choice between changing and clinging.

4. **Chain events by cause.** Make each beat happen *because* of a character choice, so consequence - not coincidence - drives the story.

5. **Plant specific gaps.** Raise concrete open questions and pay them off, keeping the curiosity gap alive rather than explaining everything at once.

</Steps>
