## The big idea

The goal of any presentation is **persuasion, not information**. Weissman's whole method rests on one move: you are trying to carry the audience from their current position (Point A) to the action you want (Point B), and people move only when they see what is in it for *them*. Most presenters get this backwards. They fill the room with features, capabilities, specs, and data - the things *they* find impressive - and leave the audience to work out, unaided, why any of it matters. The audience's attention quietly drains away.

So the craft flips. Instead of talking about yourself, you speak from the audience's point of view and connect every message to a benefit they recognise. Instead of diving into slide software to "collect your thoughts," you decide who the audience is, what you want them to do, and which narrative path gets them there. The slides are the very last thing you touch - a delivery system for a message the presenter fully owns.

This is corporate-presentation coaching at its most disciplined: the high-stakes IPO roadshow, the funding pitch, the board update. But the logic scales to any moment where you need someone to say yes.

## The heart of it

<Steps>

1. **Answer the WIIFY - "What's In It For You?"** Weissman's central test. In every moment the audience is asking, silently, why they should care. A feature is about you; a benefit is about them. "Our platform processes 10,000 transactions a second" is a feature - "so your customers never wait at checkout" is the WIIFY. Say the benefit out loud, do not make them infer it.

2. **Find your Point B.** Before anything else, define exactly where you want the audience to end up - the specific belief or action, not a vague "understand our product." Point B governs every later decision. Audience-first planning means naming who is in the room and what you want them to think, feel, and do, then working backward to the content that gets them there.

3. **Get the ideas out, then choose a Flow Structure.** Brainstorm the raw points first, without judging them. Then pick one clear narrative structure to sequence them - chronological, problem/solution, numerical ("three reasons"), spatial, and so on. The structure is the spine that carries the audience from point to point instead of leaving them to reassemble a pile of facts.

4. **Only now, build the slides - and less is more.** With the story fixed, design thin slides: minimal text, one idea each, graphics that *illustrate* rather than overwhelm. The presenter carries the message; the slide is a visual aid, not a teleprompter. A slide crammed with bullet points forces the audience to read and listen at once, and they will do neither well.

5. **Rehearse the verbalisation - out loud.** A structured deck is not yet a persuasive talk. Speak the whole thing aloud, in real words, so the transitions and linkages actually work when a live room is watching. Rehearsing on the page is not rehearsing.

</Steps>

<Aside type="tip">
The chain is the method: **WIIFY** (why they care) sets up **Point B** (where they end up), which drives **ideas then Flow Structure** (how you get them there), which finally earns **thin slides** and **spoken rehearsal**. Skip a link - especially by opening PowerPoint first - and the rest collapses.
</Aside>

## The key ideas

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="WIIFY - benefit, not feature" icon="information">
    The audience's silent question in every moment. Convert each fact into the benefit it delivers *to them*. Weissman's habit: after any claim, add the phrase "which means that..." and finish it from the listener's side.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Persuade, do not inform" icon="rocket">
    An informative talk dumps data and hopes; a persuasive one moves people. If the audience leaves knowing more but doing nothing, the presentation failed - information is a means, action is the end.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Point A to Point B" icon="star">
    Map where the audience stands now and pinpoint where you need them. The gap between A and B is your actual job; everything on stage should shorten that gap.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Audience-first planning" icon="open-book">
    Begin with who is in the room and what you want from them, then reverse-engineer the content. The audience, not your slide library, is the starting point.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Ideas, then Flow Structure" icon="approve-check">
    Separate the two acts. Brainstorm freely, then impose one narrative order - chronological, problem/solution, numerical - so the sequence has a logic the audience can follow.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Story linkage" icon="random">
    A presentation is a story, and stories connect. Use verbal bridges so each idea flows into the next; do not leave the audience to guess how slide 4 relates to slide 3.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Less is More on the slide" icon="magnifier">
    Thin text, one point per slide, illustrative graphics. Overloaded slides split attention and drain the speaker of authority. The deck supports you; it is not the show.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Rehearse aloud" icon="seti:audio">
    Verbalisation is a distinct skill from writing. Practising the spoken words - transitions, emphasis, timing - is what turns a good structure into a confident, persuasive delivery.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## How the book unfolds

Weissman opens by naming the disease - presentations that inform instead of persuade, and that ignore WIIFY - then supplies the cure in order. First the audience-first mindset and the A-to-B, Point B framing. Next the creative work: brainstorming ideas and selecting a Flow Structure so the story exists before any slide does. Then the visual discipline of "Less is More," turning graphics into aids rather than crutches. He closes on delivery - verbalising and rehearsing so the message lands in a live room. Throughout, he draws on real roadshow and boardroom coaching for concrete before-and-after examples.

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Write Point B in one sentence.** State exactly what you want the audience to think, feel, or do by the end. If you cannot, stop - you are not ready to plan content.

2. **Profile the audience.** Name who they are, what they already believe (Point A), and what they care about. This is where the WIIFY comes from.

3. **State the WIIFY for every point.** For each message, name the benefit *to them*. If a point has no WIIFY, cut it or reframe it until it does.

4. **Brainstorm ideas, then pick one flow.** Get all the raw ideas out first. Then choose a single narrative structure - chronological, problem/solution, numerical - and sequence the ideas along it.

5. **Draft the spoken story before the deck.** Write or outline the talk as words and transitions, so the linkages exist independent of any slide.

6. **Build slides last, and keep them thin.** One idea per slide, minimal text, graphics that illustrate. You carry the message; the slide only points at it.

7. **Rehearse out loud, ideally to a listener.** Verbalise the whole talk, fix the transitions that stumble, and confirm each WIIFY actually lands.

</Steps>

## Worth knowing

Weissman built this from coaching executives ahead of IPO roadshows, where a single unconvincing session can move real money - which is why the advice is relentlessly about persuasion and the audience rather than aesthetics. The ideas predate but reinforce the wider "death by PowerPoint" critique: the tool is not the enemy, but reaching for it first is. ## Lines worth keeping

> *The goal of a presentation is not to inform but to persuade - to move the audience from Point A to Point B.*

> *In every moment the audience is silently asking one question: "What's In It For Me?" Your job is to answer it, from their point of view, always.*

> *Ideas first, structure second, slides last - never open PowerPoint to think.*

> *Less is more: the slide supports the speaker, it never replaces the speaker.*

> *A feature describes what you have; a benefit describes what it does for them - and only the benefit persuades.*

> *You cannot rehearse a presentation on paper. Say it out loud, or you have not rehearsed it.*
