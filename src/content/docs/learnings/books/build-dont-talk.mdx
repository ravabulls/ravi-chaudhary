**Book:** Build, Don't Talk — Things You Wish You Were Taught in School
**Author:** Raj Shamani
**My take:** The title is a warning against the exact trap ambitious people fall into — mistaking talking about work for doing it. But the honest tension in the book is that Shamani spends most of it teaching you how to *communicate*, because in the real economy the work and the ability to make people see the work are not separable. What I took from it: build something real first, then learn to package and communicate it so it actually reaches people. Substance without visibility dies quietly; visibility without substance collapses on contact. You need both, in that order.

---

## The Single Big Idea

Nobody rewards potential — the world rewards demonstrated value that other people can *see*. School trains you to accumulate credentials and wait to be chosen. The real world runs on the opposite logic: you build something of value, and then you make it visible through deliberate communication, personal branding, and relationships. **"Build, don't talk"** is the priority order, not a rejection of communication — first there must be something real, and *then* the skill of getting it seen is what compounds it.

Shamani's underlying claim is that the highest-leverage skills of a career are the ones no classroom teaches: how to build a personal brand, how to communicate an idea so it lands, how to network by giving before taking, how to think about money as something you grow rather than earn, and how to keep going through failure and criticism. These are learnable skills, not personality traits — and in a world where attention is the scarce resource, the person who can both do the work *and* make it visible wins.

## Speech Pointers

*Narrative beats organised by phase. Not a script — the intellectual skeleton to build from in your own voice.*

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="Opening Hook">
    **The scenario to open with**

    - Two people finish the same degree, with the same grades. Five years later one is stuck and invisible; the other is known, sought out, and building. Same credential — completely different outcomes.
    - We were taught that if we study hard and collect the right qualifications, the world will notice and reward us. So we wait to be chosen.
    - The world does not work that way. Nobody is coming to discover you. Value that nobody can see is value that doesn't count.
    - The title says it plainly: build, don't talk. But there's a twist most people miss — building isn't enough on its own either.
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="The Conflict">
    **The uncomfortable truth to surface**

    - School optimised us for permission — grades, degrees, waiting for the gatekeeper to say yes. The real world runs on the opposite: you demonstrate value and make it visible, no permission required.
    - But there's a trap on the other side too. Plenty of people only talk — they build a personality around ideas they never execute. That gets exposed fast.
    - So both failure modes are real: the silent builder nobody hears about, and the loud talker with nothing behind the words.
    - The skills that resolve this tension — personal branding, communication, networking, financial literacy — are exactly the ones no classroom ever taught us.
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="The Insight">
    **The reframe**

    - Build first — do the real work, acquire the real skill. Then broadcast — make it visible through deliberate communication and a consistent personal brand. Order matters: substance, then visibility.
    - Your personal brand is just your reputation operating at scale — what people associate with your name when you're not in the room. You already have one; the only question is whether you shape it on purpose.
    - Networks are built by giving before taking. Be useful to people before there's anything in it for you, and reputation compounds on your behalf.
    - And treat communication as a core technical skill, not a soft one. The ability to make a complex idea simple and memorable is what converts your work into decisions, opportunities, and influence.
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="The Close">
    **How to land it**

    - Stop waiting to be chosen. Nobody is going to discover the value you're sitting on — you have to build it and then make it impossible to ignore.
    - But don't mistake noise for progress. Talk that isn't backed by real work is a debt that always comes due.
    - Build something real. Then learn to communicate it so clearly that the right people cannot look away. That combination — substance plus visibility — is the whole game.
    - The things you wish you'd been taught in school are learnable right now. The only cost is starting before you feel ready.
  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Practical Takeaways

<Steps>

1. **Ship something visible before you feel ready.** Pick one real piece of work — a dashboard, a written analysis, a framework — and publish it where relevant people can see it. Demonstrated value beats described potential every time. Waiting for "ready" is just talking with extra steps.

2. **Define your one-line brand.** Decide what you want people to associate with your name — the intersection you own. Then make your public output consistently reinforce that theme instead of scattering across unrelated topics. Consistency is what turns activity into reputation.

3. **Give value before you ask for anything.** For every person or community you want to be part of your network, find one genuinely useful thing to offer first — an insight, an introduction, real help. Build the relationship on generosity, not on the ask.

4. **Practise making the complex simple.** Take one piece of your work and rewrite it so a smart person outside your field understands it in a minute. Do this repeatedly — the skill of simplifying without dumbing down is the highest-leverage communication habit you can build.

5. **Treat money as a skill to learn, not a number to receive.** Deliberately build financial literacy — how income streams, assets, and investing actually work — the same way you'd learn a professional tool. Start small, start early, and let compounding do the heavy lifting.

6. **Separate building from broadcasting — and do both.** Protect deep, heads-down time to actually build, and separately schedule time to communicate and share. Don't let broadcasting crowd out building, and don't let building stay invisible. The two are different jobs and both are non-negotiable.

</Steps>

:::tip[Connection to my work]
My whole trajectory is a live test of this book's thesis. Eight years of customs enforcement produced real, hard-won capability — risk models, dismantled fraud networks, revenue recovered — but that value was locked inside one institution, invisible to anyone outside it. Moving into an MBA and a new market, the "build" was already done; the missing half was the "broadcast" — communicating that background so it reads as an asset rather than an unusual detour. This site is that principle applied deliberately: taking real work and making it visible, consistent, and legible to people who were never in the room where it happened. The analysis was always the build. Learning to package and communicate it is the multiplier I was never formally taught — and it is the difference between expertise that compounds and expertise that stays hidden.
:::

:::note[The leadership implication]
For a team, "build, don't talk" is a caution against confusing activity and visibility with impact — but the reverse failure is just as costly. Organisations routinely lose the value of excellent work because it is never communicated to the people who make decisions; the best analysis in the building might as well not exist if leadership cannot follow it. A good leader builds a culture that rewards substance *and* insists that substance be made visible and legible — protecting deep work while treating clear communication as a core deliverable, not an afterthought. The aim is a team where nobody is rewarded for empty talk, and nobody's real work is allowed to die in silence.
:::
