## Three things to remember

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="1 · Roger, not Tiger" icon="star">
    Tiger drilled one sport from infancy; Roger Federer played many and specialised late. Both reached the top — but the sampling path, not the head start, is the rule that elite performers almost universally follow.
  </Card>
  <Card title="2 · Kind vs. wicked" icon="warning">

  </Card>
  <Card title="3 · Breadth is the engine" icon="puzzle">

## The big idea

The whole case for specialising early rests on a hidden assumption — that chess and golf represent all meaningful work. They do not. Those are kind learning environments where rules hold, feedback is instant, and repetition builds reliable expertise. Most of what matters is wicked: rules are unclear, feedback is delayed, and yesterday's pattern actively misleads. There, the person who sampled widely, changed direction, and connected distant fields outperforms the early specialist — because their range is structurally required, not a lucky bonus.

## The key ideas

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Kind vs. wicked environments" icon="information">
    Kahneman and Klein both proved right: expertise is powerful where patterns repeat and feedback is instant, and dangerous where they do not. Most professional life is wicked, and the mental model built in kind domains becomes a trap.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The sampling period" icon="open-book">
    Across sport, music, and science, elite performers almost universally sample broadly before narrowing. Sampling is not lost time — it is how match quality is found and analogical capital is built. It ends when real fit appears, not at a prescribed age.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Match quality over grit" icon="approve-check">
    Match quality is the fit between the work and who you actually are. Van Gogh and Gauguin failed at several careers before finding theirs. Repeated switching in pursuit of fit is not failure — it is the algorithm behind the most fulfilling careers.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Analogies and the outside view" icon="magnifier">
    Experts default to the inside view and overrate their own case. The outside view builds a reference class of structurally similar cases. Kepler cracked planetary motion through distant analogies alone — the more distant the analogy, the more creative the solution.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Foxes beat hedgehogs" icon="list-format">
    Tetlock's study of expert prediction was damning: the more famous the expert, the worse the forecast. Hedgehogs force every event through one lens; foxes range across many, update constantly, and win. Range is an epistemic advantage, not just a career one.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Desirable difficulties" icon="right-caret">
    Slow, effortful learning beats fast learning. Struggling to generate an answer, spacing practice, and testing yourself all feel like falling behind in the moment yet produce far stronger long-term retention.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Treat a winding path as capital, not apology.** Name what each earlier thread taught you — every field sampled is a reference library the single-track specialist does not have.

2. **Build a reference class before big decisions.** Force out five to ten structurally similar past cases, whatever their surface differences, and ask what happened to them. The outside view beats inside-view optimism.

3. **Practise analogical thinking on purpose.** When stuck, strip the problem of its domain details and hunt for the same structure in an unrelated field.

4. **Protect unstructured exploration time.** The breakthrough rarely comes from more of your main skill — it comes from its collision with something you explored for no reason.

5. **Seek disconfirming evidence.** In wicked domains the feedback you naturally get can mislead. Invite people who disagree to argue against you — build the fox habit of checking whether you are wrong.

</Steps>
