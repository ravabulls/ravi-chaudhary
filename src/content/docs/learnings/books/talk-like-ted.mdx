**Author:** Carmine Gallo
**In one line:** Great talks win because they make an audience *feel* an idea — emotion first, then evidence, wrapped around three simple points.

## Three things to remember

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="1 · Emotion opens the door" icon="heart">
    People decide with feeling and justify with facts. Lead with a story or a human stake **before** the data — open the heart, then the head.
  </Card>
  <Card title="2 · The Rule of Three" icon="list-format">
    The mind holds about three things. Group everything into **three** buckets; a fourth point doesn't add information, it erases the other three.
  </Card>
  <Card title="3 · One jaw-dropping moment" icon="star">
    Every talk needs a single striking image, number, or demo that resets the room. The concrete beats the abstract, and it's what people carry home.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## The big idea

Being right is not enough — if an idea doesn't land, it doesn't move anyone. Most presentations fail from **cognitive backlog**: the audience is buried in data with no narrative spine, so they resist the *effort* of processing it, not the findings themselves. The fix is to stop *reporting* and start *illuminating* — engage **Heart** (emotion), **Head** (novelty), and **Gut** (a memorable jolt) together.

## The key ideas

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Emotion → logic → credibility" icon="information">
    The most persuasive talks run mostly on **story and emotion**, then supporting **data**, then a light touch of **credibility** — roughly in that order. Most professional talks invert it and lose the room.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Teach me something new" icon="open-book">
    The brain is wired for novelty. A talk you remember reveals an *unknown-unknown* — a pattern or reframe you hadn't seen before.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The 18-minute rule" icon="approve-check">
    Eighteen minutes is long enough to build a real argument and short enough to avoid fatigue. Even in a long meeting, the core should land inside 18.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The one-sentence test" icon="magnifier">
    If you can't write your core message in a single tweet-length sentence, the thinking isn't finished yet — sharpen it *before* opening the slides.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Write the one sentence first.** Summarise the whole message in one line before any slide exists. Can't? Keep thinking.

2. **Open with the jaw-dropper.** Start with the most striking consequence or number, not the methodology.

3. **Force everything into threes.** Findings, recommendations, updates — three buckets, always.

4. **Cut any slide over ~40 words.** If it reads like a document, it *is* one — make it a hand-out and drop it from the talk.

</Steps>
