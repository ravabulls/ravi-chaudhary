**Book:** I Will Teach You to Be Rich: No Guilt. No Excuses. No BS. Just a 6-Week Program That Works (second edition, 2019)
**Author:** Ramit Sethi
**In one line:** Getting rich is not about skipping lattes or picking hot stocks - it is a handful of big decisions, set up once and automated, so your money flows to the things you love while boring, low-cost investments grow in the background.

<Aside type="note" title="Three countries, one system">
Sethi writes for Americans, and every account, law and figure in the book is American. These notes first cover the book itself and its **US playbook**, then translate the same six-week system for **Germany and the EU** and for **India**. Those two sections are *beyond the book*: they apply Sethi's methods to local rules, checked in November 2026. Tax rules and limits change every year, so treat them as a starting point, not as financial advice.
</Aside>

## Three things to remember

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="1 · Spend extravagantly on what you love, cut mercilessly on what you don't" icon="heart">
    Sethi hates budgets because nobody keeps them. Instead he uses a **Conscious Spending Plan**: of your take-home pay, **50-60 percent** to fixed costs, **10 percent** to investments, **5-10 percent** to savings goals and **20-35 percent** to guilt-free spending. Get the **Big Wins** right - automation, the right job, a negotiated salary, the big purchases - and you never have to feel guilty about a coffee again.
  </Card>
  <Card title="2 · Automate it once, then live your life" icon="setting">
    Willpower fails; systems don't. In the **Automatic Money Flow**, your pay arrives and is routed straight to retirement accounts, savings goals, bills and the full credit card balance before you can spend it. *Your money management must happen by default.* Set up properly, it takes less than ninety minutes a month.
  </Card>
  <Card title="3 · Boring, cheap investing beats the experts" icon="approve-check">
    Nobody can time the market, actively managed funds fail to beat it about 75 percent of the time, and **fees compound** - a 1 percent fee can eat around 28 percent of your returns over 35 years. Start early, buy low-cost index funds (one **target date fund** is the "85 Percent Solution") and keep buying when markets fall. *Would you rather be sexy or rich?*
  </Card>

## The big idea

Sethi's own money story starts with failure. His parents told him he would have to pay for university with scholarships, so "like a good Indian son" he applied for about sixty and won hundreds of thousands of dollars - then invested his first $2,000 cheque and promptly lost half of it. That sent him to personal-finance books, informal classes he taught at Stanford and, in 2004, the *I Will Teach You to Be Rich* blog. The book followed in 2009; this second edition (2019) keeps the system and adds the psychology - the **invisible money scripts** we absorb from parents and society, such as *"You're throwing your money away on rent"*, *"Credit cards are a scam"* or *"The stock market is gambling."*

His diagnosis is that money is like food. We don't track what we spend, we spend more than we admit, and we debate minutiae - interest rates, hot stocks - instead of acting. Most people do not need an adviser or a clever strategy. They need accounts at solid banks, automatic money management, a few simple investments and **thirty years** of letting money grow. The book's slogan for this is the **85 Percent Solution**: *I'd rather act and get it 85 percent right than do nothing.* Being **sexy** - trading, tipping stocks, chasing the best rate - feels clever; being **rich** comes from buy-and-hold investing that wins over the long term.

Time matters more than brilliance. In the book's table, Sally invests $200 a month from 35 to 45 and then stops; Dan invests the same $200 a month from 45 to 65. At 8 percent, Sally has **$181,469** at 65 and Dan **$118,589** - he invested for twice as long and is still about $60,000 behind. *The single most important thing you can do to be rich is to start early.* And "rich" is personal. Sethi's own definition is making career decisions without money as the reason, helping his parents retire, and living in a nice New York apartment while not owning a car. The system exists to fund your **Rich Life**, whatever that means to you - which is why he calls it spending outside the spreadsheet.

## The heart of it

The book is a **six-week program**. Each week sets up one piece of financial infrastructure, and each builds on the one before.

<Steps>

1. **Week 1 - Optimize your credit cards** <Badge text="credit" variant="tip" />
   Get your credit report and score, make sure your cards charge no fees, automate paying the full balance, negotiate fees and interest, and - if you have debt - make a dated plan to pay it off.

2. **Week 2 - Beat the banks** <Badge text="banks" variant="tip" />
   Set up a no-fee, no-minimum checking account and a high-interest savings account at a *separate* bank, split into sub-accounts for each savings goal.

3. **Week 3 - Get ready to invest** <Badge text="accounts" variant="tip" />
   Open the right investment accounts in the right order - the Ladder of Personal Finance - even if you start with just $50 or $100 a month.

4. **Week 4 - Conscious spending** <Badge text="plan" variant="tip" />
   Find out what you actually spend, then build a Conscious Spending Plan that sends money to what you love and cuts what you don't.

5. **Week 5 - Save while sleeping** <Badge text="automate" variant="tip" />
   Link every account, move all bills to one date and set up the Automatic Money Flow so the plan runs itself.

6. **Week 6 - Invest** <Badge text="invest" variant="tip" />
   Learn why experts can't beat the market, then pick one target date fund or a few index funds, buy them and automate monthly contributions.

</Steps>

After the six weeks, **Chapter 8** shows how to maintain and grow the system (feed it more, rebalance once a year, run an annual check-up), and **Chapter 9** turns to what the money is for: relationships, weddings, salary negotiation, cars, houses and giving back.

### The three tools that carry the system

**1. The Ladder of Personal Finance - which accounts, in which order.** Each rung builds on the previous one; if you can't reach the top, do your best for now.

<Steps>

1. **Take the full 401(k) match.** If your employer matches contributions, put in just enough to get 100 percent of the match. *This is free money, and there is, quite simply, no better deal.*

2. **Pay off credit cards and other debt.** Card debt at around 14 percent interest beats any investment return you could hope for.

3. **Open a Roth IRA** and contribute as much as you can.

4. **Max out the 401(k)** - go back and contribute above the match, up to the annual limit.

5. **Use a Health Savings Account (HSA)** if you have one - it doubles as an investment account with unusual tax advantages.

6. **Open a regular taxable investment account** for anything left over - and invest in yourself (a business, a degree), because there's often no better investment than your own career.

</Steps>

**2. The Conscious Spending Plan - how much goes where.** Percentages are of take-home pay, after tax.

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Fixed costs · 50-60%" icon="document">
    Rent, utilities, insurance, debt payments, groceries, transport, phone. Total last month's bills from your statements - "85 percent of the way there" - then **add 15 percent** for costs you forgot (car repairs, doctor visits). Sethi even keeps a "stupid mistakes" line, now $200 a month.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Investments · 10%" icon="rocket">
    Retirement accounts first. Contributions taken from your pay before it reaches you (like a 401(k)) count towards the 10 percent. If you invest less, find the money elsewhere - usually in guilt-free spending.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Savings goals · 5-10%" icon="star">
    Predictable expenses you pretend are surprises: holiday gifts, trips, a wedding, a house deposit, an emergency fund. *You already know you're going to buy Christmas gifts every December! Plan for it in January.*
  </Card>
  <Card title="Guilt-free spending · 20-35%" icon="heart">
    Restaurants, bars, clothes, trips - whatever you love. Because saving and investing already happened automatically, this money is genuinely free to spend, with no guilt.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

**3. The Automatic Money Flow - when it moves.** Assuming you are paid on the 1st:

<Steps>

1. **1st-2nd: pay arrives.** Part goes straight to your 401(k); the rest lands in checking. Treat checking like an e-mail inbox - everything arrives there and is then filtered away. Keep a $500 buffer at first.

2. **5th: savings and investing.** Automatic transfers to your savings goals (typically 5-10 percent) and to your Roth IRA (about 10 percent of take-home pay, minus what already went to the 401(k)). Waiting until the 5th gives you a few days to cancel if pay is late.

3. **7th: bills and the card.** Bills are paid automatically, mostly by credit card for points and protection, and an automatic transfer pays the card **in full** - so you never pay interest or a late fee.

</Steps>

<Aside type="tip">
**How the tools fit together.** The ladder decides *which accounts* to open, the Conscious Spending Plan decides *how much* goes into each, the Automatic Money Flow decides *when* it moves, and Week 6 decides *what to buy* inside the investment accounts. Set it up once over six weeks; afterwards your main job is to feed it and leave it alone.
</Aside>

## The key ideas

### Mindset and the Rich Life

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Sexy or rich?" icon="star">
    People love to debate hedge funds and hot stocks - Sethi compares them to *two elementary school tennis players arguing about the string tension of their racquets*. Experts' stock picks are wrong more than half the time. Boring buy-and-hold investing wins over the long term. **Do this:** notice when you are discussing money instead of acting on it, and do the next small action instead.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The 85 Percent Solution" icon="approve-check">
    Getting started matters more than becoming an expert. A system that is "good enough" now beats a perfect one you never build, and mistakes made early cost little. **Do this:** set a deadline for each decision - Sethi gives five minutes to choosing a debt-payoff method - and move on.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Invisible money scripts" icon="comment">
    Psychologist Brad Klontz describes them as unconscious beliefs about money handed down from childhood. Sethi lists debt scripts such as *"Everyone has credit card debt"* or *"I'm just trying to do my best"* - the last one, he says, is the most hopeless because it means "life happens to me". **Do this:** write down three things your family always said about money and ask whether each still serves you.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Big Wins, not lattes" icon="rocket">
    The Big Wins are *the five to ten things that get you disproportionate results*: automating saving and investing, finding a job you love, negotiating salary, and the big purchases. *There's a limit to how much you can cut, but no limit to how much you can earn* - people on $50,000 and $750,000 a year buy the same bread. Sethi's three levers form the CEO Method: Cut costs, Earn more, Optimize spending. **Do this:** list your five biggest money decisions of the next year and spend your energy there, not on coupons.
  </Card>
  <Card title="From the clouds to the street" icon="sun">
    "Freedom" and "security" are too vague to motivate. Ask *why* you want your next $10,000 and make the answer concrete: a taxi to happy hour instead of a sweaty train, a friend's ticket on a trip, a museum or Broadway show every quarter, or - one of Sethi's first goals - ordering appetizers at a restaurant, which he never did as a child. Every December he and his wife ask what they could do next year that they'll remember for fifty years. **Do this:** write three street-level things your money is for.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Ignore the noise" icon="warning">
    After his "Save $1,000 in 30 Days" challenge, Sethi got furious replies explaining why it couldn't work for them. *Cynics don't want results; they want an excuse to not take action.* Friends and family will have tips; listen politely, then stick to the program. Being "unapologetically different" is part of a Rich Life. **Do this:** check investments no more than once a month and spend under ninety minutes a month on money.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

### Credit: make the card companies work for you

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Your credit score is a Big Win" icon="bars">
    The US FICO score (300-850) is built from payment history (35 percent), amounts owed (30), length of history (15), new credit (10) and types of credit (10). On a $200,000, 30-year mortgage in 2018, a top score meant 4.279 percent and $355,420 in total; a score of 620-639 meant 5.868 percent and $425,585. Sethi's own car loan came at 4.9 percent instead of 1.9 percent because he had "not enough credit sources" - more than $2,200 extra. **Do this:** check your credit report for errors once a year.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The Six Commandments of Credit Cards" icon="list-format">
    **Pay on time** and automate it - one missed payment can cost 100+ points, a late fee around $35 and an APR up to 30 percent. **Get fees waived.** **Negotiate a lower APR** (it works about half the time). **Keep your main cards for a long time, active but simple** - put a small automatic charge on each at least every three months. **Get more credit only if you have no debt** (utilization makes up 30 percent of your score). **Use the perks** - extended warranties, rental-car and trip-cancellation cover. **Do this:** call your card company once a year and ask for the full list of benefits.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Negotiate like an Indian" icon="comment">
    Sethi's father bargained five straight days for a car and then walked out over free floor mats. The son's version is a polite phone call. On a late fee: *"I've been a customer for four years and I'd hate for this one fee to drive me away from your service. What can you do to remove the late fee?"* Always end with strength: not "Can you remove this?" but "I'd like to have this removed." Keep a log of every call - date, time, name, rep ID, notes. **Do this:** make one call this week about a fee you are paying.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The minimum-payment trap" icon="warning">
    At 14 percent APR with a 2 percent minimum payment, a $1,000 phone takes 9 years and 2 months to repay and costs $732.76 in interest; $10,000 of furniture takes over 32 years and $13,332.06. Of $5,000 in card debt, Dumb Dan paying the minimum needs 25+ years and $6,322.22 of interest; Smart Sally paying a fixed $100 is done in 6 years 4 months ($2,547.85); at $200 it takes 2 years 6 months ($946.20). **Do this:** always pay a fixed amount well above the minimum.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Five steps out of card debt" icon="approve-check">
    1) **Find out exactly what you owe** - card, balance, APR, minimum, in one sheet. 2) **Decide what to pay first** - highest APR (cheapest) or smallest balance (the motivating "snowball") - in under five minutes. 3) **Negotiate the APR down.** 4) **Decide where the money comes from** - not balance transfers, retirement accounts or home-equity loans, but cut spending. 5) **Get started this week.** A plan turns debt from a "hot" emotional topic into a "cool" math problem. **Do this:** write down the date your debt will be gone.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Mistakes to avoid" icon="seti:lock">
    Don't open store cards for a discount - the shopper saving 10 percent on $40 of socks opens a high-interest card to save $4: *whenever you say, "It couldn't hurt," IT VERY MUCH COULD HURT.* Don't close accounts within six months of applying for a mortgage or car loan. Don't play the zero-percent transfer game for a few dollars. Do buy big items by card, because the card company will fight a merchant dispute for you. **Do this:** keep two or three good cards and stop collecting more.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

### Banks: no fees, no tricks

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Two accounts at two banks" icon="setting">
    Your checking account is *the backbone of your financial system*; savings is where money for specific goals waits. Sethi wants them at **separate banks**, because savings you can't see instantly are savings you don't raid. His own setup: an online checking account for everything coming in and a separate online savings account that pulls a fixed amount every month and is "a receiver, not a sender". **Do this:** keep about one and a half months of expenses in checking and move the rest to savings, even if it's only $30.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Sub-accounts for every goal" icon="list-format">
    A good savings bank lets you create virtual sub-accounts - emergency fund, wedding, house deposit, next holiday - each with its own automatic transfer. Every dollar then has a job, and spending on a goal you saved for carries no guilt. **Do this:** open one sub-account per goal and give each a monthly amount.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Don't be a rate chaser" icon="random">
    Sethi's reader Mike moved $40,000 between banks every few months to earn 0.65-0.85 percent more - about $300 a year, or $0.82 a day. Teaser rates ("6 percent for the first two months!") are a red flag in themselves. The real money is in avoiding fees and investing. *Stop focusing on picking up pennies and instead focus on the Big Wins.* **Do this:** pick good no-fee accounts once and move on.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Fees are negotiable" icon="comment">
    One overdraft fee can wipe out a year of interest. Banks spend a lot to win customers and don't want to lose you over a $5 monthly fee, so first-time fees are usually waived if you ask. Watch for minimum-balance requirements, up-sells to "premium" accounts and bundled credit cards. **Do this:** call and say you'd like to confirm your account has no fees and no minimums - and threaten to leave if it does.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

### Accounts: invest before you feel ready

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Saving is not enough" icon="bars">
    Most people don't invest not for lack of information but because of fear, complexity and inertia. At 8 percent, $50 a week becomes $39,181 in ten years. *It's ironic that people are afraid of "possibly" losing money in the stock market, when they will certainly run out of money if they don't invest.* **Do this:** open an investment account this week, even with $50 a month.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Employer money first" icon="star">
    Only one in five people contributes enough to get the full company match. With a 1:1 match up to 5 percent on a $60,000 salary, $3,000 of yours becomes $6,000 a year; started at 25, Sethi estimates over $1.6 million with the match against just over $800,000 without. Pre-tax contributions also mean "usually 25 to 40 percent more" money compounding. **Do this:** find out exactly what your employer adds and never leave it on the table.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Discount broker, not salesperson" icon="magnifier">
    Use a low-cost discount brokerage, not a full-service firm that charges to sell you research. Robo-advisers are fine but, Sethi argues, their fees don't justify what they offer. *Whether you choose a robo-advisor or Vanguard or another low-fee brokerage is a minor detail. Pick one and move on.* **Do this:** choose a provider in one evening, not one month.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Don't cash out when you change jobs" icon="warning">
    Sethi cites a figure that half of twentysomethings cash out their retirement accounts when they leave a job - paying tax and a penalty and losing decades of growth. Move the money to your own account (or the new employer's plan) instead. **Do this:** on your last day, start the transfer, not the withdrawal.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

### Conscious spending

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Cheap vs. conscious" icon="heart">
    *Cheap people care about the cost of something. Conscious spenders care about the value of something.* Lisa spends about $5,000 a year on shoes but lives in a tiny room and funds her retirement; John spends over $21,000 a year going out, but owns hardly any furniture and invests automatically; Julie, on about $40,000 in San Francisco, still saves over $6,000 a year. All three pay themselves first. **Do this:** name the two things you'll spend extravagantly on - and two you'll cut.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Go after one or two Big Wins" icon="rocket">
    An 80/20 look at spending usually shows most overspending comes from a few categories - for Sethi, eating out, travel and clothes. Cut gradually: from about $500 a month on eating out, to $475, $450, $400 and so on, halving it in six months. *I'd rather have people cut their spending by 10 percent and sustain it for thirty years than cut 50 percent for just a month.* **Do this:** finish the sentence "I probably spend too much on ___" and target that.
  </Card>
  <Card title="À la carte and envelopes" icon="pencil">
    A reader on $50,000 found 30 percent of her after-tax income went to subscriptions. Cancel them all for two months and buy only what you use - gym members overestimate their visits by more than 70 percent. For a problem category, use envelopes: a fixed monthly amount, and when it's empty, it's empty. *These are training wheels. Build the habit first. Systematize it later.* **Do this:** list every subscription and cancel the ones you wouldn't buy again today.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Earn more when you can't cut more" icon="right-arrow">
    If the plan doesn't fit, earning is the answer. For a raise, start three to six months before your review: agree specific goals with your boss, send updates, collect evidence, then ask as a partner - "How do we make this work?" Other routes: a better-paid job (*you have more leverage than you'll ever have* during hiring) or freelance work. **Do this:** book a meeting this month to ask your boss how you can be a top performer.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Irregular costs and extra money" icon="list-format">
    Known irregular costs - Christmas, registrations, holidays - become monthly savings goals ($500 of gifts means $42 a month from January). Unknown ones sit in the 15 percent buffer. Windfalls: half for fun, half invested. Raises: *It's okay to increase your standard of living a little - but bank the rest* - of a $4,000 raise, spend $1,000 and invest $3,000. **Do this:** decide your raise rule before the raise arrives.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The plan says no for you" icon="seti:lock">
    With a plan, turning down an expensive invitation is easy: "Sorry, it's not in my plan this month." It isn't personal - it's just your system. Review it weekly at first (thirty minutes on a Sunday) and expect it to become accurate after a year or two. **Do this:** run your plan for one month before fine-tuning a single percentage.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

### Automation: save while sleeping

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="The power of defaults" icon="setting">
    When companies switched to automatic enrolment, 401(k) participation rose from about 40 percent to more than 90 percent. People do whatever takes no effort, so make saving the default. *Nobody really cares about managing their money. Hell, I don't even care.* **Do this:** treat every money habit as something to automate, not to remember.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Do more before you do less" icon="right-arrow">
    Linking accounts and moving billing dates takes a few hours (allow three to five days for links to verify); after that, money management needs almost no time for the rest of your life. Call each company to align due dates: *"Hi, I'm currently being billed on the seventeenth of each month, and I'd like to change that to the first of the month."* **Do this:** block one afternoon for the setup.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Start small, let it grow" icon="star">
    If you can't afford your target savings yet, set up a $5 transfer "to prove to yourself that it works" - then raise it. The same system that handles $100 a month handles $10,000. Expect some waste: Sethi no longer chases a $5 extra tip, because *in any meaningful system, there's always a certain amount of waste.* **Do this:** switch on every transfer now, even at token amounts.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Irregular income" icon="warning">
    Freelancers first work out bare-bones monthly costs, then build a buffer of three months of them (six is better) before investing, then pay themselves a steady "salary" from it. Sethi advises setting aside 40 percent of freelance income for tax. If you're paid twice a month, run the flow twice or keep a buffer to simulate monthly pay. **Do this:** build the buffer first, then automate as if salaried.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

### The myth of financial expertise

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Nobody can time the market" icon="seti:clock">
    A Putnam study over fifteen years: staying in the S&P 500 returned 7.7 percent a year; missing the ten best days cut it to 2.96 percent; missing the thirty best days made it negative. $10,000 became $30,711, $15,481 or $6,873. *Focus on time in the market, not timing the market.* **Do this:** keep investing automatically, especially when the news is frightening.
  </Card>
  <Card title="How the industry hides failure" icon="magnifier">
    Fund managers fail to beat the market 75 percent of the time. Funds that fail are closed and vanish from the statistics (survivorship bias); companies start "incubator" funds and market only the winners; star ratings don't predict success. A scammer can "predict" stocks perfectly by mailing half his list one tip and half the opposite. **Do this:** judge any fund on ten or more years of after-fee results, never on last year.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Fees compound too" icon="bars">
    According to the book, a 1 percent fee costs about 28 percent of your returns over 35 years (and 39 percent over fifty), and a 2 percent fee can cost 63 percent over fifty years. At $100 a month for 25 years, a 0.14 percent index fund grows to $92,967 against $81,007 at 1 percent. Sethi's target is 0.1-0.3 percent. *In investing, fees are your enemy.* **Do this:** look up the total annual cost of every fund you hold.
  </Card>
  <Card title="You probably don't need an adviser" icon="person">
    Most young people don't. If you do use one, they must be a **fiduciary** paid a fee, not a commission. Red-flag words: "whole life insurance", "annuities". Ask: *"What were your after-tax, after-fee returns for the last ten, fifteen, and twenty years?"* Sethi likens wealth managers to a firm that charges $2 for worse salt but delivers it on a leather tray. **Do this:** put every adviser conversation in writing and ask how they are paid.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

### Investing made simple

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Asset allocation beats stock picking" icon="puzzle">
    A 1986 study found that more than 90 percent of a portfolio's volatility comes from asset allocation - the split between stocks, bonds and cash. *Your investment plan is more important than your actual investments.* Diversification goes deep within a class; allocation goes across classes. Young investors can be mostly in stocks; bonds come in as you age. **Do this:** decide your stocks-to-bonds split before choosing any fund.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Target date funds: the 85 Percent Solution" icon="approve-check">
    Sethi's favourite investment: one fund, named after the year you expect to retire (around 65), that holds a mix of funds, rebalances itself and grows more conservative with age. Not perfect, but easy and cheap. Pick a later year if you want more risk, an earlier one for less. **Do this:** if you want one decision only, make it this one.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Or build it from index funds" icon="list-format">
    For the more engaged, Yale's David Swensen allocation: 30 percent domestic stocks, 15 percent developed international, 5 percent emerging markets, 20 percent real estate investment trusts, 15 percent government bonds and 15 percent inflation-protected bonds - three to seven low-cost index funds (around 0.2 percent), bought one at a time as you meet each minimum. **Do this:** split each month's investing by your percentages, not evenly across funds.
  </Card>
  <Card title="An account is not an investment" icon="warning">
    A reader's friend paid into her retirement account for almost ten years but never chose a fund, so the money sat as cash - $3,000 that could have been worth more than $12,000. *A ROTH IRA IS JUST AN ACCOUNT.* **Do this:** after every new account, log in and check your money is actually invested.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Keep buying in a downturn" icon="right-arrow">
    *The test of a real automatic investor is not when things are going up, but when they are going down.* People who cancelled their automatic plans in late 2008 made a big mistake; in October 2018 one of Sethi's accounts fell more than $100,000 and he kept investing. Lump sums beat gradual investing about two-thirds of the time, but regular automatic buying takes the emotion out. **Do this:** never pause contributions because markets fell.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Fun money, crypto and the Amazon lesson" icon="random">
    At fifteen Sethi bought three stocks: two went to zero or bankrupt; his Amazon shares grew to $297,754. The real lesson was luck - *It's very important to know WHY you win and why you lose* - and that starting early mattered most. Keep "fun" bets (single stocks, sector funds, crypto) to 5-10 percent, after your core portfolio and six months of emergency funds. **Do this:** set a cap for speculation and write it down.
  </Card>
  <Card title="FIRE and the Crossover Point" icon="sun">
    The Crossover Point is when investment income covers all your expenses. On $80,000 a year with $6,000 monthly costs, saving 10 percent takes about 38 years; halving expenses cuts it to just over twelve; a 30 percent raise invested in full, 22; both together, nine. Sethi likes that FIRE shows big savings rates are possible but warns against a miserable, anxious path - as one early retiree wrote, *I built my savings, but I never built my life.* **Do this:** pick a savings rate that funds the future without cancelling the present.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

### Keeping the system running

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Feed the system" icon="rocket">
    Once it's set up, growth depends on how much you put in. At 8 percent, $100 a month becomes $18,294 in ten years and $95,102 in 25; $1,000 a month becomes $182,946 and $951,026. **Do this:** every time your income rises, raise your automatic transfers before your lifestyle.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Rebalance once a year" icon="random">
    If one part of your portfolio grows beyond its share, don't sell it - pause new contributions to it and send money to the others until the balance returns (in Sethi's example, about eight months). Target date funds do this for you. **Do this:** set a yearly calendar reminder.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Taxes and selling" icon="document">
    *Investing in tax-advantaged retirement accounts is the 85 Percent Solution for taxes.* Higher earnings are taxed only on the part in the higher bracket, so never refuse a raise "for tax reasons". Sell only for an emergency, a consistently terrible investment or a reached goal. For emergencies, go to savings first, then extra income, then family, then retirement money, and a credit card only as the very last resort. **Do this:** keep money you need within five years out of the market.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The annual check-up" icon="list-format">
    Every December: review the spending plan (three hours), negotiate phone, insurance, internet and bank fees (two), check retirement contributions and funds (two), review debt and your credit report (two), use card points and perks (one), plan to earn more, and review insurance and - if you have dependents - your will. Ask people five to ten years older what they wish they'd started earlier: an emergency fund of six to twelve months, the right insurance, saving for children only after your own retirement. **Do this:** put the check-up in your calendar now.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

### Money in real life

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Parents and money" icon="person">
    Parents rarely ask for help, so ask questions instead: where they learned about money, what they'd wish for, what they earn, spend and save, what fees they pay. Then suggest one or two actions. On sharing your own numbers, Sethi worried it might "get weird" with his parents and found they mostly wanted to know he was happy - *these are Indian parents I'm talking about.* *Sharing numbers without context is a bad move.* **Do this:** open with "How did you learn about money?"
  </Card>
  <Card title="Partners: the Big Meeting" icon="heart">
    Start broad and vulnerable, then hold a meeting where each partner brings accounts, debts and rates, monthly spending, income and goals. Begin with goals - including "Does either of you need to support your parents?" - and show your own spending first. If incomes differ, split costs in proportion (on $5,000 and $4,000, a $3,000 rent becomes $1,680 and $1,320). Argue about the plan, not the person. **Do this:** agree on one shared savings goal with an automatic transfer from each of you.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Weddings: plan for the $35,000 reality" icon="star">
    Everyone wants a "simple" wedding and almost nobody has one; the US average was about $35,000. Save years ahead, engaged or not: at 22 with a wedding five years out, $583 a month. Halving the guest list cuts the cost by only 25 percent because the fixed costs stay - attack those (his sister had her invitations printed in India for a fraction of the US price). **Do this:** open a wedding or big-event sub-account now. Prenups, Sethi adds, are only needed by the 1 percent with very unequal assets.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Salary: the fastest legal way to make money" icon="right-arrow">
    *Negotiating is 90 percent about mindset and 10 percent about tactics.* Nine basics include: frame everything around the company's benefit, have another offer, come prepared (most of the negotiation happens outside the room), negotiate total compensation, stay cooperative, smile and practise with tough friends. Never reveal your current salary, never make the first offer, never lie. *"Fifty thousand dollars is a great number to work from. We're in the same ballpark, but how can we get to fifty-five thousand?"* **Do this:** rehearse the conversation out loud before every offer.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Cars: keep it ten years" icon="setting">
    The biggest decision is not the brand but how long you keep it. Budget from your spending plan, calculate the total cost of ownership, buy reliability, never lease, and don't sell in fewer than seven years. Sethi had seventeen dealers bid by e-mail at year-end and bought $2,000 under invoice. **Do this:** get written quotes from several dealers and let them compete.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Houses: a purchase first, an investment second" icon="warning">
    Buy only if the numbers work: **20 percent down**, total monthly cost no more than **30 percent of gross income**, and you'll stay **ten years or more** (agents' fees alone are usually 6 percent). Yale's Robert Shiller found US home prices rose only 0.6 percent a year on average from 1915 to 2015. The true monthly cost of owning runs 40-50 percent above the mortgage payment, and renters aren't "throwing away money" - if they invest the difference. Sethi rents by choice. **Do this:** run the full buy-vs-rent numbers before you look at a single house.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Big purchases and giving back" icon="open-book">

Then give: *You don't have to be rich to be a philanthropist, just as you don't have to be rich to invest.* Sethi's community raised over $300,000 to build thirteen schools. **Do this:** ask what one big thing you'll do for others this year.

## How to use it - the US playbook (from the book)

This is Sethi's own checklist, with the American institutions he names.

<Steps>

1. **Week 1 - Credit.** Get your free credit report once a year at annualcreditreport.com and your score from MyFICO. Hold two or three no-fee cards - his rule: a travel card for travel and eating out, a cash-back card for everything else. Automate the full payment, call to waive fees and lower the APR, and if you have debt, start the five steps this week.

2. **Week 2 - Banks.** A no-fee, no-minimum checking account plus a high-yield online savings account at a different bank, with sub-accounts per goal. Avoid big banks that live on fees.

3. **Week 3 - Accounts.** Climb the ladder: 401(k) to the full match, clear card debt, Roth IRA, max the 401(k), HSA, then a taxable account - at a low-cost discount brokerage. The book's limits ($5,500 for a Roth IRA in 2018, $19,000 for a 401(k) in 2019) are long out of date; Sethi himself tells readers to search for the current figures.

4. **Week 4 - Spending.** Build the Conscious Spending Plan on take-home pay (50-60 / 10 / 5-10 / 20-35) and choose one or two Big Wins.

5. **Week 5 - Automation.** Pay day → 401(k) and checking; 5th → savings and Roth IRA; 7th → bills and the card in full.

6. **Week 6 - Investing.** In the 401(k), the most aggressive fund you're comfortable with (avoid money-market funds). In the Roth IRA, a target date fund such as Vanguard's Target Retirement 2040, 2045 or 2050 - whichever year is closest to when you turn 65 - or the Swensen mix of index funds. Automate monthly contributions.

7. **Every December - the annual check-up.** Spending plan, fees, contributions, debt, credit report, perks, earning more, insurance and will.

</Steps>

## How to use it in Germany and the EU

<Aside type="caution" title="Beyond the book">
This section is not in the book. It maps Sethi's methods onto German rules as of November 2026. Figures change every year - check them before you act, and for personal advice use a fee-only adviser (Honorarberater) or a consumer advice centre (Verbraucherzentrale).
</Aside>

**What carries over unchanged:** the Rich Life, the 85 Percent Solution, the Conscious Spending Plan, automation, low-cost index investing, negotiating and the Big Wins. **What changes:**
- **Your pension starts before your pay arrives.** You and your employer each pay roughly 9 percent of gross salary into the statutory pension, and health, care and unemployment insurance are deducted too. The 10 percent for investments in Sethi's plan therefore comes *on top* of a compulsory pension.
- **Credit means SCHUFA, not credit card history.** Credit cards matter far less, and the US points game barely exists, because EU rules cap the fees card issuers earn per transaction.
- **The 401(k) and Roth IRA** become the company pension (bAV), the new Altersvorsorgedepot from 2027, and an ordinary ETF savings plan.
- **US funds are off limits.** EU retail investors cannot buy US-domiciled funds such as Vanguard's target date funds, because they lack the EU key information document. You use **UCITS ETFs** instead, usually domiciled in Ireland or Luxembourg.

<Steps>

1. **Week 1 - SCHUFA and the cards you need**
   Your SCHUFA score (100-999, based on twelve published criteria) is free to view in the SCHUFA app or online account, and you can request a free copy of all data held on you under Article 15 GDPR. What helps: pay every bill and phone contract on time, keep a long-standing current account, avoid many credit applications in a short period. What hurts most is debt you can't service: an expensive overdraft (Dispo), revolving credit cards that only charge a small monthly instalment, buy-now-pay-later and consumer loans. Choose a free card that is **settled in full every month** by direct debit - that is Sethi's rule of paying in full, automatically, built in. The German version of his phone negotiation is the **switch**: compare and change mobile, electricity, gas, internet and car insurance (most car policies can be cancelled by 30 November for the new year), using the online cancellation button companies are required to offer.

2. **Week 2 - Girokonto plus Tagesgeld**
   Open a free current account (Girokonto) and a separate instant-access savings account (Tagesgeld), ideally at another bank, with sub-accounts or "spaces" for each savings goal. Deposits are protected up to **€100,000 per person per bank** across the EU. Don't chase introductory rates for new customers - Sethi's rate-chaser lesson applies exactly. Switching is easy: by law your new bank must offer an account-switching service that moves your standing orders and direct debits.

3. **Week 3 - The German ladder**
   1. **Free employer money: the company pension (bAV).** If you convert salary, your employer must add 15 percent when it saves social contributions; many add more. In 2026, up to €4,056 a year is free of tax and social contributions and up to €8,112 free of tax. In retirement, payouts count as taxable income and, above an allowance, attract health-insurance contributions, and insurance-based contracts can be expensive and rigid. It is strongest when your employer pays well beyond the legal 15 percent - ask HR what they add and what the contract costs.
   2. **Kill expensive debt:** overdraft, revolving card balances, instalment loans.
   3. **Emergency fund in Tagesgeld** - Sethi's ten-year plan says six to twelve months of spending eventually; start with three.
   4. **Altersvorsorgedepot (from 1 January 2027).** The Riester successor, and the closest thing to an IRA: the state adds **50 cents per euro** on the first €360 a year and **25 cents** on contributions from €361 to €1,800 - up to **€540 a year** - plus up to **€300 per child** for parents receiving child benefit (at least €120 a year paid in). Contributions up to €1,800 are tax-deductible, growth is tax-free and payouts are taxed, starting between 65 and 70. The standard product's costs are capped at 1 percent a year. No new Riester contracts can be signed from 2027; existing ones continue. Compare offers carefully - consumer advocates warn of a sales push.
   5. **ETF savings plan in an ordinary securities account** - flexible, no lock-in. Put in a tax-exemption order (Freistellungsauftrag) so that **€1,000** of investment income a year (€2,000 for couples) stays tax-free; above that, capital gains tax is 25 percent plus solidarity surcharge (26.375 percent, plus church tax if you pay it), and equity ETFs get a **30 percent partial exemption**. Accumulating funds pay a small annual advance tax (Vorabpauschale), based on a base rate of 3.20 percent for 2026.
   6. **Self-employed?** You have no bAV, so the Altersvorsorgedepot, the Rürup (basic) pension for high earners and a larger ETF plan do the work.
   For children, the state's **Frühstart-Rente** (€10 a month into a depot for each child aged 6 to 18) passed the Bundestag in October 2026 and is due to start in 2027.

4. **Week 4 - The spending plan on your net pay**
   Apply the percentages to your **net** pay. Typical fixed costs: warm rent (rent including heating and service charges), electricity, internet and phone, public transport, liability insurance (Haftpflicht - cheap and essential), household contents insurance and the broadcasting fee. Good German savings goals: holidays, Christmas, the annual service-charge settlement (Nebenkostennachzahlung), a rental deposit (up to three months' basic rent) and a car. Filing a tax return is a German Big Win - work expenses such as commuting often bring money back.

5. **Week 5 - Automate around the end of the month**
   German salaries usually arrive at the end of the month, so set standing orders (Daueraufträge) for the 1st: to Tagesgeld and its sub-accounts, and to your depot, where the ETF savings plan runs on a fixed date. The bAV is deducted through payroll; rent goes by standing order; utilities and the credit card are collected by direct debit (SEPA-Lastschrift). Keep a small buffer in the Girokonto.

6. **Week 6 - Invest with UCITS ETFs**
   Sethi's one-fund solution in Germany: one low-cost **global equity ETF** - MSCI World, MSCI ACWI or FTSE All-World - usually accumulating, with annual costs often around 0.1-0.25 percent. Since there is no ready-made target date fund in most German depots, set your risk by mixing that ETF with Tagesgeld or a euro government-bond ETF, and shift towards the safe part as you age. Sethi's red flags translate directly: avoid capital-forming life insurance and expensive unit-linked pension policies, commission-based "financial advisers", and actively managed funds with a front-end load (Ausgabeaufschlag) of up to 5 percent.

</Steps>

### Big Wins in Germany

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Salary negotiation" icon="right-arrow">
    Research market pay and any collective agreement (Tarifvertrag) for your role, negotiate the annual gross figure plus extras (bAV contribution, job ticket, training, home office). The EU Pay Transparency Directive will ban questions about previous pay and give applicants a right to know the pay range, but Germany missed the June 2026 deadline and its law is not expected before 2027 - until then, Sethi's rule stands: don't name your current salary first.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Rent or buy" icon="warning">
    Renting is normal in Germany, so Sethi's point that renters are not throwing money away fits well. Buying adds transfer tax of 3.5-6.5 percent depending on the state, around 2 percent for the notary and land registry and often an agent's fee of up to about 3.57 percent. Banks prefer you to pay at least these costs from savings. Instead of a 30-year fixed mortgage, look at a long fixed-rate period (Zinsbindung) and the right to make extra repayments (Sondertilgung). His three tests still apply.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Car or no car" icon="setting">
    Keep a car ten years, buy reliability, don't lease - leasing is popular in Germany, but Sethi's maths is the same everywhere. In cities, a public transport subscription may let you skip a car entirely, which is the biggest car win of all.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Insurance, not investment" icon="seti:lock">
    Get liability insurance, and income protection (Berufsunfähigkeitsversicherung) if you depend on your salary. Keep insurance and investing separate - Sethi: *Insurance is almost never a good investment, despite what financial salespeople (or clueless parents) will tell you.*
  </Card>
  <Card title="Elsewhere in the EU" icon="open-book">
    The €100,000 deposit guarantee and UCITS ETFs work across the EU, but tax wrappers are national: France has the PEA equity savings plan, Ireland taxes ETFs on a deemed sale every eight years, the Netherlands taxes an assumed return on wealth. Build the same ladder from your country's pieces: employer pension, a personal tax-advantaged account, then an ordinary investment account.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## How to use it in India

<Aside type="caution" title="Beyond the book">
This section is not in the book. Sethi grew up in an Indian family and the book is full of it - negotiating "like an Indian", Indian parents, four Indian weddings in a few months - but all its advice is American. This section applies his methods to Indian rules as of November 2026. Check current rates and limits, and for personal advice use a SEBI-registered investment adviser.
</Aside>

**What carries over unchanged:** the Rich Life, conscious spending, automation, low-cost index investing, negotiation and the Big Wins. **What changes:**
- **Provident fund is compulsory** for most salaried employees, and salaries are quoted as CTC, which is not what reaches your account.
- **The new tax regime is now the default**, so many old deductions no longer matter.
- **Family is part of the plan:** supporting parents, contributing to family weddings and festivals are fixed costs and savings goals, not surprises.
- **The sales culture is different:** insurance sold as investment, commission-paid fund plans, gold and property as default "investments", and trading tips on messaging apps.

<Steps>

1. **Week 1 - CIBIL and the card trap**
   India has four credit bureaus (TransUnion CIBIL, Experian, Equifax and CRIF High Mark); CIBIL scores run from 300 to 900, and 750 or more is considered good by most lenders. RBI rules give you **one free full credit report a year from each bureau**. Always pay the **total amount due**, never just the "minimum amount due" - interest on unpaid balances commonly runs above 3 percent a month, more than 40 percent a year. Lifetime-free cards are easy to get, which is Sethi's no-fee rule. Avoid converting purchases to EMIs and buy-now-pay-later, keep the share of your limit you use low, and think hard before guaranteeing anyone's loan. His phone script works here too: ask for a first-time late fee or annual fee to be reversed.

2. **Week 2 - A salary account and a separate savings home**
   Keep a salary account that charges no minimum-balance penalties, and put savings goals somewhere separate - a second bank, recurring deposits or an auto-sweep fixed deposit. Deposits are insured by DICGC up to **₹5 lakh per depositor per bank**, interest included (a rise to ₹7.5 lakh has been proposed but not adopted). Small finance banks pay more; stay within the insured limit. Watch the small fees Sethi hates: SMS charges, debit card fees, penalties for low balances.

3. **Week 3 - The Indian ladder**
   1. **Provident fund (EPF) - the employer-match rung.** You pay 12 percent of basic pay (plus dearness allowance) and your employer pays 12 percent, part of it into the pension scheme. It is part of your CTC, so it isn't free money in the US sense, but it is tax-advantaged, automatic saving. The Voluntary Provident Fund lets you add more through payroll. On a job change, transfer it using your UAN - never withdraw it.
   2. **Clear expensive debt:** card balances, personal loans, BNPL.
   3. **Emergency fund and protection.** Six months of expenses, including EMIs and support for parents, in a sweep deposit or liquid fund. Add health insurance (a family floater plus a super top-up) and term life cover if anyone depends on your income - protection, never investment.
   4. **NPS through your employer.** Under the new tax regime, your employer's NPS contribution of up to 14 percent of basic plus DA is tax-deductible - ask HR to structure part of your CTC this way. The money stays locked until 60, and newer scheme options allow private-sector subscribers a higher equity share.
   5. **Equity index funds by monthly SIP** - the Roth IRA role: flexible, liquid, low-cost.
   6. **PPF for the safe slice** - ₹500 to ₹1.5 lakh a year, 15-year term, **7.1 percent tax-free** (October to December 2026).
   **Tax:** the Income-tax Act 2025 has applied since 1 April 2026 with the same rates. Under the default new regime, income up to ₹12 lakh is effectively tax-free (₹12.75 lakh for salaried people after the ₹75,000 standard deduction). The old deductions for PPF, ELSS and life insurance premiums (for decades known as Section 80C) only help if you choose the old regime.

4. **Week 4 - The spending plan on in-hand pay**
   Build the plan on what actually reaches your account after tax and PF - not on CTC. Typical fixed costs: rent or home-loan EMI, groceries, domestic help, utilities, school fees, insurance premiums and **money you send to your parents**. Sethi's Big Meeting asks "Does either of you need to support your parents?" - give it a fixed line instead of an afterthought. Savings goals: Diwali and other festivals, family weddings and gifts, trips home, parents' medical costs.

5. **Week 5 - Automate around salary day**
   Salary usually arrives on the last or first working day. Set SIP dates two to five days later, through NACH mandates or UPI AutoPay; put recurring deposits or sweeps on the same days for savings goals; pay the credit card by auto-debit for the **total amount due**; route VPF through payroll and set a standing instruction for PPF.

6. **Week 6 - Invest the Sethi way**
   Use **low-cost index funds** - a Nifty 50 fund as the one-fund core, with Nifty Next 50 or a broad Nifty 500 fund for depth - and always choose **direct plans**. Regular plans build a distributor's commission into a higher expense ratio, exactly the fee drag Sethi warns about. Long-term equity gains above **₹1.25 lakh a year** are taxed at **12.5 percent** (after 12 months); short-term gains at 20 percent. His red flags have Indian twins: ULIPs and endowment or money-back policies that mix insurance and investment, commission-led "advice", stock tips on WhatsApp and Telegram, and futures and options trading - a 2024 SEBI study found that 93 percent of individual F&O traders lost money between FY22 and FY24. The Indian fiduciary is a SEBI-registered investment adviser (RIA), who charges a fee, not a commission.

</Steps>

### Big Wins in India

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Salary and the CTC trap" icon="right-arrow">
    Switching jobs usually brings a bigger jump than an internal raise, and Sethi's preparation rules apply: market data, competing offers, a plan for your first months. Negotiate the **fixed** component and in-hand pay, not the headline CTC padded with variable pay, ESOPs and one-time bonuses. Indian employers routinely ask for current pay and payslips, so anchor on your expected figure with market evidence.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The wedding fund" icon="star">
    Sethi's wedding chapter fits India even better than America: plan the amount early, save monthly in a dedicated account, and cut **fixed** costs (venue, decor, photography, jewellery making charges) rather than only the guest list. Talk to both families early about who pays for what, and never take a loan for a wedding.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Rent vs. buy" icon="warning">
    *"You're throwing your money away on rent"* is the first invisible script Sethi lists. Stamp duty and registration typically add around 6 to 8 percent depending on the state (some states charge women buyers less), and RBI rules cap home loans at 75-90 percent of the property value. Check RERA registration for projects under construction. His tests hold: a large down payment, a manageable EMI, a ten-year horizon.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Gold and property are not a plan" icon="bars">
    Treat gold as a small slice of the portfolio, bought through gold ETFs or funds rather than jewellery with making charges. Property is, in Sethi's words, *a purchase first - a very expensive one - and an investment second.*
  </Card>
  <Card title="Helping parents" icon="person">
    Use Sethi's question list with parents, consider health insurance for them early, and remember his warning about sharing numbers without context with relatives. Support is part of a Rich Life - plan it, automate it and protect your own retirement too.
  </Card>

## How the book unfolds

An **open letter** looks back on ten years since the first edition: the advice held up, but Sethi admits he ignored the emotions around money, was too prescriptive about what a Rich Life should be, and should never have printed bank interest rates. The **introduction**, "Would You Rather Be Sexy or Rich?", sets out the key messages, the ten rules for a Rich Life and the six-week plan. **Chapters 1 to 4** cover credit cards and debt, bank accounts, the Ladder of Personal Finance and the Conscious Spending Plan; **Chapter 5** automates everything; **Chapter 6** takes apart financial expertise, fund ratings and fees; **Chapter 7** explains asset allocation, target date funds, index funds and the Swensen model. **Chapter 8** is short "extra credit" on feeding, rebalancing and checking the system each year, and **Chapter 9**, the longest, covers student loans, parents, partners, weddings, prenups, salary negotiation, cars, houses and giving back. Each of the six program chapters ends with timed action steps, and the book is full of word-for-word scripts and reader stories.

## Worth knowing

<Aside type="caution" title="Honest limits">
The system - automate, spend consciously, invest cheaply and early - is sound and widely shared by independent advisers. But the book is written for Americans in 2019: its interest rates, contribution limits, fund tickers, card products and tools are dated, and Sethi himself regrets printing figures that went stale. Its assumed 8 percent average return after inflation is an optimistic long-run average, not a promise; the book also quotes 13 percent for one decade and an 11.5 percent nominal long-run figure. A few numbers don't agree with themselves: the Sally-and-Dan gap is given as $80,000 in the text but $60,000 in the table; the cost of a 1 percent fee appears as 28, "around 30" and 39 percent depending on the period; and the 30-year house-cost table doesn't add up to its printed total. The credit-card rewards strategy relies on US card economics that don't exist in Europe and work differently in India. The 20 percent allocation to real estate funds in the Swensen model is unusually high for a private investor, and putting everything in stocks in your twenties assumes a stable income and an emergency fund. Expect a fair amount of promotion for Sethi's website and paid courses. Read past those, and the core - Big Wins, conscious spending, automation and low-cost investing - travels well to any country.
</Aside>
