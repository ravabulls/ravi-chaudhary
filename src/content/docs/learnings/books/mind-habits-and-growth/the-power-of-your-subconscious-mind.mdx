## The big idea

Murphy's central claim is that the mind works on two levels. The **conscious** mind reasons, weighs, and selects; the **subconscious** mind accepts uncritically whatever the conscious mind hands it *repeatedly and with emotion* - and then quietly works, day and night, to make it real in health, wealth, and relationships. On this view the subconscious cannot tell a vividly imagined scene from a lived one, so **belief and mental imagery become the true levers of a life**. Change what you habitually believe and picture, the book teaches, and outer circumstances follow like a print follows its negative.

It is worth saying plainly at the outset: this is an **inspirational classic, not science**. Murphy writes in the New Thought tradition and repeatedly calls his method a "law" and a "science of mind," but the healing stories he offers are anecdotes, not controlled evidence, and the mechanism he describes is not how modern psychology or medicine understands belief and the body. Read it, then, as a **technique of mindset and autosuggestion** and a period document of a very influential idea - not as a proven account of how the world works.

Held at that angle, the book still earns its place. Its practical core - watch your dominant thoughts, refuse to rehearse fear, and deliberately feed your mind calm, confident, present-tense pictures - overlaps with ideas people find genuinely useful. The notes below present its claims faithfully as **the book's own teaching**, without endorsing them as fact.

## The heart of it

<Steps>

1. **Two levels of mind.** The conscious mind is the reasoning gatekeeper - it observes, judges, and decides what to accept. The subconscious is the vast storehouse and worker beneath it. The whole method rests on this split: you use the conscious mind to *choose*, and the subconscious to *carry out*.

2. **The subconscious accepts, it does not weigh.** It has no sense of humour and no argument - whatever the conscious mind impresses on it as true, it treats as a command, whether that belief is helpful or ruinous. All the real censorship, Murphy says, happens up in the conscious mind before the idea is handed down.

3. **Feeling is what impresses.** A thought held with **faith and emotion** is what actually sinks in; flat, mechanical repetition without feeling tends not to take. The subconscious responds to the *conviction* behind the words, not the words alone.

4. **The techniques follow from this.** If belief plus feeling is the lever, then the practical tools are simply ways to feed the subconscious a chosen belief cleanly: **affirmation**, **visualisation / mental imagery**, **autosuggestion**, and above all using the **relaxed, drowsy pre-sleep state**, when resistance is lowest and a picture slips in most easily.

</Steps>

<Aside type="note">
These are presented as the book's own teaching - a matter of faith and technique, not of demonstrated evidence. Murphy calls it a "science," but the support is anecdotal. Treat the model below as a mindset method to try, not a proven mechanism.
</Aside>

## How the book unfolds

The book opens by laying out the **two-mind model** and its core claim, then devotes most of its length to *applying* that single idea across the domains people care about. Early chapters establish how the subconscious works and how belief impresses it; a pivotal chapter recasts **prayer as the scientific use of the mind**. From there Murphy moves domain by domain - **healing and health**, **wealth and success at work**, **marriage and relationships**, **sleep**, **fear**, **forgiveness**, and **ageing** - each built on the same template: a principle, a batch of **healing and success anecdotes**, and a short technique or affirmation to try. It is less a linear argument than the **same key turned in many locks**, which makes it easy to dip into and repetitive by design.

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Name one belief to change.** Pick a single fear or "I can't," and write its positive opposite as a short, present-tense statement - specific, believable, and worded as already true.

2. **Affirm with feeling.** Repeat it calmly and often, *feeling* it as accomplished. The emotion, Murphy stresses, matters far more than the exact wording or the number of repetitions.

3. **Visualise the end.** Picture the resolved scene in sensory detail - as if it has already happened - rather than the striving to reach it. Dwell on the finished result, not the gap.

4. **Use the drowsy pre-sleep state.** As you fall asleep, quietly rehearse the affirmation or image and let the relaxed, receptive mind absorb it without conscious argument.

5. **Reframe, don't wrestle.** When worry surfaces in the day, deliberately *replace* the thought with its wanted opposite instead of arguing with the fear - Murphy calls resistance a trap that only feeds it.

6. **Refuse to rehearse the negative.** Stop replaying feared outcomes and mentally rehearsing failure; treat those images as commands you would not knowingly give.

7. **Persist without strain.** Return to the practice steadily and expect it to work, dropping the anxious "is it working yet?" checking, which itself affirms doubt.

</Steps>

## Worth knowing

<Badge text="Context" variant="note" />

The book belongs to the **New Thought** movement - a late-1800s American current (Phineas Quimby, Ernest Holmes, and others) holding that mind shapes matter and that right belief brings health and abundance. Its claims are **not supported by peer-reviewed science**: the healing stories are uncontrolled anecdotes, and the "subconscious as literal command-taker" is a metaphor, not a mechanism from clinical psychology. Read critically, some of its practices rhyme loosely with things that are studied - the relaxing effect of calm bedtime routines, the way expectation can colour experience, the value of not endlessly rehearsing anxiety - but the book overstates all of it into a universal law. Its lasting influence is real: it sold in the millions and helped seed the modern self-help and "law of attraction" genre. Take the **mindset and autosuggestion** as tools worth trying; take the "science" framing with caution, and never as a substitute for medical care.
