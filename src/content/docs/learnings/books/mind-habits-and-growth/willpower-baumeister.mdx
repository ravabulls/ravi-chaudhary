## Three things to remember

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="1 · One tank, and it runs low" icon="approve-check-circle">
    You draw on the **same reservoir** for every act of self-control - focus, patience, biting your tongue, hard choices. Spend it on one thing and measurably less is left for the next. Baumeister called this **ego depletion**.
  </Card>
  <Card title="2 · Willpower runs on glucose" icon="information">
    Self-control is metabolically expensive. When blood sugar drops, willpower drops with it - which is why your worst decisions arrive when you are tired, hungry, or drained.
  </Card>
  <Card title="3 · The best use it least" icon="star">
    People with the strongest self-control do not win temptation battles - they never enter them. Habits, bright-line rules, and a temptation-free environment mean the crucial moment is never a fight.

## The big idea

Willpower is a single, finite resource - closer to a muscle than to a moral trait. Every act of self-control draws down one account, and that account fatigues with use and runs on glucose. But the book's deepest turn is the reversal in its second half: because willpower is finite, the answer is **not to rely on it more**. Strip discipline of its moral halo and it stops being a character verdict and becomes a budget to be managed, protected, and - over months - trained.

## The key ideas

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Ego depletion" icon="approve-check-circle">
    Every act of self-control draws from one limited energy source that fatigues like a muscle. Resist one temptation and you are genuinely weaker against the next - not lazy, just lower on fuel.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The glucose connection" icon="information">
    Self-control is physically expensive. When blood sugar falls, so does the capacity to resist, decide, and persist. Fatigue and hunger reliably precede lapses - the tank is literally low.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Decision fatigue" icon="star">
    Making choices drains the same reservoir as resisting temptation. A depleted mind defaults to the easy option - deferring, or accepting the status quo. The famous parole-board study: prisoners were far likelier to be freed early in a session or just after a meal than late in a long docket.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Close the loop with a plan" icon="puzzle">
    Unfinished tasks nag and consume attention until the loop is closed. The fix is not to finish everything but to make a **concrete plan** - a specific plan quiets the nagging as well as completion does.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Bright lines beat fuzzy rules" icon="magnifier">
    Clear, unambiguous rules are far easier to keep than vague intentions. "One is fine" reopens the debate every time; "none on a work night" is decided once. A bright line converts a repeated battle into a single past decision.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Offense, not defense" icon="open-book">
    Structure the environment so the resource is rarely taxed. Precommit, build habits, remove temptation in advance. Willpower is for emergencies, not for daily operation - and clear, non-competing goals keep you from spending it all at once.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Schedule hard decisions for peak fuel.** Front-load consequential, irreversible calls into the morning or straight after a meal - never at the tired, hungry end of a draining day.

2. **Convert repeated battles into bright-line rules.** Wherever you keep re-fighting the same temptation, replace the fuzzy intention with one unambiguous rule you decide once.

3. **Close open loops with concrete plans.** When unfinished tasks nag, don't power through the anxiety - write down what, when, and in what order. The plan quiets the loop and frees attention.

4. **Engineer temptation out of reach.** Remove the option in advance rather than resisting in the moment. Make the good choice the path of least resistance so no battle ever starts.

5. **Protect the base and train the muscle.** Guard sleep and steady blood sugar, and strengthen self-control with small, consistent standards - start with one keystone habit rather than overhauling everything at once.

</Steps>

## Lines worth keeping

> *"The best decision makers are the ones who know when not to trust themselves."*

> *"People with strong willpower structure their lives so as to conserve it. They don't schedule endless self-control marathons."*

> *"It's not so much the good properties of a plan that help you. It's the fact that you've made a plan at all."*
