**Book:** The Art of Thinking Clearly
**Author:** Rolf Dobelli
**In one line:** We cannot say for sure what makes us successful, but we know what destroys success - so learn to recognise the 99 predictable errors in your thinking, remove the big ones from important decisions, and let your intuition handle the rest.

---

## Three things to remember

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="1 · Errors are systematic, so they are fixable" icon="star">
    A thinking error is not a random slip but a repeat pattern that pulls everyone in the same direction - we overestimate our knowledge far more often than we underestimate it, and fear a loss more than we value an equal gain. Because the errors are predictable, you can learn to spot them in yourself and in others, the way Franklin's lightning rod made thunderstorms less frightening without making them rarer.
  </Card>
  <Card title="2 · Subtract, do not add" icon="approve-check">
    Michelangelo said he made David by removing everything that is not David. Dobelli's method is the same: *negative knowledge (what not to do) is much more potent than positive knowledge (what to do)*. You will not find seven steps to an error-free life here, only a list of traps to stop walking into.
  </Card>
  <Card title="3 · Think hard only where it matters" icon="setting">
    Dobelli does not try to live error-free. For decisions with large consequences he takes out his list and checks it like a pilot. For small decisions, and inside his circle of competence, he lets intuition decide. Thinking is tiring - spend it where a mistake would cost you.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## The big idea

Dobelli is a novelist and entrepreneur, not a psychologist, and he says so: he started this list for himself, to stop making foolish gambles with the money his books had earned, after a dinner with Nassim Taleb in 2004 got him reading the research on heuristics and biases. He calls himself a translator of other people's science. The book is the list - 99 short chapters, each naming one systematic error, showing it in an everyday story, and ending with a way to defend against it. It is explicitly not a how-to book; he describes it as *insurance against too much self-induced unhappiness*.

Why do sensible people keep making the same mistakes? The epilogue gives three explanations. First, our brains were built for small hunter-gatherer groups - we are "hunter-gatherers in Hugo Boss" living in a world far more complex than the one we evolved for, so instincts like running when the group runs now mislead us. Second, the brain evolved to persuade, not to find the truth: *we think like lawyers*, building the best case for a conclusion we already reached. Third, mental shortcuts are often genuinely useful, so they persist even where they fail. Knowing the names of the errors does not make you immune, but it gives you categories to catch them before they do lasting damage.

## The heart of it

Dobelli's own rules for using the list, from the epilogue, are the most practical part of the book.

<Steps>

1. **Sort the decision by its consequences.** <Badge text="filter" variant="tip" />
   Large and hard to reverse - a job, a partner, an investment, a big purchase - gets slow, rational thinking. Small - which drink, which route - gets your gut, because a wrong call costs little.

2. **For the big ones, run the checklist like a pilot.** <Badge text="checklist" variant="tip" />
   Go through the errors one by one. The ten blocks below are ordered so that the most damaging and most common errors come first; for most decisions the first block is enough.

3. **Know your circle of competence.** <Badge text="competence" variant="tip" />
   Inside it - what you have practised for years - intuition is fast and good. Outside it, intuition is confident but unreliable. *It's smaller than you think.*

4. **Remove errors rather than chasing brilliance.** <Badge text="via negativa" variant="tip" />
   Warren Buffett, quoted in the book: *"Charlie and I have not learned how to solve difficult business problems. What we have learned is to avoid them."* Eliminate the downside and the upside largely takes care of itself.

5. **Expect to fail sometimes.** <Badge text="humility" variant="tip" />
   Studies show that people who know about hindsight bias still fall for it. The aim is fewer and smaller mistakes, not none.

</Steps>

<Aside type="note">
**How the 99 chapters are arranged here.** The book has no structure beyond chapter numbers. For these notes every chapter has been placed in one of ten blocks, and the blocks are in order of practical priority: block 1 holds the errors that do the most damage in everyday decisions and are worth learning first; later blocks are more specialised. Each card gives the chapter number, the gist with the book's own example, and **Do this** - the defence in practical form.
</Aside>

## The key ideas

### 1 · Start here: the errors behind most bad decisions

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Confirmation bias · ch. 7" icon="warning">
    We read new information so that it fits what we already believe, and file contradicting evidence as "exceptions". Gil's diet "works" because every loss counts and every gain is a fluctuation. Dobelli calls it the mother of all misconceptions. **Do this:** when you hear the word "exception", listen harder. Like Darwin, write down contradicting observations immediately, before your memory quietly deletes them.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Murder your darlings · ch. 8" icon="warning">
    The vaguer a belief, the stronger confirmation bias: optimists and cynics both find daily proof, and online feeds show you only views you already hold. **Do this:** write down your key beliefs - about your career, health, money, the people around you - and actively look for evidence against them. Dropping a belief that feels like an old friend is hard work, but essential.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Overconfidence effect · ch. 15" icon="warning">
    Asked for ranges they were 98 percent sure of, people were wrong 40 percent of the time. 93 percent of US students rate themselves above-average drivers; experts are often worse than laypeople, only more certain. There is no opposite "underconfidence" effect. **Do this:** assume you know less than you feel you do, distrust confident predictions, and plan with the pessimistic scenario.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Sunk cost fallacy · ch. 5" icon="warning">
    Sitting through a bad film because the ticket is paid, keeping a failing ad campaign because "we've invested so much", staying in a bad relationship, holding a falling share because of its purchase price. The Concorde is the classic case. **Do this:** for every "we've come this far", ask only one question - from today, do the future costs and benefits justify going on? What is spent is spent.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Survivorship bias · ch. 1" icon="warning">
    We see the rock stars, successful founders and best-selling authors, never the graveyard of those who tried and failed - so we overestimate our chances. Failed firms never enter a stock index, and failures do not write success books. **Do this:** before you copy a winner, look for the people who did the same things and failed. Visit the graves of once-promising projects, investments and careers.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Base-rate neglect · ch. 28" icon="warning">
    A thin, bespectacled German who likes Mozart is far more likely to be a truck driver than a literature professor, because there are vastly more truck drivers. Only about 20 percent of firms survive five years. **Do this:** before judging the details, ask how common each outcome is in general. Doctors learn *when you hear hoofbeats, don't expect a zebra*; Dobelli tells elite MBA students that most will end up in middle management, not on a board.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Incentive super-response tendency · ch. 18" icon="warning">
    People do what they are paid to do, not what you intended. Hanoi paid for dead rats and people bred rats; managers paid on targets work to lower the targets. *"Never ask a barber if you need a haircut."* **Do this:** when behaviour puzzles you, ask what incentive is behind it - Dobelli says it explains about 90 percent of cases. Agree fixed prices instead of hourly rates, and treat an adviser's tip in light of their commission.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Outcome bias · ch. 20" icon="warning">
    We judge decisions by results. A million monkeys trading at random leave one "genius" after twenty weeks, and the media will find his secret. Three surgeons with 0, 1 and 2 deaths in five operations may be equally good. **Do this:** judge a decision by the information and reasoning available at the time. If your reasons were sound, keep the method even after one bad result.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Loss aversion · ch. 32" icon="warning">
    A loss hurts about twice as much as an equal gain pleases. It keeps investors in losing shares and employees away from risks whose downside is a dismissal. **Do this:** notice when fear of a loss, not the size of the opportunity, is driving you. Use it when persuading others: a message about what people lose by not acting works better than one about what they gain.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Social proof · ch. 4" icon="warning">
    We feel right when we do what others do - looking up because others look up, laughing with canned laughter. In Asch's line experiment a third of people gave an obviously wrong answer to match the group. It is useful mainly in unfamiliar situations, like picking the restaurant full of locals. **Do this:** treat "the most popular" as no evidence at all. *"If fifty million people say something foolish, it is still foolish."*
  </Card>
  <Card title="Availability bias · ch. 11" icon="warning">
    We judge how likely something is by how easily examples come to mind: a grandfather who smoked and lived to 100, plane crashes over diabetes. We even use a wrong map rather than none. **Do this:** ask whether you are judging by vivid examples or by actual frequency. Spend time with people who think differently and work in other fields; they bring the examples you do not have.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Hindsight bias · ch. 14" icon="warning">
    After the fact everything looks inevitable - the 2008 crash, Google's dominance, the First World War. It makes us think we are better forecasters than we are, and so we take more risk. Even people who know about it fall for it. **Do this:** keep a journal of your predictions - career, weight, markets, politics - and compare them with what happens. Read diaries and old newspapers, not just tidy history books.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

### 2 · Deciding well

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Alternative blindness · ch. 71" icon="random">
    We compare an offer only with doing nothing: MBA or no MBA, the bond or the savings account, an arena or an empty plot. Warren Buffett: *"Each deal we measure against the second-best deal that is available at any given time."*. **Do this:** before saying yes, name at least one other real option - a cheaper programme, another hospital, a different use of the money - and compare against that.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Paradox of choice · ch. 21" icon="random">
    Too much choice paralyses, worsens decisions and leaves us less satisfied. A stall with 24 jams sold a tenth of what a stall with 6 sold. **Do this:** write down your criteria before you look at the offers, and stick to them. Aim for good, not perfect - *"good enough" is the new optimum.*
  </Card>
  <Card title="Inability to close doors · ch. 68" icon="random">
    We keep every option open even when it costs us: two dozen half-read books, three partners and no relationship. In Ariely's game, players chasing shrinking doors scored 15 percent less. General Xiang Yu burned his ships so his army could only fight. **Do this:** write a not-to-pursue list for your life, as a strategy says what a company will not do, and test new options against it.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Decision fatigue · ch. 53" icon="random">
    Willpower runs down like a battery. Israeli judges granted about 65 percent of parole requests after breaks, falling almost to zero before the next one. Tired deciders choose the easy option and buy on impulse. **Do this:** make important decisions, and schedule important presentations, early in the day or right after a break and a meal. Do not decide big things when depleted.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Action bias · ch. 43" icon="random">
    Goalkeepers dive left or right although a third of penalties go down the middle - standing still looks worse. Young police officers who rush in cause more casualties than senior ones who wait. **Do this:** in a new or unclear situation, hold back until you can judge your options. Munger: avoid *"just doing any damn thing just because you can't stand inactivity."*
  </Card>
  <Card title="Omission bias · ch. 44" icon="random">
    Harm caused by not acting feels less bad than harm caused by acting - not vaccinating, not launching new products, not declaring income. It shows up where the right action is clear but we let things happen. **Do this:** when doing nothing is a choice, count its consequences as if you had acted. *"If you're not part of the solution, you're part of the problem."*
  </Card>
  <Card title="Default effect · ch. 81" icon="random">
    We stick with the house wine, factory settings and the pre-selected option. Making organ donation the default raised take-up from about 40 to more than 80 percent. Where there is no default, the status quo becomes one. **Do this:** review your defaults - subscriptions, contracts, fees, routines - and ask if you would choose each one today. To change others' behaviour, change the default.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Fear of regret · ch. 82" icon="random">
    We regret acting against the crowd more than staying with it, so we play safe and keep things we no longer need. "Last chance" offers - the last plot with a sea view - make us panic. **Do this:** when you hear "last chance", ask whether you wanted it before the deadline appeared. Most "unique" opportunities come round again.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Information bias · ch. 59" icon="random">
    More information does not mean a better decision. Dobelli spent two hours researching hotels and chose the one he liked first; German students guessed better than Americans which US city was bigger because they knew less. **Do this:** decide what you need to know, get it, and stop. *"The greatest obstacle to discovery is not ignorance - it is the illusion of knowledge."*
  </Card>
  <Card title="Affect heuristic · ch. 66" icon="random">
    A quick like or dislike decides how we rate both risks and benefits - if we like something we see low risk and high benefit. Even morning sunshine moves stock markets. We replace "What do I think?" with "How do I feel?" **Do this:** for important choices, list risks and benefits separately and on paper, so the first feeling does not set both.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Overthinking · ch. 90" icon="random">
    The centipede who analysed his legs and starved; golfer Jean van de Velde collapsing on the last hole; students whose jam rankings went wrong once they had to justify them. **Do this:** trust your gut for practised skills, your circle of competence and age-old questions such as whom to trust. Think slowly and soberly for complex, unfamiliar ones such as investments.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Hyperbolic discounting · ch. 51" icon="random">
    Most people wait 13 months instead of 12 for an extra $100, but take $1,000 today over $1,100 in a month - "now" distorts everything. The marshmallow test linked patience with later success. **Do this:** notice when "now" is doing the choosing, and stay away from expensive instant credit. "Live each day as if it were your last" - but only once a week.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Endowment effect · ch. 23" icon="random">
    What we own seems worth more: mug owners wanted $5.25, buyers offered $2.25. Near-ownership counts too - the bidder at the end of an auction, the candidate rejected in the final round. **Do this:** value what you have as if you were buying it today. Don't cling to things; treat possessions as on loan.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

### 3 · Getting things done

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Planning fallacy · ch. 91" icon="seti:clock">
    We plan too much every day despite years of evidence. Only 30 percent of students met their realistic thesis date; on average they needed 50 percent more time and finished a week after their worst case. The Sydney Opera House cost 14 times its budget. More detailed plans make it worse. **Do this:** check how long similar projects actually took, and run a premortem: imagine it is a year later and the plan has failed, and write for five minutes about why.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Procrastination · ch. 85" icon="seti:clock">
    We delay unpleasant but important tasks because the reward comes later, and self-control is limited. **Do this:** remove distractions (Dobelli turns off the internet to write), refuel with food and rest, and set deadlines - external ones work best; your own only work broken into steps, each with a date. His neighbour finished her thesis in three months: a room without phone or internet, three public deadlines printed on her business cards.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Zeigarnik effect · ch. 93" icon="seti:clock">
    Unfinished tasks keep nagging at us - but only until we have a clear plan for them. Students who made a detailed study plan stopped worrying about the exam. **Do this:** break messy projects into concrete steps and write them down (David Allen uses 20 to 50 per project). Keep a notepad by the bed and write tasks and next steps when you cannot sleep.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Strategic misrepresentation · ch. 89" icon="seti:clock">
    The more is at stake, the more people exaggerate - "consider it done" in interviews, six-month book deadlines, mega-project budgets. The bid with the most hot air wins. **Do this:** judge people by their past performance, not their promises. For projects, compare with similar ones, have someone tear the plan apart, and write penalties for overruns into the contract.
  </Card>
  <Card title="It'll-get-worse-before-it-gets-better · ch. 12" icon="seti:clock">
    A doctor, consultant or politician who predicts "It'll get worse before it gets better" is right either way. Dobelli's appendicitis got worse for days on such advice. **Do this:** when you hear it, ask for clear, verifiable milestones and check them soon. Real dips exist - a career change, a reorganisation - but their progress can be measured.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Effort justification · ch. 60" icon="seti:clock">
    The more effort we put into something, the more we overvalue it: a parachute pin pounded into the chest, a restored Harley never sold, an exhausting MBA, IKEA furniture. **Do this:** after a big investment of time, judge the result as if a stranger had produced it - only the result.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Not-invented-here syndrome · ch. 74" icon="seti:clock">
    We love our own ideas. Dobelli's wife served his own fish sauce as a top chef's and he hated it. Companies prefer in-house software and ignore subsidiaries' solutions; roundabouts took 30 years to spread. **Do this:** separate generating ideas from judging them - one group creates, another rates. Look back at your ideas of the past ten years: how many were truly outstanding?
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

### 4 · Seeing yourself honestly

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Self-serving bias · ch. 45" icon="magnifier">
    Success is ours, failure is the market's, the exam's or the economy's fault. Five flatmates estimated their share of taking out the rubbish at 320 percent in total. **Do this:** ask a friend who tells you the truth - or even someone who dislikes you - for an honest view of your strengths and weaknesses.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Introspection illusion · ch. 67" icon="magnifier">
    We trust our own soul-searching more than other people's claims. In one experiment people happily explained why they preferred a face they had not chosen. When others disagree, we assume they are ignorant, stupid or malicious. **Do this:** treat your inner certainties with the same scepticism as a stranger's claims. Be your own toughest critic.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Illusion of control · ch. 17" icon="magnifier">
    We think we influence what we cannot: choosing lottery numbers, throwing dice hard, placebo buttons at crossings and on office thermostats. Some control does help endurance. **Do this:** focus on the few important things you really can influence, and let the rest go - *Que sera, sera.*
  </Card>
  <Card title="Falsification of history · ch. 78" icon="magnifier">
    Our memory rewrites old opinions to match today's, so we believe we were always right. Even vivid "flashbulb" memories are reconstructed: three years after the Challenger disaster, a quarter of people's accounts did not match a single detail of what they wrote at the time. **Do this:** assume much of what you remember is wrong; keep written records of what you thought, and be glad when you catch a mistake.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Cognitive dissonance · ch. 50" icon="magnifier">
    Like Aesop's fox calling the grapes sour, we reinterpret failures to protect our self-image - the loud car becomes a safety feature, the rejected job a test of "market value". **Do this:** when you catch yourself explaining away a bad decision, name it honestly. As Dobelli's friend told him: *you'll never get the grapes that way.*
  </Card>
  <Card title="Chauffeur knowledge · ch. 16" icon="magnifier">
    Planck's chauffeur could recite the lecture but not answer a question. Many speakers, anchors and executives have learned the show, not the substance. Munger: *"It's not terribly important how big the circle is. But it is terribly important that you know where the perimeter is."* **Do this:** test experts by whether they say "I don't know" outside their field - and know the edge of your own circle of competence.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Domain dependence · ch. 76" icon="magnifier">
    Knowledge does not transfer between fields. Markowitz won a Nobel for portfolio theory and split his own savings 50/50; doctors smoke; a great small-group presenter fails in front of a hundred. **Do this:** do not assume success in one field makes you - or anyone - good in another, and practise applying what you learn in the setting where you will need it.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Déformation professionnelle · ch. 92" icon="magnifier">
    *If your only tool is a hammer, all your problems will be nails.* Surgeons reach for the scalpel, engineers for structure, and everyone for Excel. Munger's answer is multiple models from multiple disciplines. **Do this:** add two or three mental models from fields far from your own. Dobelli says it takes about a year to absorb the key ideas of a new field.
  </Card>
  <Card title="False-consensus effect · ch. 77" icon="magnifier">
    We assume most people think like us: students who agreed to wear an "Eat at Joe's" sign thought 62 percent would agree; those who refused thought 67 percent would refuse. Inventors overrate features customers do not care about. **Do this:** assume your view is not the majority, and before you write off people who disagree, question your own assumptions.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Illusion of attention · ch. 88" icon="magnifier">
    Half of viewers miss a gorilla walking through a basketball video. Drivers following satnav drove into a flooded ford for two weeks. Phoning while driving slows reactions like alcohol, hands-free or not. **Do this:** ask regularly what nobody is talking about - the "gorilla in the room". Watch the periphery and silences, not just the noise.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Expectations · ch. 62" icon="magnifier">
    Expectations change reality: randomly labelled "bloomers" gained more IQ points because teachers expected them to; placebos work for about a third of patients; Google lost billions for missing forecasts. **Do this:** raise expectations for yourself and the people you love; lower them for things you cannot control, like markets. Anticipating nasty surprises is the best protection against them.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

### 5 · When others try to persuade you

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Anchor · ch. 30" icon="comment">
    Any number we see first pulls our estimates towards it - the last two digits of a social security number changed what students bid for wine, and random listing prices swayed professional estate agents. Sellers set prices early for this reason. **Do this:** in a negotiation, name your number first or deliberately ignore theirs; set your own reference point from research before you hear any figure.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Contrast effect · ch. 10" icon="comment">
    We judge relative to what is next to us: $3,000 of leather seats feels small beside a $60,000 car; people walk ten minutes to save $10 on food but not on a $1,000 suit. Gradual changes such as inflation go unnoticed. **Do this:** judge each price or option on its own; ten dollars is ten dollars. And do not go out with your supermodel friends.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Framing · ch. 42" icon="comment">
    Meat labelled "99 percent fat free" seems healthier than "1 percent fat"; a plan that "saves two hundred lives" wins, the same plan described as "kills four hundred people" loses. A falling share price becomes a "correction", a fired employee is "reassessing his career". **Do this:** restate the message in the opposite frame before deciding, and remember every fact you receive - even from friends, even in this book - is framed.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Scarcity error · ch. 27" icon="comment">
    "Only while stocks last", invitation-only accounts, an estate agent's "A doctor from London saw the plot of land yesterday". When an option is taken away we suddenly want it more. **Do this:** judge products and offers only on price and benefits; how fast they are disappearing or who else is interested is irrelevant.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Reciprocity · ch. 6" icon="comment">
    We hate being in someone's debt: a flower from a stranger leads to a donation, a free ticket to a game leads to an order, one boring dinner leads to a cycle of them. **Do this:** decline free samples and gifts from people who want something from you, or decide in advance that a gift creates no obligation.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Liking bias · ch. 22" icon="comment">
    We buy from people who are attractive, similar to us and seem to like us; the world's top car salesman sent every customer a card saying "I like you". A shared love of sailing closed an eight-figure deal. **Do this:** judge the product independently of the seller - imagine you do not like them.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Authority bias · ch. 9" icon="comment">
    In Milgram's experiment more than half went to the maximum shock because a professor said so. Experts missed 2008; airlines made flying safer by training co-pilots to challenge captains. **Do this:** notice which authority is influencing a decision, and challenge experts politely - and invite challenge if you are the authority.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Halo effect · ch. 38" icon="comment">
    One striking feature colours everything: Cisco's culture was praised when the share price soared and damned when it fell, though nothing else changed. Good-looking people are judged more honest and intelligent. **Do this:** factor out the most striking feature. Orchestras audition behind a screen; in interviews, judge the answers, not the looks or the brand on the CV.
  </Card>
  <Card title='"Because" justification · ch. 52' icon="comment">
    "May I go before you because I have to make some copies?" worked almost as well as "because I'm in a rush" at the copier. Any reason calms people; no reason infuriates them. **Do this:** when you ask for something, give your reason. When others give you a reason, check whether it actually explains anything.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Winner's curse · ch. 35" icon="comment">
    In auctions the winner usually overpays: the highest of many estimates is usually too high, and rivalry pushes it higher. Buffett's advice on auctions: *"Don't go."* **Do this:** if you must bid - for a job, a flat, a contract - set your maximum beforehand, take 20 percent off, write it down and do not go a cent above it.
  </Card>
  <Card title="House-money effect · ch. 84" icon="comment">
    We spend found, won or gifted money more carelessly than earned money, which is why sign-up credits and free miles work. **Do this:** treat windfalls exactly like your salary - put them in the bank or into something useful, not straight into the casino of your choice.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Forer effect · ch. 64" icon="comment">
    A vague personality description stitched from horoscopes got an average score of 4.3 out of 5 for accuracy. It works because statements are general, flattering and only positive. Consultants' reports can work the same way. **Do this:** test anyone who claims to read people with Dobelli's blind test - descriptions for twenty anonymous people, and see if most recognise their own.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

### 6 · Happiness, envy and gut feelings

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Hedonic treadmill · ch. 46" icon="heart">
    Lottery wins, promotions and dream houses lift happiness for only a few months; breakups hurt for about three. A banker's lakeside villa made him unhappier because of the hour-long commute. **Do this:** avoid negatives you never get used to (commuting, noise, chronic stress), expect only short-lived joy from things, and invest in free time, autonomy, passions and friendships.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Envy · ch. 86" icon="heart">
    We envy those closest to us in age, job and neighbourhood - *potters envy potters*. Moving into a richer area swaps your peer group and brings status anxiety. Balzac: envy is the most stupid of vices. **Do this:** stop comparing, find a niche where you are the best, however small. Dobelli's wife's rule: *it's okay to be envious - but only of the person you aspire to become.*
  </Card>
  <Card title="Social comparison bias · ch. 72" icon="heart">
    We hold back people who might overtake us. Guy Kawasaki: *"A-players hire people even better than themselves."* B-players, he says, hire C-players so they can feel superior. Isaac Barrow gave up his professorship to Newton. **Do this:** hire, recommend and support people better than you; in the long run you gain from them and learn from them.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Volunteer's folly · ch. 65" icon="heart">
    A photographer earning $500 an hour who spends a morning building birdhouses helps less than if he worked one extra hour and paid a carpenter. **Do this:** volunteer where your specific skill is needed; otherwise donating money usually helps more. The exception is fame - a celebrity's presence brings publicity.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Contagion bias · ch. 54" icon="heart">
    We cannot shake the feeling that objects carry the essence of their owners: nobody wants Hitler's freshly washed sweater, and people throw darts badly at photos of loved ones. **Do this:** notice when a "connection" to a person or event is driving your judgement of an object, a place or a deal - and decide on the facts.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Association bias · ch. 48" icon="heart">
    Our brain links unrelated things - lucky underpants, an expensive ring bought because the seller was stunning, avoiding doctors on hot days. Leaders shoot the messenger and end up hearing only good news. **Do this:** take from an experience only the lesson in it - Twain's cat avoids cold stoves too. As a leader, ask explicitly for bad news, and fast.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

### 7 · Luck, risk and uncertainty

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Alternative paths · ch. 39" icon="random">
    Ten million won at Russian roulette and ten million earned in twenty years as a lawyer look the same in the bank. They are not - one path had a one-in-six chance of death. **Do this:** look at the outcomes that could have happened, not just the one that did. Success from risky bets is worth less than success from steady work.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Black Swan · ch. 75" icon="random">
    Rare, unforeseeable events - positive or negative - now matter more than ever: a 22 percent crash in one day, a world-changing invention. **Do this:** put yourself where a positive Black Swan can find you (scalable work - writing, building products) and stay away from the negative ones: *stay out of debt, invest your savings as conservatively as possible, and get used to a modest standard of living.*
  </Card>
  <Card title="Induction · ch. 31" icon="random">
    The goose is fed every day and becomes sure the farmer loves it - until Christmas. A base-jumper friend had a thousand safe jumps and died two months after citing them. **Do this:** remember that every certainty is provisional. A long run of good outcomes is not proof that the risk is gone.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Neglect of probability · ch. 26" icon="random">
    We react to the size of a prize or danger, not its likelihood - giant jackpots, cancelled flights after a crash. We overpay for "zero risk": cutting a risk from 1 to 0 percent feels better than from 5 to 2. **Do this:** for any risk or opportunity, write down both the size and the probability, and compare options on both.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Ambiguity aversion · ch. 80" icon="random">
    We prefer known odds over unknown ones (the Ellsberg boxes). Risk means the probabilities are known - casinos, mortality tables; uncertainty means they are not - currencies, markets. **Do this:** be suspicious of anyone who gives a precise percentage for an uncertain future, and learn to tolerate ambiguity rather than forcing false precision onto it.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Gambler's fallacy · ch. 29" icon="random">
    After twenty-plus blacks in Monte Carlo in 1913, gamblers lost fortunes betting on red. A roulette ball has no memory, and there is no balancing force for independent events. **Do this:** ask whether events are independent (lotteries, coins) or connected (health, markets, weather) before assuming things must even out.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Coincidence · ch. 24" icon="random">
    A choir arrived late and their church exploded; a friend calls just as you think of him. Improbable events happen because there are so many chances for them. **Do this:** draw the four boxes - it happened or not, the sign or not - and count how often the boring combinations occur. *Improbable coincidences are precisely that: rare but very possible events.*
  </Card>
  <Card title="Clustering illusion · ch. 3" icon="random">
    The brain finds patterns everywhere - faces in toast, "safe" districts in random V-1 bombings, a friend's gold-price formula that cost him his savings. **Do this:** treat any pattern you discover as chance until it is tested. If it looks too good to be true, have someone who knows statistics check it.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Beginner's luck · ch. 49" icon="random">
    Early wins make us raise the stakes - the gambler, the company that buys one firm too many, the house flipper in a boom. Dobelli's first novel was accepted by the first publisher he tried; ten others then rejected it. **Do this:** try to disprove your early success. Talent shows over a long time and against few competitors; among millions, a winning streak is probably luck.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Illusion of skill · ch. 94" icon="random">
    Fewer than 1 percent of founders succeed again, and in Kahneman's data fund managers' yearly rankings had zero correlation. Buffett: *"A good managerial record ... is far more a function of what business boat you get into than it is of how effectively you row."* **Do this:** judge how much a field rewards skill (pilots, plumbers) or luck (markets), and pick your boat carefully.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Regression to the mean · ch. 19" icon="random">
    Extremes drift back to normal by themselves: the back pain after the chiropractor, the golf round after a lesson, the worst schools after a programme. Teachers conclude that punishment works and praise does not. **Do this:** when something improves after an intervention that followed a bad patch, ask whether it would have improved anyway.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Swimmer's body illusion · ch. 2" icon="random">
    Swimmers do not get their bodies from swimming; they are good swimmers because of their bodies. Harvard may simply select bright students; MBA graduates were different before the MBA. **Do this:** ask whether a quality is the result of something or the reason people were selected for it. If you study further, Dobelli says, do it for reasons other than a bigger salary.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

### 8 · Stories, news and experts

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Story bias · ch. 13" icon="open-book">
    We turn messy reality into neat stories that feel like understanding - the driver's biography after a bridge collapse, not the design flaw. Stories make us take bigger risks. **Do this:** for every story ask who is telling it, what they want, and what they left out. Read old newspapers to see how unconnected events looked at the time.
  </Card>
  <Card title="News illusion · ch. 99" icon="open-book">
    *News is to the mind what sugar is to the body.* It favours the shocking over the relevant, almost none of it improves a decision, and it costs hours. Dobelli quit the news for three years and reports clearer thoughts, better decisions and more time. **Do this:** cut news consumption sharply - he says completely - and read long background articles and books instead.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Forecast illusion · ch. 40" icon="open-book">
    Tetlock checked 28,361 expert predictions: barely better than chance, and the most famous doom-prophets did worst. Forecasters pay no price for being wrong. **Do this:** for any prediction ask two questions - what is this person's incentive, and what is their track record? Long-range forecasts of complex systems are close to worthless.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Personification · ch. 87" icon="open-book">
    A photo of one starving girl raised twice the donations of statistics about three million. People move us; numbers do not. **Do this:** when a human story moves you, ask for the numbers and the distribution behind it. When you want to move others, give the story a name and a face.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Salience effect · ch. 83" icon="open-book">
    The eye-catching detail gets the blame or the credit - drugs found in a crashed car, a new CEO's gender, a red book cover. Prejudices grow from salient exceptions. **Do this:** fight the obvious explanation; look for the quiet, slow factors such as long-term profit growth.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Sleeper effect · ch. 70" icon="open-book">
    We forget the source faster than the message, so propaganda and attack ads gain power over weeks. **Do this:** refuse unsolicited advice, avoid ad-filled sources, and for every argument remember where it came from - *Cui bono?* Who benefits?
  </Card>
  <Card title="Twaddle tendency · ch. 57" icon="open-book">
    Floods of words hide unclear thinking, from a beauty pageant answer to academic prose. The worse a company is doing, the more its CEO talks. *Simplicity is the zenith of a long, arduous journey, not the starting point.* **Do this:** if you cannot say it simply, think more first; and if you have nothing to say, say nothing.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Cherry picking · ch. 96" icon="open-book">
    Hotels, annual reports and department presentations show only the best. Anecdotes - "My father-in-law picked it up yesterday" - are cherries too. **Do this:** ask for the "leftover cherries": failed projects and missed goals. Check whether old targets have quietly been replaced by easier ones - drawing the bull's-eye around the arrow.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Feature-positive effect · ch. 95" icon="open-book">
    We notice what is present, not what is absent - checklists catch listed risks and miss Enron; labels show twenty vitamins and hide the cholesterol. **Do this:** ask what is missing from a report, offer or checklist - and now and then notice the absence of pain, war or a crash.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Neomania · ch. 69" icon="open-book">
    We overrate the new: the future was meant to have jet packs, yet we still sit on chairs and eat with forks. **Do this:** assume whatever has survived X years will probably last another X years, and treat shiny new things with caution until time has filtered them.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Fallacy of the single cause · ch. 97" icon="open-book">
    Wars, crises and product flops never have one cause - Tolstoy's apple falls for many reasons. Hunting for one cause is hunting for a scapegoat. **Do this:** sketch all the possible causes and the causes behind them, highlight the ones you can change, and test them.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Fundamental attribution error · ch. 36" icon="open-book">
    We credit or blame people and overlook the situation: CEOs fired in bad industries, wars blamed on one man, readers asking if a novel is autobiographical. **Do this:** before judging a person, look at the circumstances that shaped what they did - "the dance of influences".
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

### 9 · Teams and organisations

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Groupthink · ch. 25" icon="seti:config">
    Smart, close-knit groups make reckless decisions because nobody wants to break the harmony - the Bay of Pigs, Swissair's expansion. **Do this:** speak up in unanimous meetings and question unspoken assumptions. If you lead, appoint a devil's advocate.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Social loafing · ch. 33" icon="seti:config">
    Each person pulls less when effort is invisible: on a rope, eight men gave about half their individual strength. Groups also spread responsibility and take bigger risks. **Do this:** keep teams small and diverse, and make individual contributions visible.
  </Card>
  <Card title="In-group out-group bias · ch. 79" icon="seti:config">
    Even groups formed by a coin toss favour their own members, see outsiders as all alike and reinforce their own views - the root of organisational blindness. **Do this:** notice when loyalty to your team, company or country is colouring your view of the facts.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Motivation crowding · ch. 56" icon="seti:config">
    Money can destroy motivation: a Swiss village's support for a nuclear site halved when compensation was offered; day-care fees for late pick-up increased lateness. **Do this:** do not pay for what people do out of goodwill or pride. With children, give fixed pocket money rather than payment per task.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Primacy and recency effects · ch. 73" icon="seti:config">
    First impressions dominate quick judgements (the same traits, in a different order, make "Alan" likeable and "Ben" not); last impressions dominate older memories. **Do this:** in interviews ask everyone the same questions and score at intervals - Dobelli scores every five minutes and averages. In meetings, speak first to influence; as chair, ask for views in random order.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

### 10 · Numbers that mislead

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Exponential growth · ch. 34" icon="bars">
    We understand linear growth, not percentages: a sheet of paper folded 50 times would reach the sun. **Do this:** use the rule of 70 - divide 70 by the growth rate to get the doubling time. Five percent inflation halves your money's value in about 14 years. And remember that nothing grows exponentially forever.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The problem with averages · ch. 55" icon="bars">
    Add Bill Gates to a bus and the average wealth becomes meaningless. *Don't cross a river if it is (on average) four feet deep.* Book sales, city sizes and actors' incomes have no meaningful average. **Do this:** whenever you hear "average", ask about the distribution - are a few extremes dominating it?
  </Card>
  <Card title="Law of small numbers · ch. 61" icon="bars">
    Small samples swing wildly: the stores with the highest and the lowest theft rates were both small rural ones. **Do this:** be wary of striking statistics about small units - schools, start-ups, towns. The size of the sample may explain the result.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Will Rogers phenomenon · ch. 58" icon="bars">
    Moving a middling member from a strong group to a weak one raises both averages without improving anything - salesmen between branches, tumours between stages. **Do this:** when averages rise in several groups at once, check whether people or cases were simply moved between them.
  </Card>
  <Card title="False causality · ch. 37" icon="bars">
    More firefighters do not cause bigger fires; books at home do not raise grades. Storks and babies declined together in Germany. *Correlation is not causality.* **Do this:** ask whether the cause could be the effect, whether a third factor drives both, or whether the link is pure chance.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Intention-to-treat error · ch. 98" icon="bars">
    Fast drivers look safer when anyone who crashed lands in the "slow" group; indebted firms look more profitable because the failures drop out. **Do this:** check whether people or cases vanished from a study or switched groups. If they did, discard the conclusion.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Self-selection bias · ch. 47" icon="bars">
    You are in the slow traffic lane more often than you think because you spend most time there; a phone survey finds no households without phones. **Do this:** before complaining about bad luck or trusting a survey, ask whether the sample selected itself.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Conjunction fallacy · ch. 41" icon="bars">
    "Chris works for a major bank, where he runs its Third World foundation" feels likelier than "Chris works for a major bank" - but adding a condition can only make it less likely. Dobelli paid extra for redundant terrorism insurance. **Do this:** be wary of plausible details and happy endings; every extra condition lowers the probability.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Simple logic · ch. 63" icon="bars">
    Bat and ball cost $1.10, the bat $1 more: the ball is 5 cents, not 10. Five machines make five shirts in five minutes, so a hundred make a hundred in five. Most people trust the first answer. **Do this:** greet even simple questions with a moment of doubt and check the quick answer before you use it.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## How the book unfolds

There is no plot and almost no structure. A short **introduction** explains how the list began and what a cognitive error is: a systematic, repeatable deviation from rational thought. Then come **99 chapters** of two or three pages each, in no particular order, each built the same way - a story or experiment, the name of the error, more examples from business, investing, medicine and private life, and a closing piece of advice. Many chapters cross-refer to each other because, as Dobelli notes, the errors are related. The **epilogue** steps back: it explains the via negativa, the hot and cold theories of irrationality, the three reasons our brains err, and Dobelli's personal rule for when to use the list and when to trust intuition. A notes section points to the research behind each chapter.

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Learn block 1 first.** Twelve errors - confirmation bias, overconfidence, sunk costs, survivorship, base rates, incentives, outcome bias, loss aversion, social proof, availability and hindsight - cover most everyday mistakes. Read one card a day for two weeks.

2. **Build your own checklist from it.** Turn each card in block 1 into one question you can ask before a decision: *What would prove me wrong? What have I already spent that I should ignore? How common is this outcome in general? Who benefits if I say yes? Would I judge this the same if it had turned out badly?*

3. **Use the checklist only for big decisions.** A job offer, a move, a large purchase, an investment, a hire. For small daily choices, go with your gut, as Dobelli does.

4. **Start a prediction journal.** Write down what you expect about your career, applications, money and the world, with a date. Review it every few months. It is the best cure for hindsight and overconfidence.

5. **Before any negotiation or purchase, read block 5.** Anchors, contrast, framing, scarcity, reciprocity and liking are the tools sellers, recruiters and landlords use. Set your number first and write down your walk-away point.

6. **Run a premortem on every important plan.** Imagine it has failed a year from now and write for five minutes about why. Then look at how long similar projects really took.

7. **Write a not-to-pursue list.** Projects, offers and opportunities you have decided in advance to decline. Check new temptations against it instead of deciding afresh.

8. **Cut the news and read books.** Try a month without news apps and notice whether you miss anything that would have changed a decision.

</Steps>

## Worth knowing

<Aside type="caution" title="Honest limits">
Dobelli writes as a translator of other people's research, not as a researcher, and the book's strength is also its weakness: each error gets two pages and one or two famous studies, with no discussion of how strong the evidence is. Some of the studies he leans on have since been questioned or failed to replicate in later research, notably the willpower-as-a-battery work behind the decision-fatigue and procrastination chapters, and the marshmallow test's link to later success, so treat individual numbers as illustrations rather than settled facts. Several chapters overlap heavily - confirmation bias, story bias and cherry picking are close cousins - and a few read more like opinion than science, such as the advice to quit the news entirely or the remarks linking test scores with religious belief and anxiety about uncertainty with political views. The book was also criticised after publication for borrowing heavily from Nassim Taleb's work, which Dobelli credits in several chapters. None of this removes the value of the list: the errors themselves are well established, and naming them is still the fastest way to start noticing them.
</Aside>

## Lines worth keeping

> Negative knowledge (what not to do) is much more potent than positive knowledge (what to do).

> If fifty million people say something foolish, it is still foolish.

> No matter how much you have already invested, only your assessment of the future costs and benefits counts.

> Never ask a barber if you need a haircut.

> "Good enough" is the new optimum.

> If you have nothing to say, say nothing.

> Simplicity is the zenith of a long, arduous journey, not the starting point.

> A business strategy is primarily a statement on what not to engage in.

> It's okay to be envious - but only of the person you aspire to become.
