**Author:** David Eagleman

## Three things to remember

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="1 · Not hardware, livewire" icon="random">
    The brain isn't a fixed machine — it's a system that constantly rewrites its own wiring. Plasticity isn't a bonus feature; it's the core operating principle, running **for life**, not just in childhood.
  </Card>
  <Card title="2 · It configures to its inputs" icon="setting">
    At birth the brain is largely **world-agnostic**. It absorbs whatever data streams arrive and shapes itself to them — which is why children soak up language and culture, and why brains adapt to very different bodies.
  </Card>
  <Card title="3 · It just wants information" icon="puzzle">
    The brain will learn to use **any** channel that delivers useful data — even a novel one. That means new senses are possible: feed it a signal, and it works out how to read it.
  </Card>

## The big idea

We picture the brain like a circuit board — parts soldered in place, each with a fixed job. Eagleman replaces that with **livewiring**: a dynamic system that continuously reshapes its own circuitry around experience, needs, and the body it finds itself in. Regions aren't defined by permanent assignments but by the **inputs** they receive; change the inputs and the territory gets repurposed. Everything downstream — learning, recovery from injury, aging, brain–machine interfaces — follows from taking that rewiring seriously.

## The key ideas

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Plasticity is the operating system" icon="seti:clock">
    Rewiring isn't a fringe repair mechanism — it's how the brain runs, all the time, throughout life. The circuitry you have is a snapshot of the experiences you've had.
  </Card>
  <Card title="World-agnostic at birth" icon="open-book">
    The newborn brain doesn't come pre-loaded for one environment. It configures itself to whatever reaches it — sights, sounds, language, a particular body — which is why adaptation is so fast and so total.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Cortex is contested territory" icon="list-format">
    Senses **compete** for cortical real estate. Lose one — say sight — and the "unused" visual cortex is rapidly annexed by touch and hearing. Brain regions are defined by their inputs, not fixed jobs.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The Potato Head model" icon="puzzle">
    Sensory substitution: feel sound through a vibrating vest, "see" through the tongue. The brain accepts input from novel devices and learns to interpret it — so we could, in principle, **add** new senses.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Relevance gates change" icon="star">
    Repetition alone doesn't rewire you — **mattering** does. Neuromodulators open the door to plasticity only for what's important. One theory: dreaming defends the visual cortex from takeover during the long dark of night.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Feed it what matters.** Since relevance gates rewiring, attach real stakes or emotion to what you're learning — don't just repeat it, make it count.

2. **Expect slow, then sudden.** Change accumulates below the surface. Keep supplying the right inputs even when nothing seems to shift yet.

3. **After injury, reroute.** Recovery works by handing lost functions to intact circuitry — practice the hard channel deliberately rather than avoiding it.

4. **Design for the brain's hunger for data.** Whether a tool, a lesson, or an interface, deliver a clean, consistent signal — the brain will learn to read almost any channel.

</Steps>

## Lines worth keeping

> *The brain doesn't come hardwired — it livewires itself to the world it wakes up in.*

> *A region of cortex isn't defined by the job it does, but by the input it gets.*

> *Lose a sense and the territory doesn't go dark — the neighbours move in.*

> *The brain doesn't care where the data comes from; it only cares that data comes.*

> *We rewire most around what we care about, not simply what we repeat.*
