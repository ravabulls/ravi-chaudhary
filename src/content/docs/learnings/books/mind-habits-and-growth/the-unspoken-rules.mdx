## The big idea

Every workplace runs on rules no one writes down - how to ask for help, when to speak up, what "done" actually means, how much initiative is welcome before it reads as overstepping. Insiders with connected, professional families absorb these expectations at the dinner table years before their first job. **First-generation and early-career professionals** are left to reverse-engineer them in real time, then quietly judged for guessing wrong. Ng's core claim is that this, more than raw talent, is what separates those who struggle from those who thrive early on.

His move is to make the invisible explicit. Careers are not a pure meritocracy where good work speaks for itself; good work that no one notices, trusts, or connects to the real priority does not speak at all. So the task is twofold: **learn the tacit rules deliberately**, as a subject you can study rather than a test of who you are, and **manage how your competence, commitment, and compatibility are perceived** by the people who decide what you get to do next.

The tone throughout is warm and practical rather than cynical. This is not a manual for gaming the system; it is a way of giving outsiders the same quiet head start insiders never had to think about.

## The heart of it

The engine of the book is the **Three Cs** - the three questions everyone above you is silently asking, all the time. Learn to see your own behaviour through them and most "office mysteries" resolve.

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Competent" icon="approve-check">
    Can you actually do good work - accurately, on time, without needing everything spelled out? Competence is the price of entry, but it is judged through *visible signals*: work that is clear, reliable, and easy to check. Sloppy formatting or a missed detail quietly taxes the trust you've earned.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Committed" icon="rocket">
    Do you care, take ownership, and treat the work as yours rather than just clocking hours? Commitment shows in follow-through, in flagging problems early, and in doing a bit more than the literal ask - it is what makes people hand you bigger things.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Compatible" icon="heart">
    Are you someone people trust and want to work with? Compatibility is about reliability and ease, not charm or fitting a mould - being pleasant to deal with, respecting norms, and making others' jobs easier rather than harder.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Manage the perception" icon="star">
    The Three Cs are not fixed traits; they are **impressions you shape** through what you deliver and how you show up. The person doing quietly excellent work no one sees is often outpaced by the one whose competence and commitment are *legible* to the room.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Read the room" icon="list-format">
    Norms on communication, initiative, and self-advocacy vary enormously by team. **Observe before you act** - watch how people ask questions, share credit, and push back - then adapt to the local style instead of assuming your instincts are universal.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

<Aside type="tip">
  The Three Cs tie together in one habit: before acting, ask "how will this land against Competent, Committed, and Compatible?" - then choose the move that strengthens the weakest one. That single question turns vague anxiety about "doing well" into something you can actually steer.
</Aside>

## The key ideas

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Tacit knowledge is learnable" icon="open-book">
    The "obvious" moves aren't obvious - they're taught, just invisibly, and mostly at home. Reframing unwritten norms as a subject you can study, not a verdict on your worth, is what makes the whole gap closable.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Find the real ask" icon="magnifier">
    The stated task hides the true priority. Understand what the boss and organisation actually want - the outcome, the constraint, the audience - then **deliver more than the literal request** instead of technically satisfying it.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Managing up" icon="information">
    Your manager is a person with pressures and preferences, not a task-dispenser. Learn how they like to communicate, keep them free of surprises, and make it easy to say yes to you - that is what earns you autonomy.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Initiative, calibrated" icon="rocket">
    Taking initiative is prized, but only when it's read correctly. Do more than asked *within* the norms - check before you go big, so proactivity reads as ownership rather than overstepping or going rogue.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Ask for help the right way" icon="puzzle">
    Bring your attempt and a specific question, not a blank "how do I do this." Showing what you tried signals ownership and respect for people's time - it makes helping you cheap, so people actually will.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Mentors and sponsors" icon="seti:notebook">
    A mentor advises you; a **sponsor** advocates for you when you're not in the room. Both are built slowly through genuine relationships and delivered results - not asked for cold - and sponsors are what quietly move careers.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Handle mistakes well" icon="error">
    Everyone slips; what's judged is the response. Own it early, own it plainly, propose a fix, and don't hide - a well-handled mistake can *build* trust, while a concealed one corrodes it permanently.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Politics, with integrity" icon="approve-check">
    Office politics is just how influence and priorities really flow. You can navigate it honestly - understanding stakeholders, sharing credit, building goodwill - without becoming manipulative or pretending it isn't there.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## How the book unfolds

Ng opens by naming the hidden game - why the playing field isn't level and how tacit knowledge, not just merit, shapes who gets ahead. He introduces the **Three Cs** as the lens for everything that follows, then works outward through the situations a new professional actually faces: getting hired and started, decoding what your boss wants, doing the work so it lands, building relationships and mentors and sponsors, recovering from mistakes, and steering office politics. It reads as a field guide - short, situation-based chapters you can revisit when a specific moment arrives, each returning to the same underlying question of how you're being perceived.

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Decode the real priority.** Before starting anything, ask what success looks like and why it matters. Surface the true ask hidden under the stated task, then aim at *that*.

2. **Audit your Three Cs.** For any project, ask: am I signalling competence, commitment, and compatibility? Find the weakest one and fix it on purpose.

3. **Make your good work visible.** Don't assume quality speaks for itself. Give brief, clear updates so the people who matter can actually see the competence and commitment you're putting in.

4. **Observe before you act in a new team.** Watch how people communicate, take initiative, and give credit for a beat - then mirror the norms you see instead of imposing your own.

5. **Ask for help the right way.** Bring your attempt and one specific question. It signals ownership, respects people's time, and makes them glad to help you again.

6. **Recover from mistakes cleanly.** When you slip, own it early, name it plainly, and bring a fix - don't hide and hope. Handled well, it deepens trust rather than breaking it.

7. **Build sponsors, not just contacts.** Cultivate a few genuine relationships with people willing to advocate for you, deliver so they can, and navigate politics with integrity.

</Steps>

## Worth knowing

Ng writes from both sides of the gap - a first-generation college student who had to learn these rules the hard way, now a Harvard career adviser who watches others hit the same walls. That gives the book its particular value <Badge text="for first-gen and early-career readers" variant="tip" />: it assumes nothing about insider knowledge and explains what more polished advice leaves unsaid. The framing is generous - the point is not that outsiders are deficient, but that no one handed them a map that others were quietly given for free.

*Don't just do the work; make sure the right people can see and trust the work you did.*
