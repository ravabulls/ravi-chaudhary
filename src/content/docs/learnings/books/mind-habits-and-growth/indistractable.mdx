## The big idea

Distraction is not caused by your devices; it is caused by your discomfort. The phone is just the easiest escape route at the moment a task turns difficult, dull, or anxiety-inducing. Silencing notifications treats the symptom — unless you address the internal triggers driving the urge, you will always find another exit. And you cannot call anything a distraction until you know what it is distracting you *from*, which is why the answer is a four-part model, not a single hack.

## The key ideas

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Master internal triggers" icon="information">
    Almost all distraction starts with an uncomfortable feeling — boredom, anxiety, self-doubt. The external trigger is only the door; the internal trigger is why you were already looking for one.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Make time for traction" icon="star">
    A to-do list without time is a wish. Timebox your values into protected calendar blocks so the important work is scheduled before reactive demands fill the day.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Hack back external triggers" icon="magnifier">
    Ask of every ping: does this serve me, or am I serving it? Make silence the default and force each notification to justify its interruption.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Prevent distraction with pacts" icon="open-book">
    Pre-commit before temptation arrives. Effort pacts add friction, price pacts add a cost, and identity pacts change how you describe yourself — closing the negotiation entirely.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The one identity that ends it" icon="approve-check">
    "I don't check my phone in meetings" beats "I'm trying not to." An identity pact settles the question before it arises, instead of reopening it every time.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Name the internal trigger before acting.** When the urge to check something unplanned hits, pause and label the feeling — bored, anxious, this section feels ambiguous. Naming it breaks its automatic pull.

2. **Timebox your values, not just your tasks.** Before the week starts, put your most important work into non-negotiable calendar blocks, early, before reactive demands claim the space.

3. **Run a notification audit.** Disable every non-human alert that isn't actionable in real time. Silence is the default; noise must earn its place.

4. **Surf the urge for ten minutes.** When you want to escape a task, set a ten-minute timer and stay with it. The discomfort almost always subsides before the time is up.

5. **Build one pact for your hardest session.** Add friction, a cost, or an identity rule — and set it *before* the temptation, never negotiate it during.

</Steps>
