## The big idea

Distraction is not caused by your devices - it is caused by your discomfort. When a task turns hard, dull, or anxiety-inducing, the mind looks for relief, and the phone is simply the easiest exit at reach. Eyal's central claim, drawn from psychology, is that human beings are wired for dissatisfaction: even in pleasant conditions we feel a pull toward *more*, and distraction is the unhealthy way we soothe that internal itch. So silencing notifications only treats a symptom. Unless you address the internal triggers driving the urge, you will always find another exit.

The reframe is liberating rather than guilt-inducing. Distraction is a problem of *pain management*, and time management is pain management too. The counterpart of distraction is traction, and both start from a trigger - internal (a feeling) or external (a ping, a person, a device). Crucially, you cannot label anything a distraction until you know what it pulled you away from, which means planning your intentions comes first.

That is why the answer is a four-part model, not a single hack. It moves from the inside out: master the feelings that push you, make time for what pulls you, strip out the external cues that do not serve you, and lock in precommitments so willpower is not the last line of defence.

## The heart of it

The framework has four parts, worked in order from the internal cause to the external safeguard.

<Steps>

1. **Master internal triggers.** Distraction starts inside, with an uncomfortable feeling you want to escape. You cannot delete the feeling, but you can change your relationship to it by reimagining three things: the *trigger* (write it down, get curious about it, then "surf the urge" - stay with the sensation using the ten-minute rule instead of acting on it), the *task* (turn tedious work into play by finding novelty and challenge in it), and your *temperament* (drop the myth that willpower is a limited fuel you run out of - believing that makes it truer). Practise self-compassion, not self-flagellation.

2. **Make time for traction.** You cannot call something a distraction unless you know what it distracted you from - so turn your values into time. *Timebox* your whole calendar: build a schedule that reserves blocks for yourself, for your relationships, and for your work, so that intentions become appointments. Judge a day not by what you produced but by whether you did what you planned. A schedule you sync with the important people in your life removes the guesswork about where attention belongs.

3. **Hack back external triggers.** Now interrogate each external cue - every notification, ping, meeting, email, group chat, and open-plan interruption - and ask a single question: does this serve me, or am I serving it? Remove the ones that do not earn their place. Make silence the default, batch and defer where you can, and redesign your environment so useful cues remain and needless ones disappear.

4. **Prevent distraction with pacts.** A pact is a precommitment made *before* temptation arrives, when your judgement is clear, so future-you cannot renegotiate. There are three kinds: *effort pacts* add friction (an app blocker, unplugging the router); *price pacts* put money on the line; and *identity pacts* change who you believe you are - once you see yourself as "indistractable," the choice is settled before it is even offered.

</Steps>

<Aside type="tip">
Two ideas hold the whole book together. First, **traction versus distraction**: both begin with a trigger, and the only difference is direction - toward your plan, or away from it. Second, **discomfort is the real driver**: the phone is not the disease, it is the medication you grabbed for an internal ache. Fix the ache and manage the trigger, and the device is just a tool again.
</Aside>

## How the book unfolds

The book is built as the four-part model, moving from cause to safeguard. **Part one - master internal triggers** locates the true source of distraction in our discomfort and how we escape it. **Part two - make time for traction** converts values into a timeboxed calendar so you can tell traction from distraction at all. **Part three - hack back external triggers** works through devices, notifications, desks, email, group chats, and meetings, removing cues that do not serve you. **Part four - prevent distraction with pacts** adds the final layer of precommitment. Closing sections extend the model to workplaces, raising indistractable children, and relationships.

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Name the internal trigger before acting.** When an unplanned urge hits, pause and label the feeling - bored, anxious, this is ambiguous, I am tired. Naming it interrupts the automatic pull.

2. **Surf the urge for ten minutes.** Allow the distraction, but in ten minutes, not now. Stay with the task and let the feeling crest. It usually subsides before the timer ends.

3. **Timebox next week before it starts.** Put self, relationships, and work into protected calendar blocks early, before reactive demands claim the space. Now you have a definition of a "distraction" to measure against.

4. **Run a notification and external-trigger audit.** Disable every non-human alert that is not actionable in real time; batch email; question recurring meetings. Silence is the default, noise must earn its place.

5. **Build one pact for your hardest session.** Add friction (a blocker), a price (money at stake), or an identity rule ("I am indistractable") - and set it *before* the temptation, never negotiate it during.

6. **Reflect weekly and refine.** Compare what you did to what you planned, without self-judgement, and adjust the schedule and triggers. Treat each slip as feedback, not a verdict.

</Steps>

## Worth knowing

Eyal wrote **Hooked**, the playbook tech companies use to build habit-forming products - so **Indistractable** is a poacher-turned-gamekeeper move. Having taught designers how to hook users, he now hands users the counter-strategy. It also gently pushes back on pure "digital detox" and blame-the-tech narratives: the tools exploit a vulnerability, but the vulnerability - our urge to escape discomfort - was already there. Take responsibility for the internal cause, and you are no longer at the mercy of the design.
