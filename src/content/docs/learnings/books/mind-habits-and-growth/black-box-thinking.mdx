**Author:** Matthew Syed
**In one line:** Progress comes from the willingness to *examine* failure openly — the real barrier isn't the mistakes, it's our reluctance to admit them.

## The big idea

Progress in any field depends on how it **responds to failure**. The failures aren't the problem — our psychological reluctance to admit and examine them is. Syed's central contrast: aviation records every incident in a black box, investigates openly, shares findings across the whole industry, and blames no one, so the system keeps getting safer. Much of medicine historically hid or explained away errors, so the same mistakes recur and thousands die. The difference isn't the people — it's the **culture around mistakes**.

## The key ideas

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Aviation vs healthcare" icon="rocket">
    Same human fallibility, opposite results. One culture captures and studies failure; the other buries it under blame and reputation. Culture, not competence, is the variable.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Build a black box culture" icon="setting">
    Design systems that **capture failure** and feed it back into improvement — the organisational habit, not just the recording device.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Cognitive dissonance" icon="warning">
    When facts threaten our self-image we distort the facts. Prosecutors resisting **DNA evidence** in wrongful convictions shows how expertise defends error rather than admitting it.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Marginal gains" icon="bars">
    Team Sky / British Cycling: break a big goal into tiny, testable improvements. Each is trivial alone; **compounded**, they produce dramatic success.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Redefine failure" icon="approve-check">
    Treat failure as **information and opportunity**. Run experiments and randomised trials; create a blame-free culture where people report errors instead of hiding them.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Growth mindset underpins it" icon="seti:crystal">
    Learning from failure only works if you believe ability can grow. Growth mindset and grit are the psychological base of the whole approach.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Separate blame from analysis.** When something goes wrong, ask *what* failed before *who*. Blame kills the reporting you need to learn.

2. **Log the near-misses.** Capture small errors and close calls, not just disasters — that's the raw data an open loop runs on.

3. **Break big goals into marginal gains.** List the tiny components, improve and test each one, and let the increments compound.

4. **Run it as an experiment.** Frame decisions as testable bets; pre-commit to what would prove you wrong, so evidence can actually change your mind.

5. **Ask what dissonance you're protecting.** When you dismiss inconvenient feedback, check whether you're defending the truth or your self-image.

</Steps>

## Lines worth keeping

> *A closed loop is where mistakes lead to nothing; an open loop is where they lead to change.*

> *The instinct to protect our self-image is the enemy of learning.*

> *Marginal gains: small improvements, compounded, become a dramatic advantage.*
