**Author:** Dan Ariely
**In one line:** We are far less rational than economics assumes — but our mistakes are *systematic*, so they can be studied, anticipated, and designed around.

## Three things to remember

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="1 · We judge by comparison" icon="random">
    Nothing has an absolute value. We only know what something is worth *relative* to nearby options — which is exactly why a cleverly worse "decoy" can steer our choice.

  <Card title="2 · Irrationality is predictable" icon="approve-check">

## The big idea

Standard economics assumes we weigh options coolly and choose what's best for us. Ariely's experiments show the opposite: we are pulled around by relativity, free offers, expectations, and emotion — often without noticing. The hopeful twist is that these forces are **systematic**. Because we're predictably irrational, we can spot the traps in advance and build choices, defaults, and commitments that work *with* human nature instead of against it.

## The key ideas

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="The power of relativity" icon="random">
    We compare what's easy to compare. Adding a slightly worse "decoy" option (the famous *Economist* subscription setup) makes a nearby option look like a bargain and shifts what we pick.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Anchoring & arbitrary coherence" icon="seti:clock">
    An initial number — even an arbitrary one — anchors what we're willing to pay. Once set, that anchor sticks and quietly shapes later decisions.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The cost of zero" icon="star">
    FREE! is an emotional trigger, not just a price. The lure of zero makes us overvalue free things and choose against our own interest.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Social vs market norms" icon="heart">
    We live in two worlds. Offering to *pay* for a favour — or a mother-in-law's dinner — insults the social bond, and the relationship rarely recovers.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The placebo of price" icon="information">
    Expectations shape experience. A higher-priced pill "works" better because we believe it will — perceived value bleeds into real effect.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Arousal, delay & honesty" icon="pencil">
    We underestimate how differently we act when emotionally aroused, we overvalue what we own (endowment), we struggle to delay gratification, and we're only as honest as our excuses allow.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Watch for the decoy.** When a choice suddenly feels obvious, ask what nearby option is making it look good — and whether it was placed there on purpose.

2. **Question the anchor.** Before accepting a price or first number, ask: is this figure meaningful, or just the first one I saw?

3. **Distrust FREE.** When something is free, name the real trade-off (time, worse option, upsell) before letting the zero decide.

4. **Keep money out of relationships.** Treat favours as social, not transactional — a gift or thanks, never a fee.

5. **Pre-commit against yourself.** Set imposed deadlines and remove temptation in advance; future-you will not be as disciplined as you hope.

</Steps>

## Lines worth keeping

> *We don't measure things in themselves — we measure them against whatever sits next to them.*

> *An arbitrary anchor, once accepted, can shape a lifetime of "reasonable" prices.*

> *"Free" is not a price; it's an emotion.*

> *A favour paid for is a favour spoiled — money and warmth don't mix.*

> *We're honest right up to the edge of what we can rationalise.*
