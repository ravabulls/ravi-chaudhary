*Selbststudium Deutsch - von den ersten Wörtern bis B1. Ein Tag, eine Seite, zwei Stunden.*

---

One day, one page, two hours. A hundred and twenty of them runs from the first words of German to **B1** - the level of the integration course, the usual language requirement for naturalisation, and the point at which an employer treats German as a working language rather than a project.

This page is the method. The course itself is in four modules, and the map is further down.

Every page is a full session: warm up, sounds, new material, drills, scheduled review, listening, translation, speaking, and a block to copy into a paper notebook. The work is typed or spoken, never clicked from a list, because recognising an answer is not the same as producing one.

<Aside type="caution" title="Nothing here is saved. That is deliberate.">
No account, no progress bar, no cookies, no local storage. Scores live in the browser's memory and a refresh wipes them. Two reasons: I do not want a dashboard to perform for, and the spacing does not need saved state anyway. **The review schedule lives in the content** - Tag 20 is built to re-drill items from Tag 19, 18, 15, 8 and, later, Tag 1. That is Pimsleur's graduated interval recall, encoded as pages instead of as a database.
</Aside>

## The nine blocks of a session

<Steps>

1. **Aufwärmen, 5 min.** Ten items from yesterday, typed cold.

2. **Aussprache und Hören, 15 min.** Days 1 to 10 cover the sounds - vowels, umlauts, diphthongs, the consonants that trip up English speakers - and after day 10 the slot becomes shadowing. Every page has **its own forty-second clip** of real German: ten minutes shadowing it half a second behind, then five minutes listening to it cold and writing down what you caught. There is no transcript on any page, deliberately.

3. **Neue Bausteine, 20 min.** Twelve new chunks, each with exactly one unknown element, recalled three times at widening gaps inside the page.

4. **Drills, 25 min.** Four drill types borrowed from the old Foreign Service method: substitution, conversion, response, translation. Roughly sixty prompts.

5. **Wiederholung, 20 min.** The scheduled review - yesterday, two days back, five days back, and later twelve and thirty.

6. **Text und Hören, 12 min.** A short dialogue built only from chunks I already know, read while listening, then heard again without the text.

7. **Bidirektional, 8 min.** Eight sentences translated one way today and back the other way two days later, then compared against the model. The gap between my version and the model is the actual lesson.

8. **Sprechen, 10 min.** A ready-made roleplay prompt to paste into a voice AI, tuned to the day's grammar.

9. **Auf Papier, 10 min.** Dictation by hand, five sentences about my real day, and the errors the drills caught. Every third day, a Goldlist page.

</Steps>

## Why these blocks

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Production, not recognition" icon="pencil">
    No multiple choice anywhere. Multiple choice lets you coast on recognising the right answer, which is a much weaker memory than pulling it out of nothing. Everything here is typed, written by hand, or said aloud.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Chunks, never bare words" icon="puzzle">
    A word learned alone has to be assembled into a sentence under time pressure later. A chunk arrives with its grammar attached. Every item here is a usable fragment of speech.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Spacing built into the calendar" icon="seti:clock">
    Graduated interval recall works on a fixed, known curve, so it can be baked into which page re-drills what. No app required, and no data about me stored anywhere.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Paper does a different job" icon="document">
    Handwriting appears to recruit motor, visual and spatial systems that typing bypasses, which suits encoding new forms - spelling, umlauts, noun gender. Typing is better for volume. Both are used, for different jobs.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## The rules I am holding myself to

<Steps>

1. **Never miss twice.** One day off is noise. Two is how streaks die.

2. **Produce from memory on paper.** Copying German off the screen into the notebook feels productive and teaches nearly nothing. Written blind, then checked.

3. **Say every answer out loud**, even the typed ones. Silent German does not become spoken German.

4. **Speak badly on purpose.** The ten minutes with a voice AI are for making errors at speed, not for composing perfect sentences.

5. **Sunday is review only.** Every sixth day adds no new material.

</Steps>

<Aside type="tip" title="The three schedules running underneath">
Three things are timed across the pages rather than left to choose, and all three are visible in the sidebar order:

- **The review schedule.** Every day re-drills yesterday, two days back, five days back, then - from Tag 14 - twelve days back, and from Tag 31 a fifth slot thirty days back. Tag 20 pulls from Tag 19, 18, 15 and 8; Tag 50 pulls from Tag 49, 48, 45, 38 and 20.
- **The bidirectional chain.** A set of eight sentences is translated into German on one day and back into English **two days later**. Tag 08 comes back on Tag 10, Tag 11 on Tag 13, and so on.
- **The Goldlist.** A new handwritten page of twenty-five items every third day, distilled exactly **fourteen days later**, then again fourteen days after that. From Tag 29 it closes into a cycle where the day number alone tells you what to do: divisible by three means write a new page, and the other two days are a first and a second distillation.
</Aside>

## Sources and honesty

The situations and the drill types come from United States government courses that are in the **public domain**: the Foreign Service Institute's German Basic Course, Programmed Introduction and German FAST, and the Defense Language Institute's German Headstart. All the audio comes from the same place, for the same reason.

Each day's clip is a forty-second byte range of whole audio frames copied straight out of the original tape - nothing re-encoded, nothing re-recorded, so what you hear is the recording as it was made. [Every clip is credited to its source file and offset](/german/audio/CREDITS.txt). Two honest consequences: the German is studio German from the middle of the twentieth century, read slowly and clearly, which is excellent for shadowing and nothing like a radio presenter; and because the clips are cut mechanically rather than hand-picked, a few of them catch the original course's English narration. That is a property of the source, not a mistake - and if a clip is mostly English, any other day's clip will do.

**Every German sentence on these pages is written for this site**, not copied - they are about Hamburg, an MBA, and the work I actually do. Word ordering leans on the Leipzig Wortschatz frequency data, which is CC-BY licensed.

Three kinds of copyrighted material sit on my desk while I write and appear nowhere on the site. **Grammars** - Hammer's German Grammar, EasyDeutsch - are used privately to check that my explanations are correct. **Course books** - the Netzwerk neu series - are used to check that my coverage has no holes; their texts and exercises are not reproduced. **Exam material** is the delicate one: the Goethe and telc exam *formats* are published facts and I describe them, but no practice item from any Prüfungstraining book is reproduced, and the Goethe Wortlisten are used only as a checklist against my own coverage, never as content.

<Aside type="note">
**Sixty of a hundred and twenty days are built, and A1 and A2 are both complete** - 588 chunks, twenty handwritten Goldlist pages, and the grammar of both levels finished. B1's syllabus is published and its pages are not yet built.

One honest line about what this cannot do on its own. Every day has its own forty-second clip of real German, shadowed and then listened to cold with no transcript - but fifty-six clips is about forty minutes of audio, and forty minutes is close listening rather than exposure. **Listening stays the weak skill**, and what fixes it is volume - modern German audio every day - and people who do not wait for you. Tag 56 says so at length.
</Aside>
