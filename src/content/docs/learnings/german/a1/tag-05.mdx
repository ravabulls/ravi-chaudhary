Until today you have been using verbs as whole chunks: `ich heiße`, `ich wohne`, `Haben Sie Zeit?`. Today the chunk comes apart. Five regular verbs, six endings, nine pronouns, and suddenly you can build sentences that were never on a list.

The second half of the day is one rule, and it is the rule that makes German sound German: **the conjugated verb is the second element of the sentence**. Not the second word - the second *element*. Put anything you like in front of it, and the subject moves behind it. English does not do this, so it has to be drilled rather than understood.

The sounds block is about stress. German puts the weight on the stem of the word, and in a simple word that means the first syllable. Get that wrong and you can be fully correct and still hard to follow.

## 2. Aussprache - die Wortbetonung

Every German word has one syllable that carries the weight. The rule for almost everything you will meet this month:

**German stresses the stem, and in a simple word the stem is the first syllable.**

| Wort | Betont | Warum |
|---|---|---|
| wohnen | WOH-nen | simple word, first syllable |
| kommen | KOM-men | simple word, first syllable |
| lernen | LER-nen | simple word, first syllable |
| arbeiten | AR-bei-ten | still the first syllable |
| Bahnhof | BAHN-hof | compound: the first part wins |
| verstehen | ver-STE-hen | ver- is an unstressed prefix, so the stem takes it |
| Entschuldigung | ent-SCHUL-di-gung | ent- is unstressed, the stem is schuld |
| Student | stu-DENT | borrowed word, stress at the end |
| studieren | stu-DIE-ren | verbs in -ieren stress the ie |

Two groups break the first-syllable habit, and both break it for the same reason: the first syllable is not the stem. Unstressed prefixes (`be-`, `ge-`, `ver-`, `ent-`, `er-`) never take the stress, and borrowed words keep something close to their original pattern.

**The -en ending gets swallowed.** In writing, `wohnen` has a clear e in the second syllable. In speech, that e almost disappears and the ending collapses onto the n: *WOHN-n*, *KOMM-m*, *LERN-n*. This is why `wir haben` so often sounds like a single squeezed *hahm* and why spoken German sounds faster than it is. Nothing is missing - the weight simply all sits at the front and the ending is thrown away.

Say each of these three times, hitting the first syllable hard and letting the ending go: `wohnen`, `kommen`, `lernen`, `machen`, `arbeiten`, `haben`, `sprechen`.

<Aside type="caution" title="Stress is not volume">
Stressing a syllable in German means it is longer and higher, not louder. Beginners who shout the first syllable sound angry rather than clear. Lengthen it instead, and let everything after it shorten.
</Aside>

| Was machen Sie? | what do you do | job, or right now |
| Ich mache einen MBA | I am doing an MBA | der MBA, so einen |
| Er arbeitet nicht | he does not work | nicht goes after the verb |
| Sie sprechen schnell | you speak fast | say this to anyone, often |

### Die Grammatik dahinter

**Die Endungen.** Take the infinitive, cut off the `-en`, and you have the stem. Then hang six endings on it. This is the whole regular present tense:

| Person | Endung | wohnen | kommen | lernen | machen | arbeiten |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ich | -e | wohne | komme | lerne | mache | arbeite |
| du | -st | wohnst | kommst | lernst | machst | arbeitest |
| er / sie / es | -t | wohnt | kommt | lernt | macht | arbeitet |
| wir | -en | wohnen | kommen | lernen | machen | arbeiten |
| ihr | -t | wohnt | kommt | lernt | macht | arbeitet |
| sie / Sie | -en | wohnen | kommen | lernen | machen | arbeiten |

Three things fall out of that table. The `wir`, `sie` and `Sie` forms are identical to the infinitive, so half the work is already done. The `er` and `ihr` forms are identical to each other, and context tells them apart. And German has **one** present tense: `ich lerne` covers both *I learn* and *I am learning*, so stop looking for the second one.

**arbeiten takes an extra e.** A stem ending in `t` or `d` cannot have `-st` or `-t` glued straight on - `arbeitst` is unpronounceable. So an `e` is inserted: `du arbeitest`, `er arbeitet`, `ihr arbeitet`. The same happens with `finden` and `warten` later. It is a pronunciation fix, not a new rule to memorise.

**Die Pronomen.** Nine of them, but only seven shapes:

| Deutsch | English | Anmerkung |
|---|---|---|
| ich | I | small i, always |
| du | you | one person you know |
| er | he | |
| sie | she | small s |
| es | it | |
| wir | we | |
| ihr | you | more than one person you know |
| sie | they | small s again |
| Sie | you (formal) | capital S, always, singular or plural |

`sie` is doing three jobs: *she*, *they* and, with a capital, *you*. The verb ending sorts it out - `sie wohnt` can only be *she lives*, `sie wohnen` can only be *they live* or *you live*.

**Das Verb steht an zweiter Stelle.** The rule that shapes every German statement. The conjugated verb sits in slot two, and slot one can hold anything you want to foreground. If you put something other than the subject in slot one, the subject does not disappear - it moves behind the verb.

<Steps>

1. Start from the plain order, subject first:

   `Ich lerne heute Deutsch.` - subject in slot 1, verb in slot 2.

2. Decide to foreground the time instead. Move `heute` to the front - and move nothing else:

   `Heute lerne ich Deutsch.` - `heute` is slot 1, `lerne` is still slot 2, and `ich` has slid in behind it.

3. The wrong version, which is the one your English brain will produce for the next month:

   *Heute ich lerne Deutsch.* - this has the verb in slot 3. It is always wrong.

</Steps>

Laid out as slots, with the place phrase counting as one single element:

| 1 | 2 | Rest |
|---|---|---|
| Ich | arbeite | heute in Hamburg |
| Heute | arbeite | ich in Hamburg |
| In Hamburg | arbeite | ich heute |

Note that `in Hamburg` is two words but one element, which is why the verb still counts as second. The test is not words, it is blocks of meaning.

## 8. Sprechen - zehn Minuten

Paste this into a voice AI and actually speak. Ten minutes, out loud, standing up.

```text
Du bist eine freundliche Person an einem Bahnhof in Hamburg. Ich bin
Anfänger (Tag 2, A1). Sprich sehr langsam und nur mit diesen Wörtern:
Guten Tag, Hallo, Tschüss, Auf Wiedersehen, Entschuldigung, Wie geht es
Ihnen, Danke gut, Und Ihnen, Ich bin müde, Ich bin Student, Sind Sie aus
Deutschland, Wo ist der Bahnhof, Was ist das, Das ist ein Café, Sprechen
Sie Englisch, Ich verstehe nicht, Ich heiße, Ich wohne in, Ich bin aus,
Ja genau, Nein danke.
Stelle mir drei Fragen: wie es mir geht, woher ich komme, und ob ich
Student bin. Benutze nur die Formen von sein: bin, bist, ist, sind, seid.
Maximal sechs Wörter pro Satz. Wenn ich einen Fehler mache, wiederhole den
Satz einfach richtig, ohne Erklärung. Fang jetzt an und begrüße mich.
```

<Aside type="tip" title="Use Ich verstehe nicht on purpose">
Today you have the sentence that keeps a conversation alive: `Ich verstehe nicht`. Use it at least three times in the ten minutes, and do **not** follow it with English. Say it, wait, and let them say the sentence again more slowly. Learning to stay inside German while lost is a separate skill from vocabulary, and this is the day it starts.
</Aside>
