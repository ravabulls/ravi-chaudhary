Until now you have mostly been answering. Today you learn to ask, which is the single change that turns a German lesson into a German conversation - a person who can only answer runs out of things to say in ninety seconds, and a person who can ask keeps the other one talking while they catch up.

Two pieces of grammar do it. The first is almost unfairly simple: to turn a statement into a yes-no question, **move the verb to the front**. That is the whole rule. The second is negation with `nicht`, which is the other half of the `kein` you met on Tag 04 - and the only real question is where in the sentence it goes.

<Aside type="note" title="How to run this page">
Top to bottom, every answer **out loud** before you type it, nothing saved. Today the out-loud part matters more than usual, because section 1 is about melody: a German yes-no question typed correctly but said with a falling tone is still going to be heard as a statement. If you only type today's answers, you will learn half of the lesson.
</Aside>

## 2. Aussprache - die Satzmelodie

Six days on individual sounds. Today the unit gets bigger: the shape of a whole sentence.

German intonation is flatter than English and it does most of its work at the very end of the sentence. There are only two patterns you need this week:

| Satztyp | Melodie am Ende | Beispiel |
|---|---|---|
| Aussage | **fällt** | Ich wohne in Hamburg. |
| Ja/Nein-Frage | **steigt** | Wohnen Sie in Hamburg? |
| W-Frage | **fällt** | Wo wohnen Sie? |

That third row surprises people. A question with a question word does **not** rise - it falls, exactly like a statement. The `wo` has already done the job of marking it as a question, so the voice does not need to. Only the yes-no question, which has no question word to give it away, has to signal itself with the rising tone.

Say each of these pairs aloud. Same words, different ending, different meaning:

| Fällt am Ende | Steigt am Ende |
|---|---|
| Sie wohnen in Hamburg. | Wohnen Sie in Hamburg? |
| Sie kommen aus Deutschland. | Kommen Sie aus Deutschland? |
| Sie arbeiten hier. | Arbeiten Sie hier? |
| Er spricht Deutsch. | Spricht er Deutsch? |

<Aside type="caution" title="The habit English gives you">
English lets you make a question out of a statement with tone alone - *You live in Hamburg?* - and English speakers carry that straight into German. German will not accept it in the same way: the verb really does have to move. Get the word order right first, then put the rising tone on top of it. Tone without word order sounds like a foreigner guessing; word order without tone sounds flat but is understood.
</Aside>

## 3. Neue Bausteine - zwölf Stück

### Ja/Nein-Fragen: das Verb zuerst

On Tag 02 you learned the W-question. Its shape is fixed: question word first, **verb second**, subject third.

| Position 1 | Position 2 | Rest |
|---|---|---|
| Wo | wohnen | Sie? |
| Woher | kommen | Sie? |
| Was | machen | Sie beruflich? |
| Wie | heißen | Sie? |

A yes-no question is the same sentence with the question word deleted and the verb slid into the empty first slot:

| Position 1 | Position 2 | Rest |
|---|---|---|
| Wohnen | Sie | hier? |
| Kommen | Sie | aus Deutschland? |
| Arbeiten | Sie | in Hamburg? |
| Haben | Sie | Zeit? |

Compare the three shapes side by side, because this is the thing to burn in today:

| Form | Beispiel | Verb steht |
|---|---|---|
| Aussage | Sie wohnen in Hamburg. | an zweiter Stelle |
| W-Frage | Wo wohnen Sie? | an zweiter Stelle |
| Ja/Nein-Frage | Wohnen Sie in Hamburg? | **an erster Stelle** |

The Tag 05 rule that the verb comes second is not broken here - it is simply that the yes-no question is the one sentence type in German where the verb takes first position. Everything else on this page still obeys it.

### Verneinung mit nicht

`kein` from Tag 04 negates a **noun**: `Ich habe keine Zeit`, `Das ist kein Problem`. It is the negative of `ein`, and it only ever appears in front of a noun.

`nicht` negates **everything else** - a verb, an adjective, an adverb, a place. The rule of thumb is short enough to memorise today:

<Aside type="tip" title="kein or nicht">
If the thing you are cancelling is a **noun**, use `kein`. For anything else, use `nicht`. That single test will be right almost every time at A1.
</Aside>

Where `nicht` sits depends on what it is cancelling:

| Was wird verneint | Position von nicht | Beispiel |
|---|---|---|
| das ganze Verb | ganz am Ende | Ich arbeite **nicht**. |
| das ganze Verb | ganz am Ende | Er kommt heute **nicht**. |
| ein Adjektiv oder Adverb | direkt davor | Ich spreche **nicht** gut Deutsch. |
| ein Ort oder eine Herkunft | direkt davor | Er kommt **nicht** aus Berlin. |
| ein Nomen mit ein | gar nicht, nimm kein | Ich habe **keine** Zeit. |

The pattern underneath those rows: `nicht` goes as late in the sentence as it can, **except** that it jumps in front of the one specific word it is cancelling. `Ich spreche nicht gut Deutsch` cancels the *gut*, not the speaking - you do speak German, just badly. That is the sentence you will need most often this month, so learn it as one piece.

### Die zwölf Bausteine

Read each one aloud twice before you start the drill.

| Deutsch | English | Wann |
|---|---|---|
| Kommen Sie aus Deutschland? | do you come from Germany | verb first, rising tone |
| Wohnen Sie hier? | do you live here | hier is the small word that makes it natural |
| Arbeiten Sie in Hamburg? | do you work in Hamburg | |
| Nein, ich arbeite nicht | no, I do not work | nicht at the end |
| Ich spreche nicht gut Deutsch | I do not speak German well | nicht in front of gut |
| Sprechen Sie bitte langsam | please speak slowly | the most useful sentence today |
| Noch einmal, bitte | once more, please | when you missed it but nearly had it |
| Wie bitte? | sorry, what | when you missed it completely |
| Ich lerne seit zwei Monaten | I have been learning for two months | German uses the present tense here |
| Woher kommen Sie? | where do you come from | woher, not wo - it asks for origin |
| Wo wohnen Sie? | where do you live | |
| Was machen Sie beruflich? | what do you do for a living | beruflich is what turns it into a job question |

<Aside type="caution" title="wo and woher">
`Wo wohnen Sie?` asks where you **are**. `Woher kommen Sie?` asks where you came **from**. The `-her` on the end means motion towards the speaker, so `woher` is literally *from where*. Ask a German `Wo kommen Sie?` and they will understand you and quietly note that you are a beginner.
</Aside>

## 6. Text und Hören

Anna from yesterday, one week later, and this time **you** are the one asking. Every question in Ravi's lines is from today.

> **Anna:** Guten Tag, Ravi! Wie geht es Ihnen?
> **Ravi:** Danke, gut. Und Ihnen? Woher kommen Sie?
> **Anna:** Ich komme aus München. Und Sie?
> **Ravi:** Ich komme aus Indien. Wohnen Sie hier?
> **Anna:** Ja, genau. Ich wohne in Hamburg. Arbeiten Sie in Hamburg?
> **Ravi:** Nein, ich arbeite nicht. Ich studiere an der TUHH.
> **Anna:** Und was machen Sie beruflich?
> **Ravi:** Wie bitte? Sprechen Sie bitte langsam.
> **Anna:** Was machen Sie beruflich?
> **Ravi:** Ich mache einen MBA. Ich spreche nicht gut Deutsch.
> **Anna:** Sie sprechen gut! Lernen Sie lange Deutsch?
> **Ravi:** Ich lerne seit zwei Monaten.

Notice what happens in the middle: Ravi does not understand, says so in German, gets the question again, and the conversation continues. That exchange is not a breakdown - it is the most realistic thing on the page, and being able to run it is worth more than any single chunk.

Read it aloud twice, both parts, **with the melody**: rising on Anna's and Ravi's yes-no questions, falling on everything else. Then cover Ravi's lines and produce them from Anna's cues.

## 8. Sprechen - zehn Minuten

Back to ten minutes after yesterday's twenty. Today the job is reversed: **you** ask the questions and the AI answers. Stand up, and do not let it interview you.

```text
Du bist eine Person in einem Café in Hamburg. Ich bin Anfänger (Tag 7, A1).
Heute übe ich Fragen, also stelle mir selbst fast keine - antworte nur kurz
auf meine Fragen und warte dann, bis ich die nächste stelle.

Sprich langsam, maximal sechs Wörter pro Satz, und nur mit diesen Wörtern:
Guten Tag, Hallo, Tschüss, Wie geht es Ihnen, Danke gut, Und Ihnen,
Ich heiße, Ich komme aus, Ich wohne in, Ich arbeite in, Ich lerne Deutsch,
Ich studiere, Ich mache einen MBA, Ja genau, Nein danke, Entschuldigung,
Ich verstehe nicht, Einen Kaffee bitte, Was kostet das, Das ist zu teuer,
Ich habe keine Zeit, nicht, kein.

Wenn ich zwei Minuten lang keine Frage stelle, sage nur: Haben Sie eine
Frage? Wenn ich einen Fehler in der Wortstellung mache, wiederhole meine
Frage einfach richtig, ohne Erklärung, und antworte dann darauf.
Fang jetzt an und begrüße mich.
```

<Aside type="tip" title="The one thing to listen for">
Your own melody. You will get the word order right on the page and then say `Wohnen Sie hier?` with a flat or falling tone, because that is what English intonation does to a sentence that already looks like a question. Record thirty seconds of this session on your phone and listen back to the ends of your questions. It is uncomfortable and it fixes the problem faster than anything else.
</Aside>

Seventy-two chunks, six verbs in the present tense, three genders, the numbers to a hundred, both ways of saying no, and enough questions to keep a stranger talking for five minutes. A week ago you had none of it.

What you can actually do now: walk into a café in Hamburg, greet someone correctly for the time of day, introduce yourself, say where you are from and what you are doing here, ask them the same four things back, order and pay for a coffee, and say - in German - that you did not understand and would they please slow down. That last one is not a consolation prize. It is the sentence that keeps a conversation in German instead of collapsing into English, and it is why it was taught in week one rather than week six.

<Aside type="caution" title="One honest line about week two">
It gets harder on Tag 08, and not gradually. Week one worked because every sentence you met had a fixed shape you could memorise whole. Week two brings **the cases** - accusative first, then dative - and cases are the point where German stops being learnable as a phrasebook. `der Mann` becomes `den Mann` depending on what he is doing in the sentence, and every article, and later every adjective, starts changing form. Nothing you learned this week becomes wrong, but a lot of it is about to grow endings.

Expect the drill scores to drop for about four days. That is the material, not you.
</Aside>
