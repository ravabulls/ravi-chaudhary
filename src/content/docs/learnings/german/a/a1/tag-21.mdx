*Tag 21 von 60 - mein, dein, sein, ihr, Ihr, und die Familie. Zwei Stunden.*

---

Three weeks, and today is the easiest grammar day since Tag 10 - because the whole lesson is a single sentence: **possessive articles take exactly the same endings as `ein` and `kein`.**

You already know those endings. `ein - einen - einem`. `kein - keinen - keinem`. Now add `mein - meinen - meinem`, `Ihr - Ihren - Ihrem`, and you have the entire system. Nothing new to memorise, only a new stem in front of endings you have been drilling for two weeks.

The vocabulary is family, which is where possessives actually get used - and which is also the single most common thing a German will ask you about after your job.

<Aside type="note" title="How to run this page">
Two hours, top to bottom, every answer **out loud** before you type it. Nothing is saved. Because the grammar is light, the drills push further than usual into combinations - possessive plus case plus past tense in one sentence. Section 10 closes week three.
</Aside>

## 3. Neue Bausteine - zwölf Stück

### Die Possessivartikel

| Person | Possessivartikel | English |
|---|---|---|
| ich | **mein** | my |
| du | **dein** | your (informal) |
| er | **sein** | his |
| sie | **ihr** | her |
| es | **sein** | its |
| wir | **unser** | our |
| Sie | **Ihr** | your (formal) |
| sie (Plural) | **ihr** | their |

Two traps in that list, both worth naming now:

- **`sein` is both the verb *to be* and the possessive *his*.** Context always resolves it, but seeing `sein Bruder` after three weeks of `ich bin` is briefly disorienting.
- **`ihr` does triple duty**: *her*, *their*, and (as you saw on Tag 13) the dative pronoun *to her*. Plus `Ihr` with a capital letter is *your* formal. German simply reuses the word; the capital letter and the context carry the load.

### Die Endungen kennst du schon

Here is the whole of today's grammar, with `ein` on the left to show that nothing has changed:

| | ein | mein | Ihr |
|---|---|---|---|
| **maskulin Nom** | ein | mein | Ihr |
| **maskulin Akk** | ein**en** | mein**en** | Ihr**en** |
| **maskulin Dat** | ein**em** | mein**em** | Ihr**em** |
| **feminin Nom/Akk** | ein**e** | mein**e** | Ihr**e** |
| **feminin Dat** | ein**er** | mein**er** | Ihr**er** |
| **neutral Nom/Akk** | ein | mein | Ihr |
| **neutral Dat** | ein**em** | mein**em** | Ihr**em** |
| **Plural Nom/Akk** | - | mein**e** | Ihr**e** |

<Aside type="tip" title="Why this is a free day">
The three columns are identical except for the stem. If you can say `Ich suche **einen** Bruder`, you can say `Ich suche **meinen** Bruder` - same slot, same ending, different first three letters.

So the only genuinely new thing on this page is the **plural**, which `ein` does not have: `meine Eltern`, `meine Kinder`, `Ihre Geschwister`. Plural nominative and accusative take `-e`, always, for every possessive. One ending, one rule.
</Aside>

### Die Familie

| <span class="de-der">der</span> | <span class="de-die">die</span> | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| der Vater | die Mutter | die Eltern |
| der Bruder | die Schwester | die Geschwister |
| der Sohn | die Tochter | die Kinder |
| der Mann (husband) | die Frau (wife) | - |
| der Freund | die Freundin | die Freunde |

`die Eltern`, `die Geschwister` and `die Kinder` are plural-only in normal use, which is why they are drilled with `meine`.

And one piece of real-world ambiguity: `mein Freund` means *my boyfriend* as often as *my friend*. If you mean a friend and nothing more, Germans say `ein Freund von mir` - *a friend of mine*. Worth knowing before you describe your household to a stranger.

## 6. Text und Hören

The family conversation, which is the one every German has with every newcomer.

> **Anna:** Ravi, haben Sie Geschwister?
> **Ravi:** Ja, ich habe einen Bruder und zwei Schwestern.
> **Anna:** Eine große Familie! Wo wohnen sie?
> **Ravi:** Meine Eltern und meine Schwestern wohnen in Indien. Mein Bruder arbeitet in Delhi.
> **Anna:** Und was macht Ihre Schwester?
> **Ravi:** Meine Schwester studiert. Sie will nach Deutschland kommen.
> **Anna:** Schön! Haben Sie Ihre Familie angerufen?
> **Ravi:** Ja, ich habe sie am Sonntag angerufen. Wir haben eine Stunde telefoniert.
> **Anna:** Und wie geht es Ihrer Familie?
> **Ravi:** Danke, gut. Im Sommer besuche ich meine Eltern.
> **Anna:** Haben Sie Kinder?
> **Ravi:** Nein, ich habe keine Kinder. Und Sie? Wie heißt Ihr Mann?
> **Anna:** Mein Mann heißt Thomas. Wir haben eine Tochter.
> **Ravi:** Wie alt ist Ihre Tochter?
> **Anna:** Sie ist sechs. Sie geht in die Schule.

`groß` is big, `im Sommer` is in summer, `wie alt` is how old, `die Schule` is school. All free.

Read it aloud twice, both parts. Then do the version that matters: cover Ravi's lines and answer **about your own real family**, which is what you will be doing within a week of meeting anyone.

## 8. Sprechen - zehn Minuten

The family conversation, for real. Stand up.

```text
Du bist Anna, eine Bekannte aus Hamburg. Ich bin Anfänger (Tag 21, A1).
Heute übe ich die Possessivartikel und die Familie.

Frag mich ausführlich nach meiner Familie: Haben Sie Geschwister? Wo
wohnen Ihre Eltern? Was macht Ihr Bruder? Was macht Ihre Schwester?
Haben Sie Kinder? Haben Sie Ihre Familie angerufen? Wie geht es Ihrer
Familie? Besuchen Sie Ihre Familie oft?

Erzähle auch von deiner eigenen Familie - dein Mann heißt Thomas, ihr
habt eine Tochter, sie ist sechs.

Sprich langsam, maximal acht Wörter pro Satz. Wenn ich die falsche Endung
benutze - "mit meine Schwester" statt "mit meiner Schwester" - wiederhole
meinen Satz einfach richtig, ohne Erklärung. Fang jetzt an.
```

<Aside type="tip" title="What to listen for today">
The dative endings. `mein Bruder` and `meine Schwester` will be fine because they match the English word order and feel like single words. `mit **meinem** Bruder` and `bei **meiner** Familie` are where it falls apart, because now you need gender and case at the same time and the possessive is in the way.

The shortcut that works: say the `ein` version in your head first. `mit einem Bruder` → `mit meinem Bruder`. Same ending, swap the stem. It is slow for a week and then it is not.
</Aside>

What you can do now that you could not on Tag 14: **talk about yesterday.** That is the single biggest jump in the course so far, because it doubles the range of every conversation - until this week, everything you could say was happening now. You can also make an appointment at a specific time on a named day, say what you are and are not allowed to do, refuse an invitation with a reason, and describe your family.

<Aside type="caution" title="One honest line about week four">
Week four is the consolidation week and the material is deliberately lighter: where things are, how to tell someone to do something, what you prefer, and how to join two thoughts with *because*. None of it is as structurally hard as the cases or the Perfekt.

Which means the work shifts. The last week of A1 is not about learning more grammar - it is about **getting fast enough that the grammar stops being visible**. The drill scores are not the measure any more; the measure is whether the Sprechen block runs for ten minutes without you translating in your head first. If you want one thing to focus on for the next seven days, it is that.
</Aside>
