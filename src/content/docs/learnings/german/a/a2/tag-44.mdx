The telephone is the hardest thing in a foreign language, and everybody knows it. No face, no lips to read, no gestures, compressed audio, and an impatient stranger. People who can hold a conversation in a café freeze completely on the phone.

It is also unavoidable. Yesterday's flat advert said *Besichtigung: Samstag* - and to get on that list you call.

So today is built differently from every other page. The vocabulary is small; the **rescue sentences** are the point, plus the one genuinely mechanical skill German has for the telephone: the **spelling alphabet**.

<Aside type="note" title="How to run this page">
Two hours, and more of it out loud than usual. Section 3 has the spelling alphabet, which you drill rather than read. Section 9 has the first distillation of Goldlist 10.
</Aside>

**Und dann Hören, fünf Minuten.** Same clip, different job, and this is the half that self-study usually skips.

Play the whole forty seconds **three times**, without stopping and without reading anything on this page.

1. **First pass - words.** Write down every German word you catch. Three is a result. Nine is a good day.
2. **Second pass - one sentence.** Write down one complete sentence, even if half of it is a guess.
3. **Third pass - check yourself.** Listen again and correct what you wrote.

**There is no answer key, deliberately.** No transcript is printed here and you are not meant to go hunting for one. A transcript would spare you the exact thing being trained: holding unfamiliar German in your head long enough to make sense of it. The clip never changes, so the only thing that can improve between today and the last day is you.

## 3. Neue Bausteine - zwölf Stück

### Wie Deutsche sich am Telefon melden

This catches out every newcomer. A German does not say *hello* when answering. They say **their surname**, and nothing else:

> **`Chaudhary.`**
>
> **`Müller, guten Tag.`**
>
> **`Hausverwaltung Nordstadt, Schneider, guten Tag.`**

So the first sound you hear on a German call is an unfamiliar surname at speed, which is exactly the hardest thing to catch. That is normal and it is not your failure.

And you answer the same way. Not `Hallo` - your surname. On a mobile it is slightly softer: `Chaudhary, hallo?` is fine.

### Der Anruf

| Deutsch | English |
|---|---|
| anrufen | to call |
| zurückrufen | to call back |
| der Anruf | the call |
| die Nummer | the number |
| die Vorwahl | the area code |
| die Durchwahl | the direct extension |
| besetzt | engaged |
| der Anrufbeantworter | the answering machine |
| die Nachricht | the message |
| die Verbindung | the connection |
| verbinden | to put through |
| Auf Wiederhören | goodbye, on the phone |

<Aside type="caution" title="Auf Wiederhören, not Auf Wiedersehen">
On the telephone, Germans say **`Auf Wiederhören`** - until we *hear* each other again - not `Auf Wiedersehen`. Saying `Auf Wiedersehen` at the end of a call is a small, instantly recognisable tell.

Two more small ones worth getting right:

- **`Hier spricht Ravi Chaudhary`** or **`Hier ist Ravi Chaudhary`** to say who is calling. Not `Ich bin ...`, which is not wrong but sounds translated.
- **`Einen Moment, bitte`** and **`Ich verbinde Sie`** are what you will hear before the hold music. `Ich verbinde Sie` means they are putting you through, not that they are disconnecting you.
</Aside>

### Das Buchstabieralphabet

Germans spell names on the phone with a fixed list of words - `A wie Anton` - exactly as English uses *A for Alpha*. You need this for your own name and address, every time.

| | | | |
|---|---|---|---|
| A wie Anton | H wie Heinrich | O wie Otto | V wie Viktor |
| B wie Berta | I wie Ida | P wie Paula | W wie Wilhelm |
| C wie Cäsar | J wie Julius | Q wie Quelle | X wie Xanthippe |
| D wie Dora | K wie Kaufmann | R wie Richard | Y wie Ypsilon |
| E wie Emil | L wie Ludwig | S wie Samuel | Z wie Zacharias |
| F wie Friedrich | M wie Martha | T wie Theodor | Ä wie Ärger |
| G wie Gustav | N wie Nordpol | U wie Ulrich | Ö wie Ökonom |
| | | | Ü wie Übermut |

So **Chaudhary** goes out like this, and it is worth learning as one unbroken sequence:

> **C wie Cäsar, H wie Heinrich, A wie Anton, U wie Ulrich, D wie Dora, H wie Heinrich, A wie Anton, R wie Richard, Y wie Ypsilon.**

Say it ten times today. Not five - ten. It is the single most frequently needed piece of German in an immigrant's first year, and it has to come out without thinking while someone types.

Three extras you will need:

| Deutsch | English |
|---|---|
| mit Doppel-L | with a double L |
| mit scharfem S / Eszett | with ß |
| groß geschrieben / klein geschrieben | upper case / lower case |

<Aside type="tip" title="Anton or Aachen - both are current">
The classic names above - Anton, Berta, Cäsar - are what people actually say, and what an official on the other end will use with you.

The German standards body revised the spelling table in 2022, and the current version of DIN 5009 uses **city names** instead: `A wie Aachen, B wie Berlin, C wie Chemnitz, D wie Düsseldorf, E wie Essen, F wie Frankfurt, H wie Hamburg, K wie Köln, M wie München`, and so on. The reason given was that several of the old names came from the 1930s list, where the Jewish first names had been removed - `D wie David` became `D wie Dora`, `S wie Samuel` became `S wie Siegfried`. The post-war table restored some of them, and the 2022 revision sidestepped the question by moving to cities.

Practically: **learn the names, recognise the cities.** You will be spelled at in both, and nobody will mind which you use. If you prefer the cities on principle, that is a perfectly defensible choice and is now the standard one.
</Aside>

### Die Rettungssätze

The real content of this page. Four sentences, and the point is that they must be **faster than your embarrassment**:

| Deutsch | English |
|---|---|
| Können Sie das bitte wiederholen? | can you repeat that, please |
| Können Sie bitte langsamer sprechen? | can you speak more slowly, please |
| Ich verstehe Sie nicht gut, die Verbindung ist schlecht. | I cannot hear you well, the connection is bad |
| Können Sie das bitte buchstabieren? | can you spell that, please |

`die Verbindung ist schlecht` is the most useful of the four and it is worth noticing why: it moves the problem from your German to the telephone line. It is polite, it is usually at least half true, and it buys you a slower repetition without either side losing face.

## 6. Text und Hören

The call that gets you onto the viewing list. Note how short it is - a German call of this kind runs about ninety seconds, and the whole thing is four pieces of information.

> **Schneider:** Hausverwaltung Nordstadt, Schneider, guten Tag.
> **Ravi:** Guten Tag, hier spricht Ravi Chaudhary. Ich rufe wegen der Wohnung in Eimsbüttel an.
> **Schneider:** Die 2-Zimmer-Wohnung? Die ist noch frei.
> **Ravi:** Sehr gut. Kann ich die Wohnung besichtigen?
> **Schneider:** Die Besichtigung ist am Samstag um vierzehn Uhr. Sammelbesichtigung.
> **Ravi:** Entschuldigung, können Sie das bitte wiederholen? Die Verbindung ist schlecht.
> **Schneider:** Samstag, vierzehn Uhr. Also zwei Uhr nachmittags.
> **Ravi:** Samstag um vierzehn Uhr. Danke. Soll ich die Unterlagen mitbringen?
> **Schneider:** Ja, bitte vollständig. Schufa, drei Gehaltsabrechnungen, Ausweis.
> **Ravi:** Habe ich alles dabei. Wie war Ihr Name, bitte?
> **Schneider:** Schneider. S wie Samuel, C wie Cäsar, H wie Heinrich, N wie Nordpol, E, I, D, E, R.
> **Ravi:** Danke. Und darf ich meinen Namen buchstabieren? Chaudhary - C wie Cäsar, H wie Heinrich, A wie Anton, U wie Ulrich, D wie Dora, H wie Heinrich, A wie Anton, R wie Richard, Y wie Ypsilon.
> **Schneider:** Habe ich. Dann bis Samstag, Herr Chaudhary.
> **Ravi:** Vielen Dank für Ihre Hilfe. Auf Wiederhören.
> **Schneider:** Auf Wiederhören.

`die Sammelbesichtigung` is a group viewing - everyone at once, which is the norm in Hamburg. `vollständig` is complete, from yesterday's advert.

Read it aloud twice, both parts. Then read it a third time **holding your phone to your ear with the other hand covering your eyes**, so you cannot see the page. The point is not the text; it is doing this with no visual support.

<Aside type="tip" title="Repeat the time back - always">
Look at Ravi's line after the repetition: `Samstag um vierzehn Uhr. Danke.`

He repeats it. That one habit prevents the most common and most expensive phone failure in a foreign language - agreeing confidently to the wrong day or the wrong time.

German uses the **24-hour clock** for appointments: `vierzehn Uhr`, `achtzehn Uhr dreißig`. If someone says `vierzehn Uhr` and you are not instantly sure, say it back and add the conversion: **`Also zwei Uhr nachmittags?`** No German will think less of you for it, and plenty of them do the same thing.
</Aside>

## 8. Sprechen - zehn Minuten

Three calls, and the model is instructed to be hard to hear. Stand up, and hold your phone to your ear even if the audio is coming from the laptop.

```text
Du bist am Telefon. Ich bin Lernender (Tag 44). Wir machen DREI kurze
Anrufe, jeder maximal zwei Minuten.

ANRUF 1 - Hausverwaltung. Du meldest dich nur mit dem Namen "Schneider".
Ich frage nach der Wohnung und einem Besichtigungstermin. Nenne einen
Termin im 24-Stunden-Format.

ANRUF 2 - Arztpraxis. Du gibst mir einen Termin und buchstabierst MIR
einen Namen, den ich zurückbuchstabieren muss.

ANRUF 3 - jemand hat sich verwählt und will zu "Frau Berger". Ich muss
sagen, dass hier niemand mit dem Namen wohnt.

Wichtig: sprich schnell und undeutlich, so wie am Telefon. Wenn ich
"Können Sie das bitte wiederholen" oder "langsamer" sage, wiederhole
EINMAL langsam - dann wieder normal schnell.

Verlange bei jedem Anruf, dass ich meinen Namen buchstabiere.
```

<Aside type="caution" title="The thing that actually goes wrong on the phone">
Not vocabulary. **Silence.**

The failure mode is this: you miss a word, you freeze while trying to reconstruct it, three more seconds of German arrive, and now you have lost the sentence as well as the word. The call collapses from one missed word.

The fix is to interrupt immediately, at the word itself, not at the end of the sentence. `Entschuldigung -` plus one of the four rescue sentences. Said at once it costs five seconds; said twenty seconds too late it costs the call.

So drill the rescue sentences until they are **faster than your hesitation.** That is the actual goal of today, and it is worth more than the whole spelling alphabet.
</Aside>

## 9. Auf Papier - Goldlist

<div class="de-paper">

**Diktat.** Ten-second loop on the shadowing audio, at the quiet volume from section 2. Six German words by hand, then correct in a second colour.

**Das Buchstabieralphabet, aus dem Kopf.** Write the whole list by hand - all twenty-six letters with their names, plus the three umlauts. Then check it. The ones that will be missing are Q, X and Y, which is fine, because the one you must never hesitate on is **Y wie Ypsilon**, and it is the last letter of your surname.

**Die Namen-Übung.** Write out, in full `X wie ...` form, by hand: your surname, your first name, your street, and the name of your city. **Satz des Tages.** The four rescue sentences, by hand, from memory. Then write underneath, in German, the three-line opening of a call: who you are, why you are calling, what you want.
