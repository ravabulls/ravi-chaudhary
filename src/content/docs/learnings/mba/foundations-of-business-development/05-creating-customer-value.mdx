By now we can spot an opportunity and turn it into an idea. This chapter asks the harder question underneath all of it: **does the idea actually create value for someone - enough value that they will part with money for it?** A business only survives if the answer is yes. So before we design a single feature, we need a clear, almost surgical understanding of what "value" means to a customer, and how to build an offer that fits their real needs.

<Aside type="tip">
**The definition to hold on to:** value is not what your product *does* - it's what the product *does for the customer*. A pile of clever features is worthless if it doesn't help a real person get a real, important job done better than the alternatives they already use.
</Aside>

## 1 · Value created vs value captured

Start with the money. Imagine a single customer looking at your product. Three numbers matter:

- **B** - the **benefit**, the *most* this customer would ever be willing to pay (their "walk-away" price).
- **P** - the **price** they actually pay.
- **C** - the **cost** to you of making and delivering the product.

Those three numbers slice the pie into two surpluses - and the gap between B and C is the whole reason a trade happens at all.

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-formula">
    <div>
      <div class="name">Value created</div>
      <code>B − C</code>
      <p>The total new value the deal brings into the world - the distance between what the customer <em>would</em> pay and what it costs you to make. This is the size of the pie.</p>
    </div>
    <div>
      <div class="name">Value captured</div>
      <code>P − C</code>
      <p>Your slice - the producer surplus, better known as <strong>profit margin</strong>. It's the part of the value you keep by setting a price above your cost.</p>
    </div>
    <div>
      <div class="name">Customer surplus</div>
      <code>B − P</code>
      <p>The customer's slice - the value they keep. They pay P but would have paid up to B, so they walk away better off. This is <em>why</em> they buy.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Value created (B minus C) is split between the customer (B minus P) and the firm (P minus C). A price only exists in the gap between what the customer values it at and what it costs you.</figcaption>
</div>

Two lessons fall straight out of this simple picture.

First, **value created and value captured are not the same thing.** You can create enormous value and capture almost none of it - early Google gave away search for free and captured revenue only years later, once ads arrived. Creating value is necessary; capturing it is a separate design problem (that's the business-model chapter's job).

Second, **the customer must always keep a surplus.** If P equals B, you've squeezed out every drop of their surplus and given them no reason to prefer you over doing nothing. A healthy venture leaves the customer visibly better off *and* keeps a margin for itself. The trouble for a founder is that, unlike in a textbook, **you don't know B or C yet.** Working out how much value you create, and for whom, is the first real job - and the rest of this chapter is about doing exactly that.

## 2 · The types of value

"Value" is not one thing. A customer can benefit in several different currencies at once, and it pays to name them so you don't accidentally build only one kind. A useful split - drawn from Osterwalder's value dimensions and Bain's "elements of value" research - is four families:

| Type of value | What it gives the customer | Everyday examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Functional** | The job gets done - it works, it's reliable, it saves effort, it's convenient | A drill that bores clean holes; software that never crashes; one-click checkout |
| **Economic** | Money or time saved, or income earned | Shorter assembly time on a factory line; a tool that raises the quality (and price) of the customer's own product; lower running costs |
| **Emotional** | It feels good - less stress, more fun, reassurance, delight | A well-being app that calms you; a warranty that removes worry; a game that's simply enjoyable |
| **Social / self-expressive** | It signals who you are to others - status, belonging, identity | A Rolex or a MacBook as a statement; a brand that says "I care about the planet" |

<Aside type="note">
**A rough rule of thumb from the course:** in **business-to-business (B2B)** - selling to companies - **economic value dominates**, because a firm buying from you is ultimately doing maths on cost savings and extra revenue. In **business-to-consumer (B2C)** - selling to people - **emotional and social value carry more weight**, because people buy for how a thing makes them feel and what it says about them. "B2B" and "B2C" are just shorthand for who's on the other side of the sale.
</Aside>

There's also a **hierarchy** to value - the idea that the four types stack, roughly, from basic to profound. Bain arranges them as a pyramid: you have to nail the functional basics before higher, "stickier" kinds of value even register with the customer.

The practical warning here is the one the course keeps repeating: **technological features are not value.** A longer spec sheet, a faster processor, a cleverer algorithm - none of that is worth anything until it *lands* as one of these benefits for a customer. Technology should be the thing that *enables* value, never a substitute for it.

## 3 · Jobs-to-be-Done: what is the customer really hiring?

Here's the reframe that changes how you look at your own product. It comes from Harvard's **Clayton Christensen**, and the one-liner is famous:

<Aside type="tip">
**"People don't want a quarter-inch drill - they want a quarter-inch hole."** Nobody wakes up wanting to *own a drill*. They want a hole (and really, they want the shelf on the wall, and the tidy room, and to feel like a capable person). The drill is just the thing they **hire** to get that deeper job done.
</Aside>

That's the core of **Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD)**: customers "hire" products and services to make progress on a **job** - a task or outcome they're trying to accomplish in a given situation. Focus on the **job**, not the product. Once you do, you notice a job is rarely purely practical. It has three layers:

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-pillars">
    <div class="sc-pillar">
      <div class="head">Functional job</div>
      <ul>
        <li>The concrete task to complete</li>
        <li>"Get to work across town"</li>
        <li>"Store my medical records so any doctor can see them"</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="sc-pillar">
      <div class="head">Emotional job</div>
      <ul>
        <li>How the customer wants to <em>feel</em></li>
        <li>"Feel safe and in control on the commute"</li>
        <li>"Stop worrying my files will get lost"</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="sc-pillar">
      <div class="head">Social job</div>
      <ul>
        <li>How they want to be <em>seen</em> by others</li>
        <li>"Look responsible and modern to my peers"</li>
        <li>"Be the organised patient the doctor respects"</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>One job, three layers. Miss the emotional and social layers and you build something that technically works but nobody loves.</figcaption>
</div>

Around every job sit two things worth mapping carefully:

- **Pains** - everything that's annoying, risky, or costly about getting the job done *today*, with the solutions the customer currently uses. What goes wrong, what wastes time, what they're afraid of.
- **Gains** - the outcomes and benefits the customer wants: the results they need, the things that would delight them, what they secretly dream of.

The most powerful move JTBD gives you is that it **redraws the competitive map.** If customers are hiring you to do a *job*, then **anything that does the same job is a rival** - not just products that look like yours. A milkshake bought to make a boring morning commute more bearable isn't really competing with other milkshakes; it's competing with bananas, bagels, coffee and boredom itself. Define your competition by the job, and you see threats and openings that a narrow "products like mine" view would completely miss.

## 4 · The Value Proposition Canvas: engineering the fit

Osterwalder's **Value Proposition Canvas (VPC)** takes everything above and turns it into a design tool. It has two halves that must be built to *match* each other. The whole point of the tool is that one-word idea: **fit.**

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-split">
    <div class="sc-panel rsp">
      <div class="head">Customer profile <small>the "Who?" - observe, don't invent</small></div>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Customer Jobs</strong> - the functional, emotional and social jobs they're trying to get done, in their words</li>
        <li><strong>Pains</strong> - the frustrations, risks and obstacles they hit doing the job now</li>
        <li><strong>Gains</strong> - the outcomes and benefits they want, from the essential to the "would love"</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="sc-panel eff">
      <div class="head">Value map <small>the "What?" - your offer, designed to answer</small></div>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Products &amp; Services</strong> - what you actually offer that the job can be done with</li>
        <li><strong>Pain Relievers</strong> - exactly how your offer removes or eases the customer's specific pains</li>
        <li><strong>Gain Creators</strong> - exactly how your offer produces the gains the customer is after</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Two halves of the canvas. The right side (value map, the "What?") is engineered to answer the left side (customer profile, the "Who?"). Build the left from real observation <em>first</em>.</figcaption>
</div>

So what does "**fit**" actually mean? It's not that you've addressed *every* pain and gain - that's impossible and unnecessary. Fit means your **pain relievers and gain creators line up with the pains and gains the customer cares about most** for the job they're doing. You've matched your strongest medicine to their sharpest pain. When that alignment is real, customers recognise the offer instantly as "for me." When it's absent, you get the classic failure the course keeps returning to: a technically impressive product that solves problems nobody was losing sleep over.

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-chips">
    <span class="sc-chip violet">Job the customer is doing</span>
    <span class="sc-chip violet">Their worst pain</span>
    <span class="sc-chip violet">Their most-wanted gain</span>
    <span class="sc-chip">→ fit ←</span>
    <span class="sc-chip green">Your best pain reliever</span>
    <span class="sc-chip green">Your strongest gain creator</span>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Fit is a matching exercise, not a checklist. Aim your best relievers and creators at the most important pains and gains - not at all of them.</figcaption>
</div>

The discipline the canvas forces is **sequence.** You fill in the customer profile *before* you design the value map - and you fill it from real evidence about real customers, not from your own assumptions. (That's exactly why the next chapter is about replacing assumptions with customer research.) A quick way to pressure-test any idea is to ask the canvas's key questions in order:

<Steps>

1. **What job** is the customer trying to get done - functionally, emotionally, socially? How are they doing it *right now*?

2. **What are their biggest pains** with today's solution - what's annoying, risky, or too expensive?

3. **What gains** would they value most - what would genuinely make them happier?

4. **Only then:** what could I offer that relieves those exact pains and creates those exact gains - better than the alternative they use today?

</Steps>

## 5 · The takeaway

If there's one habit to carry out of this chapter, it's this: **understand the job and the customer profile before you design the offer.** Founders love to start on the right-hand side of the canvas - the product, the features, the tech - because that's the fun part and the part they control. But the ventures with the tightest fit start on the *left*: with a job that is **real, important, and badly served today.** A real job means someone genuinely does it; an important job means they care about doing it well; an unsatisfied job means the current solutions leave painful gaps. Find a job that's all three, aim your best value at its sharpest pains and gains, and you're building on solid ground. Skip that, and no amount of engineering will save you.

## Revision summary

<Aside type="tip">
**The one-minute recap**

- **Value created (B − C)** is the whole pie; it's split into **value captured (P − C)** - your margin - and **customer surplus (B − P)**, which the customer keeps and which is the reason they buy. Creating value and capturing it are two different jobs.
- **Four types of value:** **functional** (it works), **economic** (money/time saved or earned), **emotional** (it feels good), **social/self-expressive** (it signals identity). They form a **hierarchy** - functional is the price of entry; emotional and social win loyalty. Economic value dominates in B2B, emotional/social in B2C.
- **Jobs-to-be-Done (Christensen):** customers **hire** a product to get a **job** done - "not a drill, a hole." Jobs have **functional, emotional and social** layers, plus **pains** and **gains**. JTBD **redraws competition** - anything that does the job is a rival.
- **Value Proposition Canvas (Osterwalder):** the **customer profile** (Jobs, Pains, Gains) must **fit** the **value map** (Products & Services, Pain Relievers, Gain Creators). **Fit** = your best relievers and creators aimed at the customer's *most important* pains and gains.
- **Practical rule:** understand the job and profile *first*, from real evidence; the tightest ventures start from a **real, important, unsatisfied job.**
</Aside>
