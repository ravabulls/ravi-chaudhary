Chapter 1 said the entrepreneur's job is to spot an opportunity and act on it. This chapter zooms in on a question that quietly decides how hard that job will be: **how new is the idea, really?** Two ideas can both be "innovations" and yet be worlds apart in cost, time, risk and how fiercely people push back. So we first learn to *measure* newness along a few dimensions, then face the uncomfortable truth that follows from it — **the more novel an idea is, the more it gets resisted** — and what to do about that resistance.

<Aside type="tip">
**The thread that runs through the whole chapter:** measuring innovativeness is *not* an academic exercise. Knowing how new an idea is — and to whom — lets you predict its development cost and time, how fast the market will adopt it, and **how much opposition it will trigger**, inside and outside your organisation. Novelty is where the reward is; it is also where the resistance is. Both have to be managed.
</Aside>

## 1 · Innovation comes in many forms

Before measuring newness, it helps to remember that an innovation isn't only a shiny new *product*. It can live in several places:

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-chips">
    <span class="sc-chip">Product <small>e.g. antigen test</small></span>
    <span class="sc-chip green">Service <small>e.g. Moia mobility</small></span>
    <span class="sc-chip amber">Product–service hybrid <small>e.g. Apple Pay</small></span>
    <span class="sc-chip violet">Process / structure <small>e.g. 3D printing</small></span>
    <span class="sc-chip">Business model <small>e.g. SaaS licensing</small></span>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Innovation can sit in the offering itself, in how it is produced, or in how it is sold and charged for. The Doblin group famously stretches this to "ten types of innovation".</figcaption>
</div>

Two ideas to hold on to before we go further:

- **Innovation is more than invention.** An *invention* is a new technical idea; an *innovation* is that idea actually **exploited** in the market. In shorthand: **innovation = invention + exploitation.** And crucially, an innovation does not always *need* an invention — recombining existing things can be innovation enough.
- **Incremental versus radical.** Picture technology performance rising along an **S-curve** (slow start, steep middle, plateau). An **incremental** innovation is a step *along* the current curve — a bit better, faster, cheaper. A **radical** innovation starts a *whole new curve* — a new technological principle that can eventually make the old one obsolete.

## 2 · The four dimensions of "how new?"

Here is the core tool of the chapter. "Innovativeness" is **multi-dimensional** — an idea can be barely new on one axis and wildly new on another — so we assess it from four angles. The same idea gets a different score depending on whose eyes you borrow.

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-quad">
    <div class="q blue"><div class="qh">1 · New technological solution? <small>the engineer's view</small></div><ul><li>Uses a new technological principle?</li><li>A quantum leap in performance — better, faster, smaller, cheaper, more reliable?</li><li>Makes the "old" technology obsolete?</li></ul></div>
    <div class="q green"><div class="qh">2 · New for the customer? <small>the user's view</small></div><ul><li>Solves an urgent, unmet problem?</li><li>Benefit-to-cost ratio: does the extra benefit outweigh the customer's cost of switching?</li><li>Forces new mental models, new routines, heavy learning?</li></ul></div>
    <div class="q amber"><div class="qh">3 · New to industry &amp; environment? <small>the market's view</small></div><ul><li>Endangers established value chains?</li><li>Changes the rules of the competitive game?</li><li>Clashes with current regulation, or needs new infrastructure?</li></ul></div>
    <div class="q violet"><div class="qh">4 · New for the innovating firm? <small>the company's own view</small></div><ul><li>Forces a change in firm identity or strategy?</li><li>Calls for new organisational structures?</li><li>Requires new knowledge, new partners, new networks?</li></ul></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>The four dimensions of innovativeness. A "10 out of 10" on one axis and a "2" on another is completely normal — and that mix is exactly what tells you how to manage the project.</figcaption>
</div>

<Aside type="note">
**Technological newness is not the same as value.** More technical features do not automatically mean more *perceived quality*, and better performance on a datasheet does not automatically mean more *customer value*. Malcolm McLean's shipping container carried no new physics at all, yet it was radically innovative for the *industry* — it rewrote global logistics. Score each dimension on its own; don't let a strong engineering story flatter the others.
</Aside>

The customer dimension deserves a second look, because it is the one founders most often get wrong. A customer weighs a new offering as a **benefit-to-cost ratio**, and "cost" here is much more than price. Adoption is expensive whenever it demands:

| Hidden cost of adoption | What the customer has to do |
|---|---|
| **Mental-model change** | Rethink how a task "should" work — unlearn an old picture |
| **Behavioural change** | Rewire daily routines, processes, habits |
| **Learning effort** | Acquire real new knowledge and skills before any payoff |

That is why the **QWERTY** keyboard has survived for roughly 140 years despite faster layouts existing: the switching cost (everyone re-learning) dwarfs the modest benefit. High innovativeness for the customer is a *warning light*, not a bragging point.

## 3 · Consequences: novelty cuts both ways

Now the payoff of all that measuring. Scoring the four dimensions lets you **predict how the project will behave** — and manage it accordingly. The higher an idea scores, the higher its potential reward, but also the higher its **uncertainty, its adoption time, and the resistance it meets**.

  <div class="sc-split">
    <div class="sc-panel rsp">
      <div class="head">Creating genuinely new business <small>high innovativeness</small></div>
      <ul>
        <li>Uncertain and <strong>risky</strong></li>

        <li>Needs upfront investment</li>
        <li>Disturbs routines; forces change</li>
        <li>Often starts small, with thin margins</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="sc-panel eff">
      <div class="head">Strengthening the existing core <small>low innovativeness</small></div>
      <ul>
        <li>Familiar and rather <strong>certain</strong></li>
        <li>Returns show up short-term</li>
        <li>Reliable return on investment</li>
        <li>Fits current routines, tools, culture</li>
        <li>Easy to plan and defend internally</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Same company, two very different risk profiles. A highly innovative project can't be run like a core-business project — it needs a different form of project, a different budget logic, and a realistic time-to-market.</figcaption>
</div>

There is also a **context check**: even a brilliant idea flops if the world isn't ready for it. Before betting on a novel offering, ask whether the surrounding conditions actually allow adoption — across three fronts:

| Readiness front | Questions to ask |
|---|---|
| **Technology & infrastructure** | Is the enabling infrastructure in place and accessible? Are complementary technologies available? |
| **Market & customer** | Can target customers pay? Do they understand the benefit? Will partners (distributors, installers) adopt it too? |
| **Regulation** | Do current laws and rules permit it — or block it? |

<Aside type="caution">
**Higher score, different management.** The point of the four dimensions is operational: a project that is highly novel *to the firm* needs new capabilities and a change-management plan; one that is highly novel *to customers* needs patient adoption support; one that is novel *to the industry* will provoke incumbents. You measure so you can staff, budget and time the project honestly — not to win an "innovativeness" contest.
</Aside>

## 4 · Opposition to innovation: why people resist

Here is the hard part. The problem is rarely the *idea* — it is **the people who meet the idea**. New qualities are cognitively hard to grasp, and change threatens something almost everyone holds: their comfort, their skills, their power. Even Steve Ballmer publicly *laughed* at the first iPhone; Polaroid, staring straight at digital photography, kept polishing film. When people can't build a "faster horse", they defend the horse.

Three insights explain why resistance is the *default*, not the exception:

  <div class="sc-pillars">
    <div class="sc-pillar">
      <div class="head">1 · Novelty is hard to grasp</div>
      <ul><li>Understanding the <em>new qualities</em> of an innovation is cognitively complex.</li><li>People judge the new thing with old categories — and find it wanting.</li></ul>
    </div>
    <div class="sc-pillar">
      <div class="head">2 · Inside-focus blinds</div>
      <ul><li>A firm absorbed in its own routines stops reading external signals.</li><li>Polaroid saw digital photography coming and still missed it.</li></ul>
    </div>
    <div class="sc-pillar">
      <div class="head">3 · Fear of losing</div>
      <ul><li>Beyond confusion sits real fear — of cost, effort and lost status.</li><li>"Why would I rewrite my skills for an unproven device?"</li></ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Cognitive difficulty, organisational blindness and fear of loss stack on top of one another — which is why even smart, well-run firms resist good ideas.</figcaption>
</div>

Resistance shows up on **two sides**, and it pays to keep them apart:

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-split">
    <div class="sc-panel">
      <div class="head">Demand side <small>the customers / users who must adopt</small></div>
      <ul>
        <li>High <strong>switching costs</strong>: new routines, retraining, retooling</li>
        <li>"The new thing isn't better enough to justify the risk"</li>
        <li>Fear of writing off hard-won skills</li>
        <li>Functional fixedness — can't picture a different way</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="sc-panel rsp">
      <div class="head">Supply side <small>the people inside the innovating firm</small></div>
      <ul>
        <li>The innovation could <strong>cannibalise</strong> a profitable business</li>
        <li>It devalues existing resources and capabilities</li>
        <li>New markets start small, at lower margins</li>
        <li>Threat to power, jobs, budgets and routines</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Opposition is not just "customers being slow". Some of the fiercest resistance comes from <em>inside</em> — colleagues who stand to lose from the very idea you are championing.</figcaption>
</div>

Cutting across both sides, the *reasons* group into three families:

| Type of reason | What is really going on | Typical voice |
|---|---|---|
| **Cognitive** | The novelty is genuinely hard to understand; people sit in a mental comfort zone and lack the knowledge to judge it | "I don't see how this even works." |
| **Economic** | Real money is at stake — cannibalised profits, devalued assets, sunk investments in the status quo, thin early margins | "The added value doesn't justify the investment." |
| **Social / political** | The idea threatens power, status, jobs and routines — plus **envy** and the low legitimacy granted to whoever proposed it | "Not invented here — why should *we* follow *them*?" |

<Aside type="note">
**The "not-invented-here" reflex.** Ideas born elsewhere get a hostile reception simply because they weren't ours — a social barrier dressed up as a technical objection. It is one reason the *inventor's* standing matters as much as the invention's merit: a proposal from a low-status source is judged more harshly than the same proposal from an authority.
</Aside>

## 5 · Measures to overcome opposition

Resistance is normal — **every substantial innovation faces it** — so treat it as a task, not an insult. The playbook falls into three moves: adjust your own attitude, make others *understand*, and turn opponents into allies.

First, **adapt your style**: cool down (resistance is expected); genuinely *listen* to the arguments (you might be wrong too); and diagnose *which* reason you are facing — fear, ignorance, or hard economics — because each needs a different answer.

Then match the counter-measure to the reason:

| Reason for resistance | What actually helps |
|---|---|
| **Cognitive — "I don't get it"** | Speak in *their* mental models: use concepts, terms and logic they already own. Show **real things** — prototypes, simulations, live customers — not just numbers and slide decks. |
| **Doubt — "prove it"** | Be factual; provide proof for your key claims. Start a small, **irreversible pilot** that quietly proves you right. |
| **Complacency — "why change?"** | Create a **sense of urgency**: make the cost of the *do-nothing* scenario vivid and concrete. |
| **Fear of loss — "I'll lose out"** | **Address the losers directly.** Emphasise the benefit for those who must contribute; let them win too. Design the change so it doesn't gut their position. |
| **Political / status** | **Find allies** — a powerful internal sponsor ("power promoter"), plus external voices (customers, distributors, experts). *Share* the credit; make respected authorities co-owners of the idea. |

<Aside type="tip">
**Let them win too.** The single most useful line in this section. Opposition driven by fear of losing evaporates when the "loser" is given a genuine stake in the win — a role, credit, a protected budget. Overcoming resistance is less about arguing harder and more about **redesigning the deal** so fewer people have a reason to fight you.
</Aside>

## 6 · Stakeholder analysis

All of this needs to be *organised*, and the tool for that is **stakeholder analysis** — treating the people around an innovation as deliberately as you treat the technology.

**Stakeholder management** means identifying every individual, group or organisation that will be affected by the innovation *and* could influence whether it succeeds — then working out how to handle each. The routine is four steps:

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-flow col">
    <div class="sc-node slate">1 · List every stakeholder<span class="sub">anyone who affects, or is affected by, the rollout</span></div>
    <span class="arrow">↓</span>
    <div class="sc-node violet">2 · Read their interests<span class="sub">are they aligned with — or endangered by — the idea?</span></div>
    <span class="arrow">↓</span>
    <div class="sc-node amber">3 · Rate their power<span class="sub">how much can they help or block you?</span></div>
    <span class="arrow">↓</span>
    <div class="sc-node green">4 · Decide your action<span class="sub">meet, realign, fight or circumvent each one</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Stakeholder analysis in four moves — from a raw list of names to a concrete action per person.</figcaption>
</div>

The output is best drawn as a **two-by-two grid**: a stakeholder's **influence** (low or high) against their **attitude** (for or against). Where they land tells you how to spend your limited attention.

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-axis">Attitude → &nbsp; supportive (left) &nbsp;·&nbsp; opposed (right)</div>
  <div class="sc-quad">
    <div class="q green"><div class="qh">Promoters <small>high influence · for</small></div><ul><li><strong>Use as supporters.</strong> Your engine — give them a visible role and keep them close.</li></ul></div>
    <div class="q amber"><div class="qh">Powerful opponents <small>high influence · against</small></div><ul><li><strong>Realign, convince — or isolate / circumvent.</strong> The group that can kill the project; win them over first.</li></ul></div>
    <div class="q blue"><div class="qh">Goodwill, little clout <small>low influence · for</small></div><ul><li><strong>Keep satisfied.</strong> Friendly but weak; keep them informed and happy at low cost.</li></ul></div>
    <div class="q violet"><div class="qh">Minor sceptics <small>low influence · against</small></div><ul><li><strong>Monitor.</strong> Low effort — watch quietly in case their power grows.</li></ul></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Power × attitude. Effort flows to the top row — high-influence people — and above all to the powerful opponents, who decide whether the innovation lives.</figcaption>
</div>

<Aside type="caution">
**Structural blockers count as stakeholders too.** Resistance isn't always a person with an opinion — sometimes it's how the org is wired. **Silo budgeting** is the classic trap: a hospital innovation that costs one department (say, surgery) more but saves another (recovery) far more still gets blocked, because no single budget owner sees the whole gain. Map the *budget lines*, not just the people, and manage the seams between them.
</Aside>

## Revision summary

<Aside type="tip">
**The one-minute recap**

- **Innovation = invention + exploitation.** It can live in a product, service, process or business model; **incremental** = a step along the S-curve, **radical** = a whole new curve.
- Assess innovativeness on **four dimensions**: new to the *technology*, the *customer*, the *industry/environment*, and the *innovating firm*. The same idea scores differently on each — and technical newness is not the same as customer value.
- **Consequences:** higher novelty means higher reward *and* higher uncertainty, longer adoption, more resistance. Measure it so you can budget, staff and time the project honestly — and check the world is *ready* (technology, market, regulation).
- **Opposition** comes from a **demand side** (switching costs, fear, functional fixedness) and a **supply side** (cannibalisation, devalued skills, threat to power/jobs). Reasons group as **cognitive, economic, social/political** — plus the **not-invented-here** reflex.
- **Measures:** adapt your style; make people understand (their mental models, real prototypes, an irreversible pilot, a sense of urgency); and **let the losers win too** — find allies and share the credit.
- **Stakeholder analysis:** list → read interests → rate power → decide action, then map everyone on a **power × attitude** grid (promoters, powerful opponents, keep-satisfied, monitor). Manage the people — and the budget silos — as deliberately as the technology.
</Aside>
