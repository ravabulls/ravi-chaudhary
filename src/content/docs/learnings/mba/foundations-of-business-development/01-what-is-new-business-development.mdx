This first chapter sets the frame for the whole module: what "new business development" actually is, who does it, and the rough process that turns a spark of an idea into a working business. Everything later - opportunities, ideas, customer value, strategy - hangs on the simple definition we start with.

<Aside type="tip">
**The definition to hold on to:** **entrepreneurship = opportunity recognition + opportunity exploitation.** First you *spot* a valuable opportunity; then you *do something* about it. Part A of the course is mostly the first half; Part B is mostly the second. Miss either and there's no business.
</Aside>

## 1 · What is entrepreneurship?

Two classic definitions capture the two faces of it:

- **Peter Drucker:** *"The entrepreneur always searches for change, responds to it, and exploits it as an opportunity."* - entrepreneurship is a **response to change**.
- **Joseph Schumpeter:** entrepreneurs are the source of **"creative destruction"** - the churn by which new offerings displace old ones and drive the economy forward.

Put together: the world keeps changing (technology, regulation, tastes, shocks like a pandemic), and every change quietly closes some businesses while opening others. The entrepreneur is the person who *notices* and *acts*.

<Aside type="note">
**The boiling-frog problem.** The changes that threaten today's business and create tomorrow's often arrive **slowly and incrementally**, so they're easy to miss - like the frog that doesn't notice the water heating up. Part of the entrepreneurial skill is reading weak signals early, before they're obvious to everyone.
</Aside>

## 2 · It's not only about founding a start-up

A common misconception is that "entrepreneurship" means quitting your job to found a start-up. In fact, new businesses get built in **two settings** - and the skills largely transfer:

The through-line: whether you're a founder or an intrapreneur, the job is the same - **recognise a business opportunity, match it to the resources you can command, and exploit it**.

## 3 · The process: from idea to venture

New business development isn't a lightning bolt of genius; it's a **disciplined process**. Two well-known models say much the same thing in a slightly different order:

| Stage | Bill Aulet - *Disciplined Entrepreneurship* | Grichnik et al. - *Startup Navigator* |
|---|---|---|
| **Understand** | Market analysis: potential and target customer | Problem, customer analysis, solution concept |
| **Design** | Problem & value proposition, customer research, competitive analysis | Design and "pretotyping"; value proposition, competitive & market analysis |
| **Plan** | Sales plan, revenue model, financial analysis | Development plan, resources, partnering |
| **Build & scale** | Design/pretotype → scaling and growth | Marketing & sales, financial planning, scaling and growth |

<Aside type="tip">
**"Pretotyping", not just prototyping.** Before you build the thing (a prototype), test whether anyone *wants* it with the cheapest possible stand-in (a "pretotype") - a landing page, a mock-up, a fake button. It's the theme that returns in the final chapter on **experimentation**: buy evidence before you spend on building.
</Aside>

## Revision summary

<Aside type="tip">
**The one-minute recap**

- **Entrepreneurship = opportunity recognition + opportunity exploitation.** Spot a valuable change, then act on it.
- **Drucker**: the entrepreneur searches for, responds to and exploits change. **Schumpeter**: entrepreneurs drive **creative destruction**. Watch for slow, boiling-frog changes.
- New business is built by **entrepreneurs** (outside established firms - start-ups, business plans, VC) *and* **intrapreneurs** (inside them - business cases, project budgets, core competencies). Same core job.
- It's a **disciplined process** (Aulet, Startup Navigator): understand → design → plan → build & scale. Test cheaply ("pretotype") before you build.
</Aside>
