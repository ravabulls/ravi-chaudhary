By now we know how to spot an opportunity and how to size up the customer value behind it. This chapter asks the more awkward question: once you have a promising idea, **do you actually need a strategy** — or should you just start building and see what sticks? The answer, from Gans, Scott and Stern's *"Strategy for Start-ups"*, is that a young venture faces a handful of **big, linked choices**, and thinking them through is what separates a focused start-up from an aimless one.

<Aside type="tip">
**The one idea to hold on to:** an entrepreneurial strategy is a **reasoned set of choices** about how to create and capture value under uncertainty. Gans–Scott–Stern boil it down to **four interdependent choices — Customer, Technology, Competition and Identity.** You cannot maximise all four at once; the art is making them **reinforce** each other.
</Aside>

## 1 · Do entrepreneurs even need a strategy?

There is a genuine tension here, and it is worth stating honestly before we resolve it.

On one side sits the **lean start-up** view: don't overthink it, get a rough product in front of customers, learn, and **pivot** (change direction) as you go. As Richard Branson put it — *"Screw it, just do it."* On the other side sits the worry that pure "seat of your pants" action leaves you exposed: you grab the **first** workable path you see, while a competitor quietly takes a less obvious but more powerful route to the same market.

The trap in the lean argument is subtle. Every experiment you run is also a **commitment** — it spends time, money and credibility, and it quietly closes off other paths. Building an "everything store" and building a "specialist bookshop" are not the same experiment; picking one shapes what you can plausibly do next. So "just experiment" is never free of strategy. The question is whether your commitments **add up to something coherent** or just scatter.

So the honest answer is **yes, but lightly.** A strategy here is not a 40-page business plan. It is a small set of deliberate choices that channel your experiments. And crucially, a start-up with a genuinely good idea should be able to imagine **more than one** viable way to win — if only one path exists, you probably don't have much of a business.

<Aside type="note">
**A commitment does not foreclose every future move — but it does shape them.** Choosing a path removes some future pivots and opens others. Amazon starting with books did not trap it in books; it gave it a **beachhead** (a first, defensible foothold) from which to expand into "everything". The point of strategy is to choose commitments whose future doors are worth having.
</Aside>

## 2 · The four choices, at a glance

Gans, Scott and Stern argue that almost every early venture must wrestle with **four domains of decision-making**. A start-up that has not thought through at least these four is unlikely to create and capture value in a lasting way.

The rest of the chapter walks through each choice, its central trade-off, and a worked example — then shows why the four cannot be pulled apart.

## 3 · Choice 1 — Customer: pick your beachhead

Identifying customers and their needs is usually the **first** step in any go-to-market plan. But there is a catch that trips up most founders: the **target customer is not necessarily the first customer.** You validate the whole idea by winning the *right* early adopters — a **beachhead** — and then expanding from there.

Amazon is the classic case. Jeff Bezos did not want to run a bookshop; he wanted the "everything store". But he **started** with book readers, because books were a beachhead from which he could later push into every other retail category. Choosing that first segment shaped everything — the technology he needed, the identity he built, the rivals he took on.

A useful warning from the customer session: **the beachhead you pick depends on the strategy you are playing.** They are not the same target:

- **Crossing the chasm:** choose hard-to-reach early adopters who are **highly influential** on the mainstream that follows.
- **Disruption:** choose **over-served or non-consuming** customers who are low-priority for the established players — so nobody big fights back yet.

<Aside type="note">
**Why "choose one" matters.** Most start-ups face *too many* plausible beachheads. Chasing several at once **dissipates effort and destroys coherence**, making it likely that every bet fails. The discipline is to test a couple cheaply, then make a limited-but-real commitment to one — to give your value-creation hypothesis a real chance.
</Aside>

## 4 · Choice 2 — Technology: how far to push it

The technology choice is tightly bound to the customer choice — the two are interrelated. Amazon *could* have built a simple ordering system for existing shops. Instead, because it wanted customers to buy the "long tail" of books that no physical shop could stock, it had to invest well beyond a shopping cart — into a database and search engine that could guide readers through millions of titles. The customer ambition **forced** the technology ambition.

The underlying tool here is the **technology S-curve**: a new technology improves slowly at first, then rapidly as key choices are locked in, then flattens as it hits physical limits. Once returns to effort start falling, it may be time to jump to a **new** S-curve. Start-ups often win precisely by betting on a young, flawed technology on a steep new curve that incumbents — wedded to the old curve — cannot easily follow.

        <li>Push the tech far; build a defensible, hard-to-copy position</li>
        <li>Patents, proprietary know-how, first-mover assets</li>
        <li>Aims at long-term <strong>value capture</strong></li>
        <li>Slower to market; higher up-front cost</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="sc-panel">
      <div class="head">Move fast with "good enough"</div>

        <li>Ship a workable product; iterate with customers</li>
        <li>Prioritise time-to-market, feedback and low cost</li>
        <li>Expects competition; relies on <strong>speed</strong> to stay ahead</li>
        <li>Less defensible on day one</li>

</div>

<Aside type="tip">
**The S-curve is partly a choice, not just a fact.** Whether a technology looks "disruptive" depends in part on the trajectory *you* choose to develop it along. A minimum viable product aimed at a niche can deliberately put you on a new, steep curve — the seed of a later disruption.
</Aside>

## 5 · Choice 3 — Competition: collaborate or compete?

Here is the choice founders most often get wrong by ignoring it. For the **same** idea, you can either **collaborate** with the established players — licence to them, partner, become a supplier, or get acquired — or **compete** against them head-on. These are genuinely different businesses with different risks.

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-split">
    <div class="sc-panel eff">
      <div class="head">Collaborate with incumbents</div>
      <ul>
        <li>Access to their resources, supply chain and customers</li>
        <li>Enter a big, established market <strong>faster</strong></li>
        <li>Mutes competitive pressure — you are not the enemy</li>
        <li><strong>Cost:</strong> incumbents hold the bargaining power</li>
        <li><strong>Cost:</strong> slow, bureaucratic; you capture a smaller slice</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="sc-panel rsp">
      <div class="head">Compete against incumbents</div>
      <ul>
        <li>Freedom to build the value chain you envision</li>
        <li>Serve customers the incumbents overlooked</li>
        <li>Independent bargaining power; capture more of the pie</li>
        <li><strong>Cost:</strong> you face richer, better-resourced rivals</li>
        <li><strong>Cost:</strong> you may wake a sleeping giant</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Two orientations toward the established players. Biotech start-ups mostly <em>collaborate</em> (licensing to Big Pharma); Netflix <em>competed</em> its way past Blockbuster. Same kind of question, opposite answers.</figcaption>
</div>

Sitting underneath this is a second, related tension the competition session calls **control versus execution** — *when* do you compete? Do you compete **for** the market today by locking up control (patents, proprietary networks, owned resources), or **in** the market tomorrow by executing faster and building capabilities (no heavy IP, open platforms, speed)?

There is a real reason control and execution trade off against each other — **Arrow's disclosure problem:** to sell or license an idea you often have to reveal it, but revealing it reduces what a buyer will pay for it. Protecting control therefore raises the cost and friction of every deal, while prioritising execution lowers those frictions but leaves less to defend.

| Dimension | Invest in **Control** | Invest in **Execution** |
|---|---|---|
| **What you build** | Patents, first-mover assets, proprietary networks | Superior product, lower cost, capabilities |
| **Time to market** | Slow | Fast |
| **Source of future returns** | Unique, protected **assets** | Unique **capabilities** and speed |
| **Founder's logic** | Long-term appropriability; protect the "vision" | Feedback, experimentation, cheap iteration |

## 6 · Choice 4 — Identity: the kind of company you choose to be

The last choice is the softest to describe and the easiest to underrate. **Identity** is how the venture represents itself — to its employees, to the market, and to the partners who hold complementary assets. It bundles together the **founder's purpose**, the **internal capabilities and culture** you build, the **external position** you stake out, and the **ecosystem** you plant yourself in.

Choices here do two jobs at once: they create a **narrative** about what the company stands for, and they **signal** to everyone what behaviour and capabilities to expect. Bezos framing Amazon as the low-price "everything store" (not a bookseller) told Wall Street, staff and suppliers exactly what to expect: relentless price-cutting, even at the expense of early profits.

<Aside type="note">
**Identity is a constraint you accept on purpose.** A disruptor's identity — young, hungry, lean, eager for the fight — attracts a certain kind of hire and investor and rules out the patient, deal-making, IP-lawyer culture of a licensing business. Choosing who you are is also choosing who you are **not**, which is exactly why it makes the other three choices easier.
</Aside>

## 7 · Why the four choices are interdependent

This is the heart of the framework. The four choices are **not** a checklist you fill in independently — each one **constrains** the others, so you cannot maximise all four at once. What you are really searching for is **coherence**: a set of choices that reinforce one another into a single, believable story.

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-flow col">
    <div class="sc-node amber">Choose to <strong>collaborate</strong> with an incumbent</div>
    <span class="arrow">↓</span>
    <div class="sc-node violet">…so you invest in <strong>generalisable, transferable</strong> technology that plugs into their systems</div>
    <span class="arrow">↓</span>
    <div class="sc-node slate">…which points you at <strong>the incumbent's existing customers</strong>, not a rebel niche</div>
    <span class="arrow">↓</span>
    <div class="sc-node violet">…so your <strong>identity</strong> becomes an "ideas factory" and trusted partner, not a hustling disruptor</div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>One choice cascades into the next. Flip the first domino to "compete" and every downstream box changes too — niche customers, a bespoke value chain, and a lean, hungry identity. That is interdependence in action.</figcaption>
</div>

The Amazon story shows the same coherence from the other side. The customer choice (book readers as a beachhead) demanded a particular technology choice (a search engine over millions of titles), which fitted a particular identity (the low-price everything store), which set up a particular competitive stance (compete with all of retail, not partner with booksellers). Pull any one choice out and the others stop making sense.

<Aside type="tip">
**Coherence beats optimisation.** Do not ask "what is the best possible customer / technology / competitor / identity in isolation?" Ask "which *combination* reinforces itself?" A merely-good set of choices that fit together beats four individually brilliant choices that pull in different directions.
</Aside>

## 8 · Where this is heading — the Strategy Compass

Here is the elegant payoff, and the bridge to the next chapter. Rather than optimise four dials separately, Gans, Scott and Stern show that the choices collapse onto **two make-or-break trade-offs**:

<div class="sc-chips">
  <span class="sc-chip amber">Collaborate or compete?</span>
  <span class="sc-chip violet">Build a moat (control) or storm a hill (execution)?</span>
</div>

Cross those two axes and you get a **2×2** — the **Entrepreneurial Strategy Compass** — with four **generic go-to-market strategies**. Each is a coherent bundle of the four choices, so picking a quadrant answers "which customers, which technology, which identity, which rivals" all at once.

## Revision summary

<Aside type="tip">
**The one-minute recap**

- **Yes, entrepreneurs need a strategy — a light one.** Every experiment is also a **commitment** that closes doors, so a strategy is a **reasoned set of choices** that keeps your experiments coherent, avoiding both paralysis and drift.
- The framework (Gans–Scott–Stern) is **four interdependent choices: Customer, Technology, Competition, Identity.**
- **Customer:** pick a **beachhead** — the first customer is not always the target customer; serving one means saying no to others (Amazon started with books).
- **Technology:** how far and fast to push it — **control/defensible** vs **execution/good-enough**; ride the **S-curve** and know when to jump to a new one.
- **Competition:** **collaborate** with incumbents (licence, partner, be acquired) or **compete** head-on — plus the **control vs execution** timing question, sharpened by **Arrow's disclosure problem**.
- **Identity:** the company you choose to be — purpose, culture, position, ecosystem — best revealed by **what you refuse to do**.
- **You cannot maximise all four at once.** They **constrain** each other; aim for **coherence**, not four separate optimisations.
- These choices collapse onto **two axes** (collaborate/compete × control/execution) → the **Strategy Compass** and its four generic strategies (next chapter).
</Aside>
