Everything up to now was about **deciding** a strategy - analysing the market, choosing a position, setting a price. This chapter is the hinge. Before you can go and **deliver** a strategy, you have to get someone to say *yes* to it: an investor, a board, a boss holding the budget. That "yes" almost never comes from a fat report nobody reads. It comes from a good **pitch**.

So this is the bridge chapter. It's less about *what* CERMEDES should do and more about *how you sell the answer* - how you compress a term's worth of analysis into fifteen minutes that actually land.

<Aside type="tip">
**The one idea to hold onto:** a strategy that can't be explained clearly can't be approved. Structure isn't decoration you add at the end - it's the thing that lets a busy listener follow you at all. Get the structure right and the persuasion mostly takes care of itself.
</Aside>

## 1 · What a pitch actually is

A **pitch** is a short, compelling presentation of an idea, product or business, told so that *any* listener grasps the concept quickly. Not a demo, not a full report - a tight case for one idea.

Three things define it:

- **It's short.** The whole point is brevity. If it takes an hour, it isn't a pitch.
- **It has a target.** You're trying to **win someone over** - an investor to fund you, an executive to greenlight your project. There is a specific decision you want.
- **The challenge is excitement, not completeness.** The hard part isn't fitting everything in; it's conveying the *overall concept* in a way that makes someone lean forward. You are choosing what to leave out.

### 1.1 The 15-minute rule

In this course every pitch has to happen in **15 minutes or less**. That constraint is the lesson, not an inconvenience.

A time box forces prioritisation. When you *can't* say everything, you're pushed to decide what's genuinely most interesting - and that decision is exactly the thinking a good pitch needs. It also sends a quiet message of respect: the audience's time is valuable, and a tight pitch treats it that way.

## 2 · What the audience wants to learn from you

Before you build anything, know what questions are running through the listener's head. They differ depending on *who* you are pitching as. A **start-up founder** is asking for investment in a new venture; a **consultant** is recommending a fix for an existing business. Same skill, different checklist.

| # | Start-up pitch (winning an investor) | Consulting pitch (winning an executive) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Who are you, and why are you here? | - |
| 2 | What problem are you solving? | What problem are you solving? |
| 3 | What's your product / how do you solve it? | What's your solution to the **business** problem? |
| 4 | What makes it special and superior? | - |
| 5 | What's your track record - the data? | What **analysis** results support your case - the data? |
| 6 | How big is the market potential? | What's the **implementation plan** for the project? |
| 7 | How will you make a profit? | What's the **business case** behind the proposal? |
| 8 | Who's on the team, and their roles? | - |
| 9 | How much capital do you need? | What **resources** do you need? |
| 10 | Why is this the best idea, and why *now*? | What's the cost of **doing nothing**? |

The two columns rhyme, but the emphasis shifts. A founder has to prove *the team and the upside are real* (who you are, why now, market size, profit). A consultant is talking to people who already run the business, so the burden moves to *rigour and consequence*: what does the analysis show, how will we actually execute it, and - the killer closing question - **what does it cost us if we do nothing?** That last one flips the risk. Instead of "why is my idea worth the risk," you make *inaction* the risky choice.

For the CERMEDES game, we're firmly in the **consulting** column: we're not inventing a start-up, we're recommending what an existing power-tool maker should do next.

## 3 · The storyline - a script for the listener

Here's the core mechanic of the whole chapter. A **storyline** is a *script book* that guides a listener closely through complex material. It's the sequential backbone of the presentation - the order of ideas plus the supporting arguments, data and examples that hang off each one.

Its real job is subtle: a good storyline gets the listener to *adopt your way of seeing the problem*. If they take on your structure and your way of rating what matters, then your argument almost carries itself, because they're now reasoning along the same rails you laid down.

<Aside type="note">
The **simplest** storyline is just a **table of contents** - a hierarchical list of headings, like the outline of a scientific paper. If you can write a clean, logical contents page, you already have a storyline skeleton. Everything below is about making that skeleton *persuasive*, not just orderly.
</Aside>

### 3.1 The basic arc: Interest → Desire → Action

Underneath every storyline is a three-beat emotional arc borrowed from classic persuasion:

  <div class="sc-flow">
    <div class="sc-node amber">Interest<span class="sub">draw attention</span></div>
    <span class="arrow">→</span>

The two templates that follow are just two concrete ways to run that arc - one built for **consulting**, one for **storytelling**.

### 3.2 Variant (a): Situation → Complication → Resolution (consulting)

This is the workhorse for a business recommendation. It maps neatly onto Interest → Desire → Action.

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-flow">
    <div class="sc-node amber">Situation<span class="sub">interest · we all agree</span></div>
    <span class="arrow">→</span>
    <div class="sc-node violet">Complication<span class="sub">desire · but something changed</span></div>
    <span class="arrow">→</span>
    <div class="sc-node green">Resolution<span class="sub">action · so here's what to do</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Open on solid, shared ground; disturb it with a change or problem; then resolve the tension with your recommendation.</figcaption>
</div>

| Beat | What it does | The trick to getting it right |
|---|---|---|
| **Situation** | A general opening statement about the subject the audience already knows and will **agree** with | Make it **self-sufficient** (needs no set-up) and **non-controversial** (they nod immediately). You're establishing common ground, not arguing yet. |
| **Complication** | The statement that raises the new question or problem - the "plot" of the pitch | It **disturbs** the stable situation: *something went wrong*, *something could change*, *here's a different view*, *here's something you hadn't considered*. This is what creates the need for an answer. |
| **Resolution** | The call to action - your proposal, your funding ask, whatever you want them to do | It **answers** the question the Complication raised, and is followed by **specific next steps** (e.g. a short project letter). |

**Worked example - a power-tool company** (close cousin of CERMEDES):

| Situation | Complication | Resolution |
|---|---|---|
| **Sales are declining - EBIT is falling.** 20% less net revenue Q1 year-on-year; a 50% crash in the value segment; the high-end segment stable; loyalty among professional customers fading. | **The portfolio has a structural problem.** The market is drifting away from the value segment; professionals are switching to low-cost, one-time-use tools; private customers are moving up to luxury high-end kit. | **Launch a major restructuring project.** Cut costs by phasing out the value segment; "skim the cream" with a luxury line (the "golden handle" bars); lift gross margin by moving the low-cost line to online sales. |

Notice the shape: nobody in the room can argue with "sales are down" (Situation). The tension comes from *why* (Complication - it's structural, not a blip). And the ask lands as the obvious release of that tension (Resolution). Three problems named → three matching solutions. That one-to-one fit between problems and answers is exactly what graders and executives look for.

### 3.3 Variant (b): Pain → Idea → Next steps (storytelling)

The second template is warmer and more emotional - better when you're rallying people behind a mission, not just fixing a P&L.

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-flow">
    <div class="sc-node amber">Pain<span class="sub">interest · a tangible problem</span></div>
    <span class="arrow">→</span>
    <div class="sc-node violet">Idea<span class="sub">desire · the big-picture solution</span></div>
    <span class="arrow">→</span>
    <div class="sc-node green">Next steps<span class="sub">action · one small ask</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Lead with a felt problem and real passion, paint the big-picture solution, then invite the audience to take one low-barrier step with you.</figcaption>
</div>

| Beat | What it does | The trick to getting it right |
|---|---|---|
| **Pain** | A tangible problem statement or short narrative | Keep it self-sufficient and non-controversial, and add **genuine personal passion** - *"it makes me sad that…"*, *"this made me reconsider…"*. Emotion is the hook. |
| **Idea** | A brief description of the solution to that pain | Develop a **value-based "big picture"**, and address the room directly with a rhetorical question: *"wouldn't it be great if…"* |
| **Next steps** | A **low-barrier** invitation to join the journey with one small step | Give crisp highlights of results so far, then a straightforward description of the next step and its outcome. Ask for something small and concrete, not the moon. |

**Worked example - the sepsis-alert app:**

| Pain | Idea | Next steps |
|---|---|---|
| 11 million people died of sepsis in 2017 - about 20% of all deaths globally, and the number-one cause of in-hospital death in the USA. Yet sepsis is **curable if caught early**. It's an emergency on the level of a heart attack or stroke - and it's the most widely underestimated disease, because clinics and local doctors so often miss a timely diagnosis. | Our goal: cut global sepsis deaths by **50% in five years**. Wouldn't it be great if every clinic worldwide could share expertise on preventing, diagnosing and curing sepsis? We built a **sepsis-alert app** that raises a warning right inside clinical health-record systems. Now is the moment to go from small-scale to wide field use. | Today we invite you to join the adventure. All we ask for now is a venture investment of **EUR 100,000**. With it we'll set up a network with the five major clinics in Hamburg to collect data and improve the app - and we offer you a fair early-bird share, today. |

Same arc, different temperature. The Pain grabs you emotionally, the Idea lifts to a vision ("wouldn't it be great if…"), and the ask is deliberately *small and specific* - a pilot in one city, not "fund our world domination." Low barrier, easy yes.

## 4 · Why structured communication works

Both templates lean on the same fact about human brains: **we can only hold about seven items in short-term memory at once** (Miller's classic finding). Beyond that, things start falling out.

So the brain copes by **hunting for patterns** - it bundles individual data bits into logical groups so it can handle the flow of information. As you sit through any presentation, a big chunk of your mental effort is spent *detecting the pattern* in the words and images coming at you.

<Aside type="note">
**The takeaway for a presenter:** if you *hand* the audience the pattern, you free up all that effort for actually understanding your point. If you don't, they burn their limited attention just trying to organise your chaos - and usually give up. Structure is a gift to the listener's working memory.
</Aside>

There's a well-known illustration of this. Imagine someone rambling: *in Zurich last week I saw loads of men with beards… and in any New York office you find moustaches and sideburns… and of course facial hair's been part of London for a decade…* - you have to work to spot what they're driving at. Compare that to leading with the point: **"facial hair has become an accepted part of business life"** - *in Zurich, in New York, and in London.* Same facts, but the second version states the pattern first and hangs three examples beneath it. That "point first, evidence grouped below" shape is the whole idea of the pyramid.

## 5 · Minto's Pyramid Principle

The **Pyramid Principle** (from Barbara Minto) is a storyline model that arranges every idea, hypothesis, argument and finding into a **hierarchy**.

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-tiers">
    <div class="sc-tier"><div class="sc-tier-head">Principal topic <span class="n">the single governing idea</span></div>the one thing this whole pitch is about</div>
    <div class="sc-tier"><div class="sc-tier-head">Key line <span class="n">a few key ideas</span></div>the handful of arguments that prove the topic</div>
    <div class="sc-tier"><div class="sc-tier-head">Supporting ideas <span class="n">grouped beneath each key idea</span></div>reasons, sub-points, logically ordered within each group</div>
    <div class="sc-tier"><div class="sc-tier-head">Detailed ideas <span class="n">the base</span></div>the data, charts and examples that back it all up</div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Key ideas at the top, detail at the bottom. Every level is a summary of the level right below it - so you can stop at any depth and still make sense.</figcaption>
</div>

The rules that make it work:

<Steps>

1. **Top-down.** Key ideas go at the top, detail at the bottom.
2. **Each level summarises the one below.** Any idea is a summary of the group of ideas directly beneath it - so a reader can stop early and still get the gist.
3. **Group only within a level.** Ideas that support the *same* key idea are grouped together; you only ever group ideas of the same level.
4. **Order within a group.** Ideas inside a group follow a logical order (by importance, by time, by structure - but *some* deliberate order).

</Steps>

Because each layer summarises the next, the pyramid hands the audience the **information pattern up front** - exactly what their pattern-hungry brain wants.

### 5.1 Two shapes of argument: pyramid vs queue

Minto contrasts two ways to structure a case, and it's worth knowing which you're using.

| | **Hierarchical pyramid** | **Sequential queue** |
|---|---|---|
| Logic | **Inductive** - group specifics into a conclusion | **Deductive** - one point leads step by step to the next |
| Home turf | **Business** | **Science** |
| Shape | Key message on top; several parallel arguments beneath, each with its own data and example | A chain: statement → because → therefore → conclusion |
| Feels like | "Here's my point, and here are three reasons it's true" | "Follow me through this proof and you'll reach the only possible conclusion" |

For a business pitch you almost always want the **pyramid** (inductive): say the answer, then support it. The **queue** (deductive) is for a scientific argument where each link genuinely depends on the previous one - powerful, but risky in a pitch, because if the audience loses one step they lose the whole chain.

### 5.2 From storyline to slides

Here's the practical payoff. A storyline written as an indented outline **expands directly into slides** - and the numbered lines become **full-sentence slide titles** that state the point.

Take the power-tool storyline as an outline:

> **The company must make structural changes to its portfolio.**
> 1. The portfolio no longer matches changing customer demand.
>    - 1.1 Professional customers are shifting from quality to low-cost…
>       - 1.1.1 …increasingly buying at DIY chains and online.
>    - 1.2 Private customers are shifting from the value segment to luxury.
> 2. The business is no longer profitable.
>    - 2.1 Sales down 20% in Q1.
>    - 2.2 Value-segment sales down 50%.

Each top-level line is a **claim written as a full sentence**, and it becomes a slide title. The sub-points become that slide's content - a chart, a couple of bullets, a segment picture. The reader can skim only the titles and still follow the entire argument, because the titles *are* the pyramid's key line. That's the test of a good deck: read the titles alone and the story holds.

## 6 · Presentation do's and don'ts

The storyline and pyramid give you the skeleton. These habits are the finish.

The through-line: a pitch is about **the listener**, not you. Lead with what they care about, make the data effortless to read, and rehearse until you can speak to any slide without notes.

## 7 · How the report is graded

The pitch is spoken; the graded deliverable in this course is a written **management report** (a deck of about 35 slides with headlines and management summaries). It's marked on four criteria - worth knowing, because they double as a **quality checklist** for the pitch too.

<div class="sc-fig">
  <table class="sc-matrix">
    <thead>
      <tr><th>Criterion</th><th>Weight</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th>Form &amp; Presentation</th><td class="hl">20%</td></tr>
      <tr><th>Thread &amp; Structure</th><td class="hl">20%</td></tr>
      <tr><th>Analytics</th><td class="hl">40%</td></tr>
      <tr><th>Recommendations &amp; Conclusions</th><td class="hl">20%</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>Analytics carries the most weight - the analysis is the substance - but the other three decide whether that substance actually lands.</figcaption>
</div>

| Criterion | What it rewards |
|---|---|
| **Form &amp; Presentation** (20%) | Appealing visuals, a concise message, efficient use of space and time. Does the problem you outline match the strategy you propose (three problem boxes → three solutions)? Is there an executive summary? |
| **Thread &amp; Structure** (20%) | A useful overall structure; **interpretation over mere description**; analysis steps that follow a logical process; headlines and intermediate conclusions along the way. Ideas should *fit together* - don't just dump company facts. |
| **Analytics** (40%) | The right analytical toolset (strategic analyses, market screening, channel evaluation) used **correctly**; going **beyond the obvious**; a genuine deep dive that customises the tools to the case. Back everything with numbers - market trends, financials, quantification. |
| **Recommendations &amp; Conclusions** (20%) | A **clear conclusion**, with a roadmap, timeline, priorities and financials. A five-step strategy is fine, but keep it simple and **prioritise** - don't drown the recommendation in steps. |

The pattern to read off this: **Analytics is 40%**, so rigour is where most marks live - but the other 60% is all about whether that rigour is *communicated* clearly and *concluded* decisively. Which is the whole point of this chapter: brilliant analysis that nobody can follow scores like mediocre analysis.

## Revision summary

<Aside type="tip">
**The one-minute recap**

- A **pitch** is a short, compelling case for one idea, aimed at winning a specific *yes* from an investor or executive. The **15-minute rule** is a feature: the clock forces you to keep only what matters and respects the room's time.
- Know what the audience is asking. A **start-up** pitch proves the team and the upside; a **consulting** pitch proves the analysis, the plan, and the **cost of doing nothing**. CERMEDES is the consulting case.
- A **storyline** is a script that guides the listener; the simplest form is a table of contents. It runs on **Interest → Desire → Action**, via one of two templates: **Situation → Complication → Resolution** (consulting) or **Pain → Idea → Next steps** (storytelling).
- Structure works because we hold only **about 7 items** in short-term memory and the brain craves patterns - so **hand it the pattern**.
- **Minto's Pyramid**: key idea on top, detail below, each level summarising the next; use the **inductive pyramid** for business, the **deductive queue** for science. An indented storyline expands straight into slides with **full-sentence titles**.
- **Do**: big picture first, full-sentence titles, visuals, audience benefit, consistency. **Don't**: bury the point, overload text, be generic, skip data, or wing it.
- The report is graded on **Form 20 / Structure 20 / Analytics 40 / Recommendations 20** - rigour earns the most, but only clear communication makes it count.
</Aside>
