Chapter 1 was about reading a situation. This one is about the choice that follows: **you cannot be everything to everyone**, so *who* exactly do you serve, and how do you make them feel your product is *for them*? The answer is a three-step flow the whole marketing world calls **STP** - Segment, Target, Position.

The single most useful warning in this chapter comes first, though. The most common - and often fatal - mistake companies make is to **rush into product development** before they've done any of this. They build the drill they think is clever, then go hunting for someone to sell it to. That's backwards. Understand the customer first; design second.

<Aside type="caution">
**The order matters.** Segment → Target → Position → *then* build. If CERMEDES (our mid-sized German power-tool maker, once a trusted brand, now struggling) skips straight to "let's add Bluetooth to the drill," it's guessing. The Bluetooth might be brilliant engineering that no target customer will pay one euro more for.
</Aside>

## 1 · The STP flow

Think of STP as a funnel that turns a big, messy, heterogeneous market into one sharp promise aimed at one well-understood group.

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-flow">
    <div class="sc-node amber">Segment<span class="sub">slice the market into groups</span></div>
    <span class="arrow">→</span>
    <div class="sc-node violet">Target<span class="sub">pick which group(s) to serve</span></div>
    <span class="arrow">→</span>
    <div class="sc-node green">Position<span class="sub">occupy a place in their mind</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>STP in one line: divide the market, choose your part of it, then plant a clear, defensible idea of your product in the chosen customers' heads.</figcaption>
</div>

Each step answers a different question:

| Step | The question | The output |
|---|---|---|
| **Segmentation** | How does this market naturally split into groups? | A set of distinct, meaningful customer segments |
| **Targeting** | Which segment(s) can we serve profitably and defensibly? | One or a few chosen segments |
| **Positioning** | How do we want our chosen customers to *think* of us? | A positioning statement + a product designed to back it up |

<Aside type="note">
**Segmentation, formally.** The old but still-perfect definition (Smith, 1956) is to view one heterogeneous market as *a number of smaller homogeneous markets*. Put plainly: split buyers into groups that are **maximally similar within** each group and **maximally different between** groups. If two "segments" behave the same, they're one segment.
</Aside>

## 2 · Developing a value proposition

Before you can position anything, you need to know what value you actually offer - and value is **not** a feature list. It's the answer to "what does this do for me?" Getting there is iterative, not a one-shot guess.

The healthy process looks like this: set your objectives, understand where you are and where you want to go, then genuinely get inside the customer's head - with **empathy and perspective-taking**, not assumptions. Crucially, you *validate* your hunches through real engagement (interviews, surveys, fieldwork) and treat your first value proposition as a **hypothesis to test and refine**, often over several iterations, before it fits.

### 2.1 The Value Proposition Canvas

The tool that makes this concrete is the **Value Proposition Canvas** (Osterwalder et al.). It has two halves that must *match*: the **customer profile** (a circle, describing the customer as they are) and the **value map** (a square, describing what you offer). You do the customer side first - honestly - and only then design the value map to fit it.

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-split">
    <div class="sc-panel">
      <div class="head">Customer profile <small>the circle - understand</small></div>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Customer Jobs</strong> - what the customer is trying to get done: tasks, problems to solve, wants and needs (functional, social, emotional).</li>
        <li><strong>Pains</strong> - the bad stuff: negative emotions, undesired costs, risks, obstacles before/during/after the job.</li>
        <li><strong>Gains</strong> - the good stuff they want: benefits, savings, positive outcomes - expected, desired, or delightful surprises.</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="sc-panel">
      <div class="head">Value map <small>the square - design</small></div>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Products &amp; Services</strong> - what you actually offer to help the job get done.</li>
        <li><strong>Pain Relievers</strong> - how your offer reduces or removes the customer's pains.</li>
        <li><strong>Gain Creators</strong> - how your offer produces the gains the customer hopes for.</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Fit is achieved when your Pain Relievers and Gain Creators actually address the customer's most important Pains and Gains for the job they're doing.</figcaption>
</div>

The whole logic collapses into one little equation worth memorising:

<div class="sc-formula">
  <div>
    <span class="name">Value</span>
    <code>Value = f(Pain Relief + Gain Creation)</code>
  </div>
  <div>
    <span class="name">Pain Relief</span>
    how much you remove of what the customer dreads
  </div>
  <div>
    <span class="name">Gain Creation</span>
    how much you add of what the customer wants
  </div>
</div>

<Aside type="tip">
You don't have to relieve *every* pain and create *every* gain - that way lies a bloated, expensive product. Fit comes from nailing the **few pains and gains that matter most** for the job. A good canvas is as much about what you deliberately ignore as what you deliver.
</Aside>

## 3 · Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD)

Here's the mindset shift that makes the canvas click. The classic line:

> *"People don't want to buy a quarter-inch drill - they want a quarter-inch hole."*

Nobody wants your product. They "hire" it to get a **job** done, and if something else does the job better, they'll fire you and hire that instead. The cosmetics version says it just as well: *"in the factory we make cosmetics; in the store we sell hope."* The drill is a means; the hole (or the shelf that's now hung, or the pride in a job done right) is the end.

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-flow">
    <div class="sc-node slate">The product<span class="sub">quarter-inch drill</span></div>
    <span class="arrow">→ hired to do →</span>
    <div class="sc-node amber">The functional job<span class="sub">make a quarter-inch hole</span></div>
    <span class="arrow">→ so that →</span>
    <div class="sc-node green">The real outcome<span class="sub">shelf up, room finished, feel capable</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>JTBD reframes competition around the job, not the product category. A picture-hanging strip that needs no hole is a competitor to a drill.</figcaption>
</div>

### 3.1 Finding needs through pains and gains

You uncover the job by interrogating the pains and gains directly. A useful set of prompts:

| Digging into **Pains** | Digging into **Gains** |
|---|---|
| What frustrates or annoys them? | What are they looking for most (design, guarantees, features)? |
| Which features are they missing today? | How do current products delight them? |
| What are the main difficulties they hit? | What would make their life easier (more service, lower cost)? |
| What social risks (status, trust) do they fear? | What positive social outcome do they want? |
| What financial or technical risks worry them? | What would make them more likely to adopt (less cost, less risk, better quality)? |
| What barriers (upfront cost, hassle) block adoption? | |

### 3.2 A worked customer profile

To make it tangible, here's the canonical example - a family choosing a cinema outing. Two different "jobs" (a date night vs. a kids' afternoon) produce very different profiles from the *same* activity, which is exactly why segmentation matters.

<div class="sc-fig">
  <table class="sc-matrix">
    <thead>
      <tr><th>For a "date night" job…</th><th>Jobs</th><th>Gains wanted</th><th>Pains felt</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th>What the couple wants</th><td>Get entertained; escape real life; enjoy a moment together</td><td>Feel included in the story; leads to great conversation after; an intimate setting</td><td>Can't get a babysitter; limited film options; too expensive; not intimate enough</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>The customer profile (circle) for a cinema "date night." Notice the pains aren't about the film at all - they're about babysitting, intimacy and choice.</figcaption>
</div>

Once you *see* those pains, the value map almost writes itself: **duo seats** and a **cocktail-bar space** (create intimacy), a **babysitting portal** (ease the babysitter pain), **small theatres** and **short run-times** (enable more options, more often). That's a value proposition built from the job outward - not from "we own a projector, let's sell tickets."

## 4 · Segmentation criteria - how to slice

So how do you actually cut the market? There's no single right cut; you pick criteria that produce groups that are genuinely different in what they *need* and how they *buy*. In business-to-business (B2B) markets, a well-known ladder runs from easy, surface-level variables to deep, hard-won ones - **least progressive to most progressive**.

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-tiers">
    <div class="sc-tier"><div class="sc-tier-head">Firmographics <span class="n">least progressive · easy to get</span></div>Industry · company location · company size. The "demographics" of a firm - public, obvious, but only a rough cut.</div>
    <div class="sc-tier"><div class="sc-tier-head">Operating variables <span class="n">middle</span></div>Their technology, how they use the product and brand, their capabilities. Tells you more about fit than firmographics do.</div>
    <div class="sc-tier"><div class="sc-tier-head">Purchasing approach <span class="n">most progressive · hardest to learn</span></div>How the purchasing function is organised, general buying policies, and the internal power structure - who really decides. Deepest insight, hardest to obtain.</div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>The B2B segmentation ladder. The deeper you go, the more useful (and the more expensive to discover) the segmentation becomes.</figcaption>
</div>

### 4.1 Need-based segmentation

The most powerful cut is usually by **need** - *why* people buy, not *who* they are. The same demographic can hide wildly different needs. A grocery example splits shoppers not by age or income but by what shopping *means* to them:

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-quad">
    <div class="q green"><div class="qh">The Quality Shopper <small>shopping = perfection</small></div><ul><li>High demand for healthy food and enjoyment; wants the best, not the cheapest.</li></ul></div>
    <div class="q blue"><div class="qh">The Food-Discoverer <small>shopping = inspiration</small></div><ul><li>Seeks novelty and experience; the trip itself is part of the reward.</li></ul></div>
    <div class="q amber"><div class="qh">The Smart-Shopper <small>shopping = hunt</small></div><ul><li>Enjoys the game of finding good value; deals are a win.</li></ul></div>
    <div class="q violet"><div class="qh">The Low-Cost / Efficient Sourcer <small>shopping = limitation / process</small></div><ul><li>Just wants it done: lowest cost or fastest process, no enjoyment sought.</li></ul></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Need-based segments of grocery shoppers. Two people of identical age and income can sit in opposite corners - which is precisely the point.</figcaption>
</div>

### 4.2 The persona

To keep a segment human (and to stop teams designing for a spreadsheet), we give each one a **persona** - a single, named, fictional character who embodies the segment. It turns "males 25-35, upper-middle income" into someone you can picture and argue about.

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-panel">
    <div class="head">Persona: "Jane Moviegoer"</div>
    <ul>
      <li>25-35 years old, upper-middle class (income around 60,000 USD)</li>
      <li>Married, two kids; prefers action movies</li>
      <li>Likes popcorn, beer and long drinks</li>
      <li>Buys tickets online; goes about once every two months</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <figcaption>A persona makes a segment concrete. When someone proposes a feature, you ask: "Would Jane care?" - and usually the answer is obvious.</figcaption>
</div>

## 5 · Targeting - choosing which segments to serve

Now you've got several segments. You can't chase all of them, so you filter. Each candidate segment faces a four-question gate - an easy mnemonic: **Differentiable, Substantial, Accessible, Actionable**. A "no" on any one knocks the segment out (or sends you back to find better segments).

<Steps>

1. **Differentiable?** - Is it genuinely distinct from other segments? (If not, you haven't found a real segment - merge it back.)
2. **Substantial?** - Is it large and profitable enough, today *and* in the future, to be worth serving?
3. **Accessible?** - Can you actually reach and serve it through channels you can use?
4. **Actionable?** - Do *we* have the resources and capabilities to serve it well?

</Steps>

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-flow">
    <div class="sc-node slate">A candidate segment</div>
    <span class="arrow">→</span>
    <div class="sc-node amber">Differentiable? Substantial? Accessible? Actionable?<span class="sub">all four = Yes</span></div>
    <span class="arrow">→</span>
    <div class="sc-node green">Keep it → score against rivals<span class="sub">any No → find new segments, restart</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>The targeting gate. Segments that clear all four questions go through to scoring; the rest are dropped or redefined.</figcaption>
</div>

### 5.1 Scoring the survivors

If more than one segment survives the gate, rank them. Build a simple weighted table: **your** criteria down the side, **your** segments across the top, and **your** scores (say 1-4) in the cells. There's no universal template - the discipline is choosing criteria that matter to *your* strategy and being honest with the numbers.

| Criterion (weight) | Segment A | Segment B | Segment C |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Size / profit potential | 4 | 2 | 3 |
| Growth outlook | 3 | 4 | 2 |
| Fit with our capabilities | 4 | 2 | 3 |
| Ease of access (channels) | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| **Weighted total** | **High** | **Medium** | **Medium** |

### 5.2 The customer-segment profile

For each segment you decide to target, write it up so the whole company shares one picture. A tidy template:

| Field | What to capture |
|---|---|
| **Segment size** | How many customers / how much spend is here today |
| **Segment growth** | Is it expanding or shrinking, and how fast |
| **Profitability** | Margins realistically achievable in this segment |
| **Needs and wants** | The jobs, pains and gains that define them |
| **Buying process** | Who's involved, how they decide, how long it takes |
| **Success factors** | What you must get right to win here |

<Aside type="note">
For CERMEDES, a plausible target is the **serious trade professional** - small building firms and craftspeople for whom a tool that fails on site costs a day's work. They're *differentiable* (very different needs from a weekend DIY buyer), *substantial*, *accessible* through trade channels, and *actionable* if CERMEDES leans on its real strength: German-engineered durability. That single choice already rules a lot of product ideas in and out.
</Aside>

## 6 · Positioning - occupying a place in the mind

Positioning is how customers *perceive* you relative to rivals: the spot your brand owns in their heads. It has two moving parts - the product you design, and the mental slot you aim for.

### 6.1 Core vs augmented product

Start with the layers of the product itself. The **core product** is the basic benefit that meets the need (a drill that makes holes). The **augmented product** wraps that core in the extras that actually create a satisfying, defensible bundle - brand, service, warranty, credit terms. Rivals can copy your core quite easily; the augmentation is often where the durable difference lives.

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-tiers">
    <div class="sc-tier"><div class="sc-tier-head">Core product <span class="n">the basic benefit</span></div>The bundle of benefits that satisfies the core customer need - the drill that reliably makes the hole.</div>
    <div class="sc-tier"><div class="sc-tier-head">Augmented product <span class="n">the defensible wrapper</span></div>Brand · services · warranties · credit possibilities · support - the extras that shape the whole experience and shield you from copycats.</div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Design the core to meet the need; augment it to win the segment and hold off competitors.</figcaption>
</div>

### 6.2 Brand architecture

When you serve several segments, you face a portfolio question: how many **brands**, and how are they related? This is **brand architecture**, and it's a spectrum from one master brand over everything to a stable of independent brands.

<div class="sc-fig">
  <table class="sc-matrix">
    <thead>
      <tr><th>Approach</th><th>Idea</th><th>Everyday example feel</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th>Branded house</th><td>One master brand stretched across all products</td><td>Google → Google Maps, Google Drive</td></tr>
      <tr><th>Sub-branding</th><td>Master brand + a distinct product sub-brand</td><td>Apple → iPhone, iPad</td></tr>
      <tr><th>Endorsement branding</th><td>Product brand leads, master brand endorses/backs it</td><td>"Courtyard by Marriott"</td></tr>
      <tr><th>House of brands</th><td>Many separate brands, parent stays hidden</td><td>P&amp;G → Ariel, Gillette, Pampers</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>Left to right, the parent brand's presence fades and each product stands more on its own.</figcaption>
</div>

The trade-off running underneath is **transfer effects vs cannibalisation**. Sharing one brand across segments spreads good reputation cheaply (a positive *transfer*), but risks two things: **cannibalisation** (a new product steals sales from your own existing one instead of from rivals) and *negative* transfer (a stumble in one product stains the shared name). A **branded house** maximises transfer but also maximises both risks; a **house of brands** firewalls each product but forfeits the free reputation and costs far more to build.

<Aside type="tip">
**The CERMEDES read.** A branded house is tempting - put "CERMEDES" on everything and let the trusted name do the work. But if the firm wants to chase cheap DIY buyers *and* keep its premium trade credibility, a cut-price line under the master brand risks negative transfer (looking cheap) and cannibalising the professional range. A distinct sub-brand or a separate value brand may protect the flagship. That's a real brand-architecture decision, not a logo choice.
</Aside>

## 7 · The positioning statement

Finally, you write the position down. The **positioning statement** is an *internal* document - deliberately plain, even dull. It is **not** a slogan or an ad; its job is to keep everyone inside the company aligned on what the product stands for, so all the outward communication pulls in one direction. The template:

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-panel">
    <div class="head">The template</div>
    <ul>
      <li>"Among <strong>[target market]</strong>, <strong>[X]</strong> is the <strong>[frame of reference]</strong> that <strong>[point of difference]</strong> because <strong>[reason to believe]</strong>."</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Four blanks, four hard strategic decisions. Filling them in is far harder than it looks.</figcaption>
</div>

The textbook example makes it stick:

> *"Among snackers, **Snickers** is the candy bar that satisfies your hunger because it's packed with nuts."*

Each blank is a real choice:

| Blank | What it is | Snickers |
|---|---|---|
| **Target market** | Who's in your customer's head - decided *before* you write; you position in *their* mind, not the whole world's | Snackers (not "everyone," not just "chocolate lovers") |
| **Frame of reference** | The category or need you compete in - set it as large as is reasonable | Candy bar (could be framed as "a snack" or even "a small meal") |
| **Point of difference (POD)** | The single most compelling, *unique* reason they'd pick you - your promise, the essence of the value prop | Satisfies your hunger |
| **Reason to believe (RTB)** | Credible evidence, inherent in the product/company, that you'll deliver the POD | It's packed with nuts |

### 7.1 The six criteria of good positioning

A statement can fill the blanks and still be weak. Test it against six criteria - all six should pass:

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-quad">
    <div class="q green"><div class="qh">1 · Relevance <small>do consumers care?</small></div><ul><li>The POD must matter to the target, not just to you.</li></ul></div>
    <div class="q blue"><div class="qh">2 · Uniqueness <small>meaningful differentiation?</small></div><ul><li>Rivals can't credibly claim the same thing.</li></ul></div>
    <div class="q amber"><div class="qh">3 · Clarity <small>will they get it?</small></div><ul><li>Instantly understood, no decoding required.</li></ul></div>
    <div class="q violet"><div class="qh">4 · Credibility <small>will they believe it?</small></div><ul><li>The RTB makes the promise convincing.</li></ul></div>
    <div class="q green"><div class="qh">5 · Attainability <small>can we deliver?</small></div><ul><li>We can actually make good on it today.</li></ul></div>
    <div class="q blue"><div class="qh">6 · Sustainability <small>can we maintain it?</small></div><ul><li>We can defend it over time against imitation.</li></ul></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Relevance and uniqueness earn attention; clarity and credibility get believed; attainability and sustainability keep it true. Fail any one and the position leaks.</figcaption>
</div>

### 7.2 Bringing it home for CERMEDES

Pulling the chapter together, a first-draft positioning statement for our recovering tool-maker might read:

<div class="sc-chips">
  <span class="sc-chip green">target: serious trade professionals</span>
  <span class="sc-chip amber">frame: power tools for the job site</span>
  <span class="sc-chip violet">POD: won't quit on you</span>
  <span class="sc-chip soft">RTB: German engineering, tested to survive daily site abuse</span>
</div>

> *"Among trade professionals, CERMEDES is the power-tool brand that keeps working when cheaper tools fail, because every tool is German-engineered and site-tested to survive daily abuse."*

Run it through the six criteria before trusting it: relevant (downtime hurts them), unique (if rivals can't match the durability story), clear, credible (heritage and testing back it), attainable (only if the tools genuinely last), sustainable (only if quality is defended). Notice how positioning loops straight back to the JTBD job - *keep my work moving* - and to the value map - *relieve the pain of a tool dying mid-job*. STP is one connected argument, not four separate slides.

## Revision summary

<Aside type="tip">
**The one-minute recap**

- **STP** = Segment → Target → Position. Do it in that order; the classic fatal mistake is rushing into **product development** before understanding the customer.
- A **value proposition** is a hypothesis to test and iterate. The **Value Proposition Canvas** matches a *customer profile* (Jobs, Pains, Gains) to a *value map* (Products/Services, Pain Relievers, Gain Creators). **Value = f(Pain Relief + Gain Creation)**.
- **JTBD**: people hire products to get a job done - "not a drill, a quarter-inch hole." Find needs by digging into pains and gains.
- **Segment** on criteria that create real difference - in B2B, the ladder runs firmographics → operating variables → purchasing approach (least → most progressive). **Need-based** cuts are strongest; make each segment human with a **persona**.
- **Target** by gating segments on **Differentiable, Substantial, Accessible, Actionable**, then score the survivors and write a customer-segment profile (size, growth, profitability, needs, buying process, success factors).
- **Position** using **core vs augmented product** and a **brand-architecture** choice (branded house → sub-branding → endorsement → house of brands), weighing **transfer effects vs cannibalisation**.
- Write the **positioning statement**: "Among [target], [X] is the [frame of reference] that [point of difference] because [reason to believe]." Then test it on six criteria: **relevance, uniqueness, clarity, credibility, attainability, sustainability**.
</Aside>
