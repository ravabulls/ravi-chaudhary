Before any supply-and-demand diagrams, this first chapter installs the *mindset*. Economics is often mistaken for "the study of money and the stock market". It's much broader than that: it's really the **study of how people make choices under scarcity**. Once you see it that way, the same toolkit explains prices, crime, marriage, pollution and politics.

<Aside type="tip">
**The whole discipline in one line:** people **want things**, resources are **scarce**, so every choice means **giving something up** - and people respond to **incentives**. Change the costs and benefits of an option, and you change what people do. That's the lever economics (and law) pulls.
</Aside>

## 1 · What economics is (and its two halves)

Economics splits into two big branches:

| Branch | Studies | Example questions |
|---|---|---|
| **Microeconomics** | How individual "economic units" (people, firms) make decisions | How does a monopoly set its output? How is a wage determined? How do criminals respond to bigger penalties? |
| **Macroeconomics** | The economy as a whole | GDP, inflation, unemployment, international trade |

This course lives almost entirely in **microeconomics** - the decisions of individuals and firms - because that's the level at which law changes behaviour.

## 2 · The rational actor: "homo economicus"

Traditional (neoclassical) economics models people as a **homo economicus** - a deliberately simplified decision-maker. It's not a claim that people *are* like this, but a useful baseline for prediction.

A homo economicus:

- is **self-interested** - does what's best for *themselves*;
- **ranks** all the alternatives and can assign a **utility** (a "value") to each;
- **maximises** that utility, given the **constraints** they face.

<Aside type="note">
**Preferences vs constraints - the key split.** Economics usually assumes **preferences are fixed** ("Roee hates spoilers, always will"), but **constraints can be changed**. A tax, a subsidy, or a fine doesn't change what you *want* - it changes the *cost* of an option, which shifts what you *choose*. **This is exactly the hinge on which law operates.**
</Aside>

## 3 · Where the "Law" comes in: Law & Economics

The course is *Economics & Law*, and the bridge between them is a field called **Law & Economics (L&E)** - "the economic analysis of law". Its core move: treat a legal rule as something that changes people's **costs and benefits**, then predict how behaviour changes and ask whether the result is good.

Two lenses run through all of L&E:

<div class="sc-split">
  <div class="sc-panel">
    <div class="head">Positive <small>what IS</small></div>
    <ul>
      <li>Describes and predicts: <em>how will people behave if the law says X?</em></li>
      <li>Factual, testable - no value judgement</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="sc-panel rsp">
    <div class="head">Normative <small>what SHOULD be</small></div>
    <ul>
      <li>Evaluates and recommends: <em>what should the law be?</em></li>
      <li>Needs a goal - usually efficiency (welfare)</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

L&E asks three questions of any legal rule:

| Question | What it's about |
|---|---|
| **Behavioural prediction** | How will people behave if the law says "X"? |
| **Efficiency** | Which rules make the "pie" of total welfare bigger? |
| **Distribution** | How is wealth split between individuals as a result? |

<Aside type="note">
**Lawyer vs economist.** A lawyer asks *what the rule means and whether it's just*; an economist asks *what the rule does to behaviour, and whether the outcome is efficient*. Classic example: should a barber be allowed to "hum"? A lawyer debates the wording; an economist notes that *banning* humming may just push barbers to play loud music instead - worse for customers - so allowing it is better. Same problem, different question.
</Aside>

## 4 · A first worked model: the economics of crime

To see the mindset in action, here's a famous L&E model (Becker, 1968). A would-be criminal weighs the **benefit** of a crime against its **expected cost** - the punishment, discounted by the chance of actually being caught and convicted:

<div class="sc-formula">
  <div>
    <span class="name">Commit the crime if…</span>
    <code>B &gt; P × S</code>
  </div>
  <div>
    <span class="name">B</span>
    the benefit from the crime (e.g. the loot)
  </div>
  <div>
    <span class="name">P</span>
    the probability of being caught and convicted
  </div>
  <div>
    <span class="name">S</span>
    the size of the sanction (e.g. years in prison)
  </div>
</div>

The insight: to **deter** crime you can raise **P** (more policing) *or* **S** (harsher sentences). It also explains why a wealthy fine-dodger and a desperate thief respond differently - their **B** differs. This one inequality is the whole "economic way of thinking" in miniature: name the costs and benefits, then predict the choice.

## 5 · One market or the whole economy? Partial vs general equilibrium

A crucial distinction for the rest of the course:

- **Partial equilibrium** looks at **one market in isolation** - e.g. just the market for coffee, holding everything else fixed.
- **General equilibrium** looks at **many markets and how they feed back on each other**.

Why it matters: a change in one market ripples into others. A positive shock to product demand makes firms hire more workers → wages rise → production costs rise → product supply falls. Sometimes that **feedback effect** is small (partial analysis is fine); sometimes it dominates and you *must* think in general equilibrium.

<Aside type="caution">
**The crime example, continued.** In *partial* equilibrium, raising the penalty **S** for a crime always deters it. But in *general* equilibrium, judges - afraid of sending an innocent person away for a very long time - may **convict less often**, lowering **P**. The feedback can partly (or fully) undo the intended effect. Ignoring other markets can give you exactly the wrong answer.
</Aside>

## Revision summary

<Aside type="tip">
**The one-minute recap**

- Economics is the **study of choice under scarcity**; every choice has an **opportunity cost** (what you gave up).
- The **homo economicus** is self-interested, ranks options by **utility**, and maximises it subject to **constraints**. Preferences are assumed fixed; **constraints (incentives) are what law changes**.
- **Law & Economics** treats legal rules as incentives and asks three questions: **behavioural prediction, efficiency, distribution** - through a **positive** (what is) or **normative** (what should be) lens.
- The **Becker crime model** (`B > P × S`) shows the mindset: name the costs and benefits, predict the choice - and note you can deter by raising the probability *or* the severity of punishment.
- **Partial** equilibrium studies one market; **general** equilibrium adds the **feedback effects** between markets, which sometimes reverse the naive conclusion.
</Aside>
