Chapter 1 defined sustainability and chapter 2 gave us the strategies for decoupling. This chapter is where those ideas meet the day job of most technology managers: **innovation**. The uncomfortable headline first - *not every innovation is sustainable*. A faster phone, a cheaper plastic bottle, a slicker app can all be genuine innovations and still make the world's environmental and social ledger worse. So the question here is precise: what has to be true for an innovation to count as **sustainable**, and how does that change the way we run the innovation process?

<Aside type="tip">
**The definition to hold on to.** A **sustainable innovation** renews or improves products, services, or technological / organisational processes so that it delivers not only better **economic** performance but also better **environmental** and **social** performance - in the short term *and* the long term (Bos-Brouwers, 2010). The load-bearing words are *not only*: it must move all three dimensions, not trade one off against the others.
</Aside>

## 1 · What makes an innovation "sustainable"

An **innovation** is simply a renewal or improvement that reaches the world - a new product, service, process, or way of organising. The word "sustainable" adds a hard test on top: the improvement has to register on **all three** dimensions of the triple bottom line (economy, environment, society) that we met in chapter 1. Miss one and it is just an innovation.

Two further points from Bos-Brouwers (2010) are worth pinning down:

- **It is the integration that sets it apart.** The thing that distinguishes a sustainable innovation from a conventional one is precisely that it weaves economic, social and environmental aspects *together*. This is why "not every innovation is sustainable" is not a slogan but a definition - the label is earned only by the ones that integrate all three.
- **They tend to be radical, not incremental.** Sustainable innovations are usually **radical or transformational** by nature (Rycroft and Kash, 2000) - they change the system rather than tweak a product. An **incremental** innovation is a small step improving what already exists (a slightly more efficient engine); a **radical** one rethinks the whole approach (rethinking mobility so the car trip is not needed). Real sustainability gains usually need the second kind, because the deep footprint is baked into the system, not the last five per cent of efficiency.

A quick illustration of the incremental / radical gap on the same everyday need - getting from A to B:

| | Incremental innovation | Radical / transformational innovation |
|---|---|---|
| **Move** | Make the petrol engine 5 per cent more efficient | Rethink mobility so the trip is shared, electric, or unnecessary |
| **Footprint effect** | Trims the last slice of impact | Changes the system that creates the impact |
| **Typical result** | Real but small; often eaten by rebound | Large, but riskier and slower to land |

<Aside type="note">
**Why "radical" matters here.** Chapter 2's efficiency strategy (do the same with less) is incremental by temperament and runs into rebound effects. Consistency and sufficiency - the deeper strategies - usually demand *radical* innovation: new materials, new business models, new patterns of use. So this "radical by nature" point is the innovation-side echo of the strategy discussion.
</Aside>

## 2 · Conventional vs sustainable innovation

If sustainable innovation were just "normal innovation, but greener", it would be easy. It is not - the economics and the cast of characters both change. The clearest map of the differences comes from **Weissbrod (2019)**.

<div class="sc-split">
  <div class="sc-panel eff">
    <div class="head">Conventional innovation</div>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Need is private / corporate</strong> - a customer wants it, or the firm wants growth.</li>
      <li>Players: the <strong>private-</strong> and <strong>corporate-problem holder</strong>, plus knowledge and infrastructure holders.</li>
      <li><strong>Single externality</strong> - spillovers appear once, in the innovation phase.</li>
      <li>Classic challenges: funding, innovation culture, tax &amp; regulation, skills, coordinated support, reading future demand.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="sc-panel rsp">
    <div class="head">Sustainable innovation</div>
    <ul>
      <li>Need also includes a <strong>public</strong> one - an environmental or social problem, not just a private want.</li>
      <li>Adds a new actor: the <strong>public-problem holder</strong> (society, regulators, future generations) on top of all the conventional players.</li>
      <li><strong>Double externality</strong> - spillovers in <em>both</em> the innovation <em>and</em> the diffusion phase.</li>
      <li>All the conventional challenges <em>plus</em> directional risk, defining success, user-vs-system design, and governance for collaboration.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

### The four differences, side by side

| Dimension | Conventional innovation | Sustainable innovation |
|---|---|---|
| **Nature of the need** | Private and corporate - someone will pay | Private and corporate **plus public** - environmental and social needs count |
| **Who is in the room** | Private- and corporate-problem holders, knowledge &amp; infrastructure holders | All of those **plus a public-problem holder** |
| **Externality problem** | **Single** externality (innovation phase only) | **Double** externality (innovation *and* diffusion phase) |
| **Extra challenges** | Funding, culture, regulation, skills, demand forecasting | The conventional set **plus** directional risk, success definition, user-vs-system duality, governance for collaboration |

### The double-externality problem - why the incentive breaks

An **externality** is a cost or benefit that lands on someone *outside* the transaction - the innovator does the work, but part of the value (or harm) spills over to others who did not pay. A **positive externality** means the innovator cannot capture all the value they create, which quietly weakens their reason to invest in the first place.

  <div class="sc-flow">
    <div class="sc-node soft">Conventional<span class="sub">one leak of value</span></div>
    <div class="arrow">→</div>
    <div class="sc-node slate">Innovation phase<span class="sub">R&amp;D spillovers to rivals</span></div>
    <div class="sc-bnd">diffusion adds<br/>nothing extra</div>
  </div>
  <div class="sc-flow">
    <div class="sc-node soft">Sustainable<span class="sub">two leaks of value</span></div>
    <div class="arrow">→</div>
    <div class="sc-node violet">Innovation phase<span class="sub">knowledge spillovers</span></div>
    <div class="arrow">+</div>
    <div class="sc-node green">Diffusion phase<span class="sub">clean-air / social benefit to all</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Conventional innovation leaks value once (rivals learn from your R&amp;D). Sustainable innovation leaks twice - again when the product spreads and its environmental / social benefit flows to everyone, paid for by no one.</figcaption>
</div>

Here is the mechanism in plain terms. In **conventional** innovation there is one positive externality: while you invent, some of your new knowledge leaks to competitors (the *innovation-phase* externality). That already dampens investment, which is why patents and subsidies exist.

**Sustainable** innovation carries a *second* one. When the clean product actually **diffuses** - spreads into use - its environmental and social benefits (cleaner air, better health, lower emissions) flow to society at large, not to the firm's bottom line. Nobody writes the innovator a cheque for the cleaner air. So the private incentive to invest is weakened **twice**: once in the lab and again in the market. This is the core reason sustainable innovation tends to be *under-supplied* by markets alone, and why the public-problem holder - regulation, procurement, standards, public funding - has to step in to close the gap.

<Aside type="caution">
**Don't confuse the two externalities.** The *innovation-phase* one is about **knowledge** spilling to rivals (true for any R&amp;D). The *diffusion-phase* one is about the **public benefit** of the product in use spilling to society. Sustainable innovation is special because it has both - that is literally what "double externality" names.
</Aside>

### The new challenges that come with it

Beyond the money problem, four fresh management headaches show up:

<div class="sc-chips">
  <span class="sc-chip violet">Directional risk - is this even the right target?</span>
  <span class="sc-chip">Defining &amp; evaluating success</span>
  <span class="sc-chip amber">User vs. system design duality</span>
  <span class="sc-chip green">Governance for collaboration</span>
</div>

- **Directional risk** - with conventional innovation the market tells you if you aimed right (people buy it or they don't). With sustainability the "right direction" is contested and long-term, so you can invest heavily and still be pointed at the wrong problem.
- **Defining and evaluating success** - profit is one number; environmental and social value are many, and slow to show up. What counts as "a win" is genuinely harder to state and measure.
- **User-vs-system design duality** - you have to design for the individual **user** (a car someone wants to drive) *and* for the **system** (a mobility system that works if millions switch). Optimising one can wreck the other.
- **Governance for collaboration** - because a public-problem holder is now in the room, you need ways to steer partnerships across firms, government and civil society - messier than a single-company project plan.

## 3 · Sustainable innovation strategy - the Pentathlon lens

So what has to change *strategically* to keep producing these innovations? A useful frame is the **Innovation Pentathlon** (Goffin, Herstatt &amp; Mitchell) - a picture of the whole innovation system as three activities standing on two foundations.

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-flow">
    <div class="sc-node slate">Ideas<span class="sub">generate options</span></div>
    <div class="arrow">→</div>
    <div class="sc-node slate">Selection<span class="sub">choose the right few</span></div>
    <div class="arrow">→</div>
    <div class="sc-node slate">Implementation<span class="sub">turn them into reality</span></div>
  </div>
  <div class="sc-flow">
    <div class="sc-node">Foundation · Innovation Strategy<span class="sub">targets, roadmaps, metrics, partnerships</span></div>
    <div class="sc-node">Foundation · People, Culture &amp; Organisation<span class="sub">skills, mindset, structures</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>The Innovation Pentathlon: three activities - Ideas, Selection, Implementation - resting on two foundations, Innovation Strategy and People / Culture / Organisation.</figcaption>
</div>

The Pentathlon's own question is exactly the exam question here: **what do you change in your innovation strategy so that sustainable innovation actually comes out of it?** Sustainability is not a fourth box you bolt on - it has to be threaded through the foundation so it shapes every activity above it:

| Strategy element | Conventional emphasis | Sustainable shift |
|---|---|---|
| **Targets** | Revenue, market share, time-to-market | Add explicit environmental &amp; social targets (emissions, circularity, fair supply) |
| **Metrics** | Financial KPIs, sales | Life-cycle footprint, resource intensity, social impact - measured, not assumed |
| **Roadmaps** | Feature and platform plans | Plans that reach *past launch* into use, maintenance and end-of-life |
| **Partnerships** | Suppliers, channel, tech partners | Add the **public-problem holder** - regulators, NGOs, cities - to share the double externality |
| **Selection criteria** | Business case, risk-reward | Business case screened *through* the three dimensions, not around them |

The through-line: because the private incentive is weakened by the double externality, sustainable strategy leans hard on **partnerships and shared targets** to make the numbers work - you deliberately bring in the actors who benefit from the public value so someone actually pays for it.

## 4 · Sustainability in the innovation process - extending Stage-Gate

Strategy sets direction; the **process** is how a firm walks an idea from "wouldn't it be nice" to "on sale". The standard reference is **Cooper's Stage-Gate model** (1980s, Robert G. Cooper) - a blueprint that breaks new-product development into a fixed set of **stages** (cross-functional work) separated by **gates** (Go / Kill decision meetings that act as quality checkpoints).

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-flow">
    <div class="sc-node slate">Discovery<span class="sub">idea generation</span></div>
    <div class="arrow">→</div>
    <div class="sc-node slate">Stage 1 · Scoping<span class="sub">quick assessment</span></div>
    <div class="arrow">→</div>
    <div class="sc-node slate">Stage 2 · Business Case<span class="sub">is it worth building?</span></div>
    <div class="arrow">→</div>
    <div class="sc-node slate">Stage 3 · Development<span class="sub">build it</span></div>
    <div class="arrow">→</div>
    <div class="sc-node slate">Stage 4 · Testing<span class="sub">validate</span></div>
    <div class="arrow">→</div>
    <div class="sc-node slate">Stage 5 · Launch<span class="sub">to market</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Classic Stage-Gate: five stages from Discovery to Launch, with a Go/Kill gate before each stage and a post-launch review at the end. It is efficient - but it stops the moment the product ships.</figcaption>
</div>

And that stopping point is the whole problem. Classic Stage-Gate ends at **Launch** - the product's actual environmental and social life (years of use, then disposal) happens *after* the process has closed the file. To make it sustainable you do two things: **extend the pipeline past launch**, and **pull life-cycle thinking into the front end**.

### Extend the pipeline: add life-cycle stages after launch

The sustainable version (building on Cooper, 2014 and Flores et al., 2018) keeps the five stages and adds two more that follow the product into the real world:

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-flow">
    <div class="sc-node slate">Discovery<span class="sub">idea</span></div>
    <div class="arrow">→</div>
    <div class="sc-node slate">1 · Scoping</div>
    <div class="arrow">→</div>
    <div class="sc-node slate">2 · Business Case</div>
    <div class="arrow">→</div>
    <div class="sc-node slate">3 · Development</div>
    <div class="arrow">→</div>
    <div class="sc-node slate">4 · Testing</div>
    <div class="arrow">→</div>
    <div class="sc-node slate">5 · Launch</div>
    <div class="sc-bnd">life cycle<br/>continues</div>
    <div class="sc-node green">6 · Usage &amp; Maintenance<span class="sub">product in use, after-sales service</span></div>
    <div class="arrow">→</div>
    <div class="sc-node green">7 · Retrieve &amp; apply the R principles<span class="sub">reduce · reuse · recycle</span></div>
    <div class="arrow">↺</div>
    <div class="sc-node violet">back to Discovery<span class="sub">loop closed</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Sustainable Stage-Gate: the five classic stages plus Stage 6 (Usage &amp; Maintenance) and Stage 7 (Retrieve the product and apply the R principles). Stage 7 feeds materials and lessons back to Discovery - closing the loop instead of ending at the landfill.</figcaption>
</div>

- **Stage 6 - Usage &amp; Maintenance.** The years the product spends *in use* (its "product life") are where most of its footprint is actually spent - energy, consumables, repairs. Designing for this stage means durability, repairability, and after-sales service that extends life rather than pushing replacement.
- **Stage 7 - Retrieve the product and apply the "R" principles.** At end of life you don't just dispose of it - you **retrieve** it and run the R principles: **reduce, reuse, recycle** (and their cousins, repair, refurbish, recover). The retrieved materials and lessons feed back into Discovery, which is what turns a straight line into a **loop**.

The **R principles** in Stage 7 are usually ranked - the earlier ones keep more value in the loop, so a good design tries them in order before falling back to the next:

| R principle | What it means | Value kept |
|---|---|---|
| **Reduce** | Need less material / energy in the first place | Highest - the impact never happens |
| **Reuse** | Use the same product again, as-is | Very high - no reprocessing |
| **Repair / Refurbish** | Fix or restore so it keeps working | High - extends the product's life |
| **Recycle** | Recover the raw materials for new products | Moderate - material saved, product lost |

That loop is the process-level expression of chapter 2's **eco-effectiveness / cradle-to-cradle** idea: waste from one cycle becomes a nutrient for the next, so the design aims to *close* the material loop rather than merely slow the leak.

### Pull life-cycle thinking into the front end

<Aside type="note">
**Why the front end matters most.** By the time you reach Development, most of a product's whole-life impact is already **locked in** by early design choices - the material, the architecture, the energy source. Deciding to design for the whole life cycle at Stage 1-2, not Stage 6, is what makes the extension real. Sustainability added late is a coat of paint; added at the front end it changes what gets built.
</Aside>

So the gates change too. In a sustainable process, each **Go / Kill** decision is screened not only on "will it sell and make money?" but on "does it hold up across all seven stages and all three dimensions?". A project that looks great to launch but has no answer for Stage 7 should stall at the gate - that is the process doing its job.

## 5 · Seeing it whole - a brief real-world example

It helps to see the three dimensions integrated in one company rather than as a checklist. **Toyota** is a useful case (we study it in full separately, so just the shape of it here). Facing falling sales and the question of what *sustainable growth* even means, Toyota reached past a quick financial fix and adopted a **"tree-ring" (Nenrin) philosophy** - grow slowly and steadily, adding a ring at a time, like a tree, rather than sprinting for one big season. Read against this chapter, that is a firm choosing the **long-term** half of the definition and treating growth as something to pace across economic, environmental and social health together, not maximise on one axis. **Nike** tells a similar story from the product side - redesigning materials and manufacturing so that environmental and social gains are engineered into the product, not apologised for afterwards.

The common thread - and the honest limit - is that no organisation is ever "completely sustainable". Sustainable innovation is a **direction of travel**: integrating all three dimensions, a bit more radically and a bit further across the life cycle, each ring.

## Revision summary

<Aside type="tip">
**The one-minute recap**

- **Sustainable innovation** (Bos-Brouwers, 2010) improves products / services / processes so they deliver better **economic *and* environmental *and* social** performance, short and long term - *not every innovation is sustainable*, and the sustainable ones tend to be **radical**, not incremental.
- **Conventional vs sustainable** (Weissbrod): sustainable innovation adds a **public need**, a **public-problem holder**, and the **double-externality problem** - positive spillovers in *both* the innovation and the diffusion phase, which weakens the private incentive to invest (so the public sector has to help).
- New challenges beyond funding and culture: **directional risk, defining success, user-vs-system design duality, governance for collaboration**.
- **Strategy** (Innovation Pentathlon): thread sustainability through the *foundation* - into **targets, metrics, roadmaps and partnerships** - so Ideas, Selection and Implementation all point the right way.
- **Process**: take Cooper's **Stage-Gate** (Discovery → Scoping → Business Case → Development → Testing → Launch, with Go/Kill gates) and **extend it across the life cycle** - add **Stage 6 Usage &amp; Maintenance** and **Stage 7 Retrieve &amp; apply the R principles** (reduce, reuse, recycle) - closing the loop back to Discovery and to chapter 2's eco-effectiveness. Design decisions at the **front end** lock in most of the impact.
- **Toyota** (tree-ring / slow-steady growth) and **Nike** show the three dimensions integrated in practice - and that "completely sustainable" is a direction, never a finish line.
</Aside>
