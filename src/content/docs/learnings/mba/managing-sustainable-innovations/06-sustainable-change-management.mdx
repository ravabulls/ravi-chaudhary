Here's a fact that surprises people: for most sustainability problems, **the technology already exists**. We know how to build solar farms, design recyclable packaging, cut factory emissions and run circular supply chains. So why is progress so slow? Because knowing *how* is the easy half. The hard half is getting a whole organisation of humans - with their habits, targets, fears and turf - to actually adopt the new way and *keep* doing it. That makes sustainability a **change-management** problem far more than an engineering one.

<Aside type="tip">
**The mindset for this chapter.** A sustainability initiative doesn't fail because the solar panel didn't work. It fails because people didn't change how they behave around it. As Kotter and Cohen put it, transformation efforts rarely die from technical issues - they die from **human** ones. So a sustainable innovation manager is, first and foremost, a manager of *change*.
</Aside>

## 1 · Why sustainability is really a change problem

**Change management** means the deliberate work of moving people and organisations from an old way of doing things to a new one - and helping them stick with it. It matters here because a sustainability solution is never just a gadget you install. Embedding it means rewiring four soft, sticky things:

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-flow">
    <div class="sc-node">Behaviour<span class="sub">what people actually do day to day</span></div>
    <div class="sc-node amber">Routines<span class="sub">the standard "how we work" processes</span></div>
    <div class="sc-node violet">Incentives<span class="sub">what gets measured, rewarded, promoted</span></div>
    <div class="sc-node green">Culture<span class="sub">the shared "way we do things here"</span></div>
  </div>

</div>

The scale of the problem is sobering. Classic estimates put the failure rate of major change efforts at somewhere around **half to two-thirds** - they simply don't deliver the results intended. And the reason is almost always the same: leaders underestimate how much the *people* side determines success. You can announce a net-zero target in a slide; you cannot announce a new culture into existence.

## 2 · Why people resist change

Resistance is not stupidity or laziness - it's a **rational, emotional human response** to being asked to give up something known for something uncertain. If you understand the reasons, you can design around them instead of bulldozing through them. The common drivers:

| Reason people resist | What it sounds like in the room |
|---|---|
| **Fear of loss** | "Will this cost me my job, my budget, my status?" |
| **Uncertainty** | "Nobody's told me what this actually means for my work." |
| **Comfort with the status quo** | "The old way works fine - why fix it?" |
| **Extra effort** | "I'm already stretched; now there's *more* to learn and do." |
| **Threat to identity or power** | "This makes my expertise or my department less important." |
| **Lack of understanding or trust** | "I don't get why we're doing this, and I don't trust the people pushing it." |

Two well-known patterns sit underneath this list. The **Kübler-Ross change curve** describes the emotional journey people ride through a big change - from shock and anxiety, into anger and denial, down through a frustrated low point, and only then up into curiosity, acceptance and finally confidence. Leaders who expect an instant "yes" panic at the anger stage; good ones know the dip is normal and plan support for it. The other is **Not-Invented-Here (NIH) syndrome** - a built-in bias against ideas that came from *outside* one's own team or company. It's a big reason external, greener solutions get quietly rejected: not because they're bad, but because "we" didn't think of them.

<Aside type="caution">
**The extra sting for *sustainability* change.** Most changes offer a clear personal payoff soon. Sustainability usually offers the opposite deal:

- The **benefits are diffuse and long-term** - a cooler planet decades out, gains shared by everyone and no one in particular, often felt most by people not yet born.
- The **costs are concrete and now** - a real budget line, a harder process, a habit I have to break *this* quarter.

So the natural human maths ("what do I get, and when?") points the wrong way. That is why sustainability change needs *even more* deliberate leadership than an ordinary reorganisation - you're asking people to pay today for a reward that is future-facing, shared and abstract.
</Aside>

You can picture any change as a tug-of-war between forces pushing *for* it and forces pushing *against* it - a **force-field**. The leader's job isn't only to add more push; it's often smarter to **weaken the resistors**, because a change shoved through against strong resistance snaps back the moment you stop pushing.

## 3 · Lewin's 3-step model - the simplest map

Kurt Lewin gave us the oldest and clearest picture of change, using an ice metaphor: to reshape a block of ice, you melt it, remould it, and let it re-freeze into the new shape. Organisations work the same way.

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-flow">
    <div class="sc-node amber">1 · Unfreeze<span class="sub">loosen the status quo</span></div>
    <div class="arrow">→<small>build urgency</small></div>
    <div class="sc-node">2 · Change / Move<span class="sub">implement the new way</span></div>
    <div class="arrow">→<small>support the dip</small></div>
    <div class="sc-node green">3 · Refreeze<span class="sub">make it the new normal</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Lewin's three steps. Skip "unfreeze" and people fight you; skip "refreeze" and the change quietly melts back to how things were.</figcaption>
</div>

| Step | What you're doing | For a sustainability change |
|---|---|---|
| **Unfreeze** | Create urgency; show why the old way can't continue; make people ready to let go | Share the climate/regulatory reality, the risk of inaction, a hopeful reason to move - loosen "we've always done it this way" |
| **Change / Move** | Put the new way into practice; guide people through the messy middle | Roll out the new process, tools and training; expect the emotional dip and resource it; involve people so it's *theirs* |
| **Refreeze** | Lock it in so it becomes the default and doesn't slip back | Bake sustainability into targets, job roles, reporting and reward, so it's just "how we work" |

The single most common mistake is treating change as one flip of a switch - announcing the new way (step 2) without unfreezing first or refreezing after. That's why so many green initiatives launch with a bang and fade within a year.

## 4 · Kotter's 8 steps - the detailed playbook

In 1995 John Kotter, studying *why* transformations fail, turned Lewin's three phases into an eight-step process. It's still the go-to checklist. Notice how it maps onto Lewin: steps 1-4 are really **unfreeze**, steps 5-7 are **change**, and step 8 is **refreeze**.

<Steps>

1. **Establish a sense of urgency.** Look hard at market, competitive and regulatory reality; help everyone feel *why acting now* matters. Without real urgency, people won't give the extra effort a change demands.

2. **Build a guiding coalition.** Assemble a group with enough power, credibility and commitment to lead the effort, and get them working as a team. One lonely champion is never enough.

3. **Form a clear vision.** Paint a picture of the sustainable future that is simple, appealing and easy to repeat - plus the strategy to get there. If you can't explain it in a minute, it's not a vision yet.

4. **Communicate the vision - relentlessly.** Use every channel, over and over; one email or town hall does nothing. And leaders must "walk the talk", becoming living examples of the new behaviour.

5. **Empower people to act.** Remove the obstacles - the systems, structures and rules that quietly punish the new way - and encourage risk-taking and non-traditional ideas.

6. **Generate short-term wins.** Deliberately plan visible, early results and celebrate them. Wins prove the change works, reward the believers and starve the resistance of ammunition.

7. **Consolidate gains and keep going.** Use the credibility from early wins to tackle deeper structures; promote people who embody the change; refresh the effort with new projects so momentum doesn't stall.

8. **Anchor it in the culture.** Make it "the way we do things around here" - connect the new behaviours to success, and change hiring and promotion rules, or renewal won't outlast the current leaders.

</Steps>

For a fast mental model, the eight steps group into three jobs:

  <div class="sc-flow">
    <div class="sc-node amber">Create a climate for change<span class="sub">steps 1-3: urgency, coalition, vision</span></div>
    <div class="arrow">→</div>
    <div class="sc-node">Engage the whole organisation<span class="sub">steps 4-6: communicate, empower, quick wins</span></div>
    <div class="arrow">→</div>
    <div class="sc-node green">Implement &amp; sustain<span class="sub">steps 7-8: consolidate, anchor in culture</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Kotter's eight steps in three phases - which line up neatly with Lewin's unfreeze, change and refreeze.</figcaption>
</div>

<Aside type="note">
**Get the problem right before you solve it.** A theme running through Kotter's early steps is *problem formulation*. Einstein reckoned framing a problem well is often more important than solving it - yet managers routinely rush past this, latching onto the most obvious symptom and "solving" the wrong thing. Studies of real projects found the majority of problems had to be sent *back* for re-framing. For sustainability, where issues are tangled and systemic, spend real time defining the problem - with multiple viewpoints in the room - before jumping to a fix.
</Aside>

## 5 · Matching the response to the resistance

Because resistance comes from different roots, the fix has to match the root. Push where you should have listened, and you make things worse. A rough map:

<table class="sc-matrix">
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Root of the resistance</th><th>Leadership response that fits</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>Lack of understanding or information</td><td>Educate and communicate - explain the why, early and often</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Fear of loss / self-interest</td><td>Negotiate, and be honest about what changes; protect or retrain where you can</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Not feeling heard / low ownership</td><td>Involve people in designing the change so it becomes theirs</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Anxiety about coping with the new way</td><td>Support and facilitate - training, time, coaching through the dip</td></tr>
    <tr><td>NIH bias against outside ideas</td><td>Reframe the idea as the team's own; give internal credit for adopting it</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Low trust in leadership</td><td>Have leaders visibly "walk the talk" and deliver on small promises first</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

## 6 · Leading a sustainability transformation in practice

Pulling the models together, leading a sustainable change comes down to a handful of moves that recur in every successful story:

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-flow col">
    <div class="sc-node">Secure genuine leadership commitment<span class="sub">not a slogan - real time, budget and role-modelling from the top</span></div>
    <div class="arrow">↓</div>
    <div class="sc-node violet">Set a compelling vision and purpose<span class="sub">a "why" people find worth the effort</span></div>
    <div class="arrow">↓</div>
    <div class="sc-node amber">Engage stakeholders and employees<span class="sub">involve them early so the change is co-owned, not imposed</span></div>
    <div class="arrow">↓</div>
    <div class="sc-node">Align incentives and structures<span class="sub">put sustainability into targets, budgets, governance and reward</span></div>
    <div class="arrow">↓</div>
    <div class="sc-node green">Celebrate quick wins, then anchor it<span class="sub">prove it works, then embed it so it can't snap back</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>The practical arc of leading sustainable change - commitment and vision at the top, ownership in the middle, and hard structural anchoring so the change outlives the launch.</figcaption>
</div>

Two themes deserve extra weight for *sustainability* specifically. First, **align the structures**, or the change is doomed - if bonuses still reward only short-term volume and cost, no amount of green vision will hold, because the incentives quietly pull people back. Governance means making sustainability a core part of how the company works: written into policies, processes and objectives, not bolted on the side. Second, expect **paradoxical tensions** - you'll chase competing sustainable goals at once, and they'll sometimes clash (a more recyclable material can carry a *higher* carbon footprint, for instance). Good leaders don't pretend these trade-offs away; they surface them, weigh them openly, and decide.

### Two contrasting ways to lead it: Patagonia and Carlsberg

The same principles show up very differently depending on where a company starts.

<div class="sc-split">
  <div class="sc-panel eff">
    <div class="head">Patagonia - values-led from birth</div>
    <ul>
      <li>Sustainability is the company's <strong>founding purpose</strong>, not a retrofit - so the "unfreeze" and "vision" steps were baked in from day one</li>
      <li>Leads through <strong>activism</strong> and provocative honesty - famously telling customers "don't buy this jacket" to confront overconsumption</li>
      <li>Change is driven by <strong>culture and identity</strong>: people join *because* of the mission, so resistance is low and the vision is genuinely shared</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="sc-panel rsp">
    <div class="head">Carlsberg - a legacy giant transforming</div>
    <ul>
      <li>A large, old, mainstream company that has to <strong>change an existing culture and supply chain</strong> - the harder, more typical case</li>
      <li>Runs a structured <strong>sustainability programme</strong> with time-bound targets (e.g. its work toward greener packaging like the Green Fibre Bottle)</li>
      <li>Uses <strong>open innovation</strong> and partnerships to solve problems it can't crack alone - every one of Kotter's steps has to be worked at deliberately</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

The contrast is the lesson. For **Patagonia**, sustainability *is* the culture, so change management is mostly about staying true to it. For **Carlsberg**, sustainability has to be *led into* a big established organisation - which is exactly where Lewin and Kotter earn their keep: create urgency, build a coalition, set a vision, deliver visible wins, and anchor it until it becomes "how we brew." Most companies you'll ever work for look like Carlsberg, not Patagonia - which is why change-management skill, not just green technology, is the thing that actually moves the needle.

## Revision summary

<Aside type="tip">
**The one-minute recap**

- Sustainability is a **change-management problem** more than a technical one: the solutions usually exist, but embedding them means changing **behaviour, routines, incentives and culture** - and change efforts fail at a high rate for *human*, not technical, reasons.
- **People resist** for rational reasons: fear of loss, uncertainty, comfort, extra effort, threats to identity or power, low understanding or trust - expect the **Kübler-Ross** emotional dip and **NIH** bias. Sustainability is *extra* hard because benefits are **diffuse, long-term and future-facing** while costs are **concrete and now**.
- **Lewin's 3 steps:** **Unfreeze** (build urgency, loosen the status quo) → **Change/Move** (implement the new way, support the dip) → **Refreeze** (anchor it as the new normal).
- **Kotter's 8 steps** expand that: urgency, guiding coalition, vision, communicate, empower, short-term wins, consolidate, anchor in culture - grouped as *create a climate → engage everyone → implement and sustain*.
- **To lead it:** secure real leadership commitment and a compelling purpose, engage and co-own with stakeholders, **align incentives and structures** (or it snaps back), celebrate quick wins, and embed it - while owning the **paradoxical trade-offs** honestly.
- **Patagonia** (values-led, sustainability *is* the culture) vs **Carlsberg** (a legacy giant deliberately transforming) show the same principles in easy mode and hard mode - and most real companies are the hard mode.
</Aside>
