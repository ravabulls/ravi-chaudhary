Every chapter before this one was about *doing* sustainability - understanding the problem, choosing strategies, innovating the product and the business model, and leading the change through an organisation. This last chapter is about the part that keeps everyone honest: **proving you actually did it**, and communicating that proof in a way people can trust.

It comes in two halves. Part 1 is **reporting** - how a company measures and discloses its sustainability performance without slipping into spin. Part 2 is the **case-study method** - a practical way to crack the kind of business case this course is examined with, because the final exam is a written case-study essay.

<Aside type="tip">
**The one idea joining the two halves.** A claim only counts once it can be *measured, proved and communicated honestly*. Reporting is how a firm does that for the outside world; a good case-study answer is how *you* do that for the examiner - state a position, back it with evidence, and lay out what to do next.
</Aside>

## 1 · Why report on sustainability at all?

Reporting is not paperwork for its own sake. Companies publish sustainability information because four different pressures push them to - and each pressure rewards a different quality of report.

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-flow">
    <div class="sc-node green">Transparency<span class="sub">accountability to stakeholders</span></div>
    <div class="sc-node violet" style="background:var(--sc-blue)">Comparability<span class="sub">judge firms on the same basis</span></div>
    <div class="sc-node amber">Regulation &amp; investors<span class="sub">legal duty + ESG demand</span></div>
    <div class="sc-node violet">Internal improvement<span class="sub">what gets measured gets managed</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>The four drivers of sustainability reporting. Notice the last one points inward - reporting is not only for outsiders; the act of measuring changes what a company pays attention to.</figcaption>
</div>

| Driver | What it means | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Transparency &amp; accountability** | Openly showing your impacts - good and bad - to the people affected by them | Builds trust; lets employees, customers, communities and NGOs hold the firm to its word |
| **Comparability** | Reporting to a common standard so numbers line up across companies and years | An investor or regulator can rank firms only if they measure the same things the same way |
| **Regulation &amp; investor demand** | Legal disclosure duties, plus **ESG** (Environmental, Social, Governance) criteria that investors now screen for | Non-compliance carries penalties; capital increasingly flows toward firms with credible ESG data |
| **Internal improvement** | Targets and metrics that steer the business itself | *"What gets measured gets managed"* - you cannot cut an emission or a risk you never counted |

That last line is the one to remember. The discipline of measuring - picking indicators, gathering data, watching them move year to year - is often what turns a vague ambition into an actual operational change.

## 2 · Materiality - and why the EU says "double"

You cannot report on *everything*. **Materiality** is the filter: report the issues that genuinely matter and leave out the trivial. The interesting question is *matter to whom?* - and that is where the EU has pushed the whole field forward.

<div class="sc-split">
  <div class="sc-panel eff">
    <div class="head">Financial materiality <small>outside-in</small></div>
    <ul>
      <li>How sustainability issues affect <strong>the company</strong></li>
      <li>Climate, resource scarcity, regulation as <strong>risks &amp; opportunities</strong> to profit and value</li>
      <li>The view an <strong>investor</strong> cares about - will this hurt or help the business?</li>
      <li>e.g. a drought threatening a factory's water supply</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="sc-panel rsp">
    <div class="head">Impact materiality <small>inside-out</small></div>
    <ul>
      <li>How the company affects <strong>society &amp; the environment</strong></li>
      <li>The firm's footprint on <strong>people and planet</strong>, good or bad</li>
      <li>The view a <strong>community or NGO</strong> cares about - what are you doing to us and the world?</li>
      <li>e.g. that same factory draining a river a town relies on</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

**Double materiality** simply insists on *both* directions at once. A company must report how the world affects it (financial) **and** how it affects the world (impact). This is the defining principle of the EU's new regime - and a real philosophical shift, because older, investor-led standards often stopped at financial materiality alone. Double materiality is the reporting-world echo of the triple bottom line from Chapter 1: the environment and society are not just risks to the business, they are ends that count in their own right.

## 3 · The frameworks and standards - a map

Sustainability reporting can feel like alphabet soup, so it helps to sort the acronyms by what job each one does. Some are **frameworks** (principles for *how* to report), some are **standards** (specific *what* to disclose), some are **mandatory law**, and some are just a shared vocabulary.

<div class="sc-fig">

| Name | What it is | Mandatory? | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| **GRI** - Global Reporting Initiative | The oldest and most widely used reporting standard; a menu of disclosures on economic, environmental and social topics | Voluntary | **Impact** materiality - the firm's effect on the world |
| **CSRD / ESRS** - Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive &amp; European Sustainability Reporting Standards | The EU's law (CSRD) plus the detailed rulebook it runs on (ESRS); phased in for many large and listed EU companies | **Mandatory** (EU) | **Double** materiality, with third-party **assurance** |
| **Integrated Reporting (IR)** | A framework to weave financial and non-financial information into one "value-creation" story rather than two separate reports | Voluntary | How the firm creates value over time across many capitals |
| **SASB / ISSB (IFRS S1 &amp; S2)** | Industry-specific, investor-focused metrics (SASB), now folded into the ISSB's global baseline standards under the IFRS Foundation | Voluntary (adopted by some jurisdictions) | **Financial** materiality for investors; S2 covers climate |
| **UN SDGs** - Sustainable Development Goals | 17 global goals to 2030; used to *frame and communicate* how a firm's work contributes | Voluntary framing | A shared language of goals, not a metric standard |
| **GHG Protocol / TCFD** | The GHG Protocol is the standard method for counting emissions (Scopes 1, 2 and 3); TCFD is a framework for disclosing climate-related financial risk | Voluntary (TCFD now absorbed into ISSB) | Carbon accounting &amp; climate-risk disclosure |

<figcaption>The reporting landscape at a glance. The big contrast to hold onto: <strong>GRI</strong> is voluntary and impact-focused, while the EU's <strong>CSRD/ESRS</strong> is mandatory and demands double materiality plus independent assurance.</figcaption>
</div>

The trend across all of these is **convergence and hardening**: voluntary, patchy disclosure is giving way to standardised, legally required, independently checked reporting. That shift is exactly what makes the next problem - greenwashing - harder to get away with.

<Aside type="note">
**One word worth knowing: assurance.** *Assurance* means an independent third party - typically an auditor - checks the reported figures and states how confident they are that the numbers are right, much as financial accounts are audited. It is the difference between a company grading its own homework and having it marked. The EU's CSRD requires assurance precisely because self-reported sustainability data, unchecked, is easy to inflate.
</Aside>

## 4 · Greenwashing - and why credible reporting is the cure

**Greenwashing** is making a company or product look more environmentally or socially responsible than it really is. It is the reason sustainability claims are met with suspicion, and it comes in a handful of recognisable shapes.

<div class="sc-fig">

| Greenwashing type | The trick | Tell-tale example |
|---|---|---|
| **Vague / undefined claims** | Feel-good words with no fixed meaning | "Eco-friendly", "natural", "green" - defined nowhere |
| **No evidence** | A claim with nothing to back it | "50% more sustainable" with no data or source |
| **Hidden trade-offs** | One green feature hides a bigger harm | "Recyclable" packaging around a hugely polluting product |
| **Irrelevant claims** | True but meaningless | "CFC-free" - when those chemicals are already banned by law |
| **Outright false** | Simply not true | A fake certification logo or invented figure |

<figcaption>Five common flavours of greenwashing. Most share a root cause: a claim that cannot be checked. The antidote is built into the claim's opposite.</figcaption>
</div>

The cure falls straight out of the diagnosis. If greenwashing thrives on claims that cannot be checked, then the defence is reporting that is:

<div class="sc-chips">
  <span class="sc-chip green">Credible - specific, evidenced claims</span>
  <span class="sc-chip blue">Standardised - a common framework, so it is comparable</span>
  <span class="sc-chip violet">Assured - independently audited, not self-graded</span>
  <span class="sc-chip amber">Balanced - reports the bad news too</span>
</div>

This is precisely why the EU's CSRD makes **assurance** compulsory: an outside auditor signing off on the numbers is what turns a marketing brochure into a trustworthy account. Good reporting is not just *nice to have* - it is the structural answer to greenwashing.

## 5 · The case-study method - because this is how you are examined

Now the practical half. The final exam is a **three-hour written case-study essay** of roughly 1,500 words: you receive a real-ish business situation and must write a structured, argued response. Marks split into **structure**, **content** (by far the largest share) and **writing**. Knowing the *method* below is worth as much as knowing the course content, because the content only scores if it is deployed inside a clear structure.

<Aside type="note">
**What a "case" actually is.** A case is a written snapshot of a real-ish business situation. It has a genuine issue, enough information to reason about it - and *no single objectively correct answer*. That last point is liberating and scary at once: you will not be marked on guessing the "right" answer, but on how well your position is **supported by evidence** from the case.
</Aside>

### The three scenario types

Almost every case is one of three kinds. Spotting which one you are holding tells you how to structure the whole essay - so this is the first move.

| Scenario | The main character must… | Signal words | Your essay's job |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Decision** | Choose between options and defend the choice | "decide", "whether", "should we…" | Recommend one option and prove it beats the rest |
| **Evaluation** | Judge something - is it good or bad, strong or weak? | "evaluate", "assess", "appraise" | Give an overall verdict weighing positives *and* negatives |
| **Problem diagnosis** | Work out *why* something happened | "problem", "why", "what caused…" | Find and prove the root cause(s), most important first |

### Reading and analysing the case

Resist the urge to read top-to-bottom highlighting everything. The efficient path is to grasp the big picture first, then dig only where it matters.

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-flow">
    <div class="sc-node slate">Skim ends<span class="sub">first &amp; last sections</span></div>
    <div class="arrow">→</div>
    <div class="sc-node slate">Scan exhibits<span class="sub">what info is where</span></div>
    <div class="arrow">→</div>
    <div class="sc-node amber">Stop &amp; think<span class="sub">which scenario is this?</span></div>
    <div class="arrow">→</div>
    <div class="sc-node violet" style="background:var(--sc-blue)">List what you need<span class="sub">to answer the question</span></div>
    <div class="arrow">→</div>
    <div class="sc-node slate">Deep dive<span class="sub">read only the relevant parts</span></div>
    <div class="arrow">→</div>
    <div class="sc-node green">Take a position<span class="sub">backed by evidence</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>The case-reading pipeline. The pivotal step is the amber one: stop reading and <em>think</em> - identify what the main character has to do and what the biggest uncertainty is, before you invest in a close reading.</figcaption>
</div>

Two questions unlock almost any case: **what does the main character have to do?** and **what is the major uncertainty?** With those answered you know your scenario type and can focus your analysis. Remember the working definition of **evidence** here: facts and numbers from the case, sound calculations built on them, reasonable inferences, and statements by the characters - anything that can be independently checked. Opinion without that backing is not evidence.

## 6 · Structuring the exam essay

The examiner rewards a **clear position, prioritised and fact-supported analysis, and a logical action plan** - in that order. Whatever the scenario, the skeleton is the same three moves: What, Why, How.

<Steps>

1. **Opening - state your position up front (What).** Do not build suspense. In the first lines, name the situation and give your answer: the recommended decision, the overall evaluation, or the diagnosed problem. The examiner should know your thesis before they finish the first paragraph. This is graded on its own for being *clear and precise*.

2. **Analysis - prove it with prioritised, evidenced argument (Why).** This is the largest part of your marks. Lay out your criteria (a case rarely states them - you *infer* them from the situation, your experience, and course concepts), then work through the evidence from **most important to least**. Explicitly weave in the frameworks from this course - the three strategies, sustainable innovation, business-model change, leadership and change management. Concepts from class are *expected*, not optional.

3. **Plan of action - say what happens next (How).** Turn your argument into concrete steps in a logical, roughly chronological order: urgent actions first, then other short-term steps, then the long-term moves. Name the **stakeholders** who must be involved (and any who might resist), and flag the **risks** - what could go wrong, and how you would soften it.

</Steps>

The three scenarios flex this skeleton only slightly. It is worth memorising the specific opening each one calls for:

| Scenario | Open by stating… | Then prove… | Always finish with… |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Decision** | The decision and your recommended option | Why that option best fits your criteria and the evidence | An action plan |
| **Evaluation** | Your overall verdict - the key positives *and* negatives | Each criterion, with evidence for and against; handle any major contingency | An action plan |
| **Problem diagnosis** | The problem, in a sentence or two, plus its symptoms | Each cause, most important first, with case evidence | An action plan |

<Aside type="caution">
**The three habits that win the marks.** (1) **Incorporate course concepts** - an answer that reads like general common sense scores far below one that names and applies the right framework. (2) **Prioritise** - lead with the strongest evidence and the biggest cause; do not treat every point as equal. (3) **Be clear and well-structured** - an examiner reading fast should never lose the thread. Structure and readability are literally on the mark sheet.
</Aside>

### A worked mini-example

To make the skeleton concrete, imagine a decision case like the practice one used in this course: a large furniture retailer must **choose how to secure a sustainable wood supply** while still hitting an ambitious growth plan, picking between several sourcing options. Here is the method applied in miniature.

</div>

Notice how the **course concepts** slot straight in: the sourcing options are really a **business-model and strategy** choice (efficiency versus effectiveness versus sufficiency of material use); securing the supply is a **sustainable-innovation** and partnership problem; and rolling it out across suppliers is a **change-leadership** challenge. That is what "incorporate concepts from class" looks like in practice - the frameworks are the criteria you judge the options with.

<Aside type="note">
**Facts versus noise.** A case deliberately mixes essential information with background colour and distractions. Part of the skill is separating the numbers and statements that bear on your criteria from the interesting-but-irrelevant detail. If a fact does not help you decide, evaluate, or diagnose - it is noise, and it does not belong in your essay.
</Aside>

## Revision summary

<Aside type="tip">
**The one-minute recap**

- **Why report:** transparency and accountability, comparability, meeting regulation and investor (ESG) demand, and driving internal improvement - because *what gets measured gets managed*.
- **Double materiality** is the EU's key principle: report both how sustainability issues affect the company (**financial**, outside-in) *and* how the company affects people and planet (**impact**, inside-out).
- **Frameworks map:** **GRI** is voluntary and impact-focused; the EU's **CSRD/ESRS** is mandatory with double materiality plus assurance; **Integrated Reporting** fuses financial and non-financial into one value story; **SASB/ISSB (IFRS S1/S2)**, the **UN SDGs** and the **GHG Protocol/TCFD** fill in investor metrics, shared goals and carbon accounting.
- **Greenwashing** - vague, unevidenced, trade-off-hiding, irrelevant or false claims - is beaten by reporting that is credible, standardised, independently **assured** and balanced.
- **Case method:** spot the scenario (**decision**, **evaluation** or **problem diagnosis**), read for the big picture, find the main character's task and the major uncertainty, then take an evidence-backed position.
- **Exam essay:** open with your position, give a prioritised and fact-supported analysis that uses course concepts, and end with a logical action plan naming stakeholders and risks. Incorporate concepts, prioritise, be clear.
</Aside>
