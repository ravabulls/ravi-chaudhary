"Sustainability" is one of those words everyone uses and few can define crisply. This first chapter fixes that: what it actually means, why it's genuinely hard (a "wicked problem"), and the core idea that everything else in the course builds on - **decoupling** human progress from environmental harm.

<Aside type="tip">
**The definition to hold on to.** Sustainability is the **balanced integration of economic performance, social inclusiveness, and environmental resilience - for the benefit of current *and* future generations** (Geissdoerfer et al., 2017). Two things to notice: it's *three* dimensions at once (not just "green"), and it explicitly cares about **people who aren't born yet**.
</Aside>

## 1 · The three dimensions (the triple bottom line)

Sustainability rests on three pillars that must be balanced together - often drawn as three overlapping circles, or as "people, planet, profit":

  <div class="sc-flow">
    <div class="sc-node green">Environment<span class="sub">planet - ecological resilience</span></div>
    <div class="sc-node amber">Society<span class="sub">people - social inclusiveness</span></div>
    <div class="sc-node slate">Economy<span class="sub">profit - economic performance</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>The three dimensions of sustainability. A truly sustainable choice has to work on all three at once - a solution that's green but bankrupts the firm, or profitable but exploitative, isn't sustainable.</figcaption>
</div>

The trap to avoid: treating sustainability as *only* environmental. The social dimension (fair work, health, inclusion, community) is an equal pillar - and the economic dimension is what keeps a business alive long enough to do any good at all.

## 2 · Sustainable development and the Brundtland definition

The most famous definition comes from the UN's **Brundtland Report** (WCED, 1987):

> *"Sustainable development is development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs."*

That single sentence hides **two kinds of justice** that pull against each other:

<div class="sc-split">
  <div class="sc-panel">
    <div class="head">Intra-generational justice <small>fairness today</small></div>
    <ul>
      <li>Meeting the needs of people alive <strong>now</strong></li>
      <li>With "overriding priority" for the world's <strong>poorest</strong></li>
      <li>e.g. lifting people out of poverty, access to food, energy, sanitation</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="sc-panel rsp">
    <div class="head">Inter-generational justice <small>fairness over time</small></div>
    <ul>
      <li>Not compromising the ability of <strong>future</strong> generations to meet their needs</li>
      <li>Don't use up or wreck what they'll depend on</li>
      <li>e.g. climate, biodiversity, finite resources</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

## 3 · Why sustainability is a "wicked problem"

A **wicked problem** is one with no clean definition, no single right answer, deep trade-offs, and where every attempted fix has knock-on effects. Sustainability qualifies because the two justices above often **conflict**:

- Historically, human development has been **tightly linked to consuming natural resources** - raising living standards usually raises the ecological footprint.
- High-income lifestyles consume far more per person than low-income ones - so improving welfare (intra-generational justice) tends to worsen environmental pressure (inter-generational justice).

<Aside type="note">
**Covid-19 as a wicked-problem illustration.** The 2020 shutdown *cut* emissions (Earth Overshoot Day fell three weeks later) - seemingly good for future generations. But the change was **forced, not chosen** (so unlikely to last), and it was **devastating for intra-generational justice**: broken supply chains pushed millions in the global south back into poverty. A "win" on one dimension was a serious loss on another. That's wickedness in a nutshell.
</Aside>

But the two aren't *always* in conflict: the world's poor depend most directly on nature (for food, fuel, materials) and are hit hardest by environmental damage - so **protecting ecosystems can itself be a form of poverty reduction**. The goal is to find those win-wins.

## 4 · The central task: decoupling (and IPAT)

If development and harm are historically linked, the whole game is to **break that link** - to keep improving human lives while *reducing* environmental impact. That's **decoupling**. A simple way to see the levers is the **IPAT equation**:

<div class="sc-formula">
  <div>
    <span class="name">Impact</span>
    <code>I = P × A × T</code>
  </div>
  <div>
    <span class="name">P - Population</span>
    number of people
  </div>
  <div>
    <span class="name">A - Affluence</span>
    consumption per person
  </div>
  <div>
    <span class="name">T - Technology</span>
    impact per unit of consumption
  </div>
</div>

Environmental **Impact** is Population × Affluence × Technology. Since we can't (and shouldn't) engineer the population factor, sustainability strategy works on the other two: **cut the Technology factor** (cleaner, more efficient ways to make and use things) and **moderate the Affluence factor** (consume less, and differently). Those two levers become the three strategies in the next chapter.

## 5 · Measuring it - because what we don't measure, we ignore

A big reason sustainability is hard is that it's **hard to measure** - there's no single clean number separating "sustainable" from "not". A nation's success is still mostly measured by wealth (GDP), which misses almost everything that matters here. A few better instruments:

| Indicator | What it captures |
|---|---|
| **Human Development Index (HDI)** | A composite of life expectancy, education and income - a fuller picture of *human* development than GDP alone (an intra-generational-justice gauge) |
| **Ecological Footprint** | How much of Earth's regenerative capacity our consumption uses |
| **Earth Overshoot Day** | The date each year when humanity has used all the resources Earth can regenerate that year - the earlier the date, the more we're living at the future's expense |

<Aside type="caution">
**The inequality behind the averages.** Progress hasn't reached everyone: hundreds of millions still live on very little, billions lack basic sanitation, and a small share of the world's population holds the large majority of its wealth. Sustainability isn't only about the planet's limits - it's about *who* gets to live within them and how fairly.
</Aside>

## Revision summary

<Aside type="tip">
**The one-minute recap**

- **Sustainability = balancing economy, society and environment** (the triple bottom line) for **present and future** generations.
- The **Brundtland** definition: meet today's needs without compromising future generations' ability to meet theirs - combining **intra-generational** (fairness now, priority to the poor) and **inter-generational** (fairness over time) justice.
- It's a **wicked problem** because those justices often conflict (development has been tied to resource use) - though protecting nature can also reduce poverty.
- The core task is **decoupling** progress from harm; **IPAT** (`I = P × A × T`) shows the levers - cut the **Technology** and **Affluence** factors.
- It's genuinely **hard to measure** - go beyond GDP to indicators like the **HDI**, the **Ecological Footprint** and **Earth Overshoot Day**, and remember the inequality hidden in averages.
</Aside>
