Chapter 4 asked *why* firms spread innovation across the globe and *how* it flows (including reverse innovation). This chapter asks two sharper, more practical questions. First: **where** does a new design need to catch on for the whole world to follow? That is **lead market** theory, and its punchline is delightfully counter-intuitive - the country that *invents* something often matters less than the country that *adopts* it first. Second: once a firm has R&D sites scattered around the planet, **how do you make each one actually earn its keep?** That is the **value diamond** - three distinct jobs a site can do, each with its own selection logic, payoff, and trap.

<Aside type="tip">
**The two ideas to hold onto.** (1) A **lead market** is the country of *adoption*, not invention - get your design accepted there and global diffusion tends to follow. (2) Every R&D site should be playing one of three deliberate roles - **substitution, complementarity, or discovery** - never just "an office we happen to have."
</Aside>

## 1 · Lead market theory - adoption beats invention

The concept comes largely from **Marian Beise** (with roots in Gerybadze &amp; Reger). A **lead market** is a country where a particular innovation *design* is the first to be **widely accepted and adopted** - and that early adoption then triggers the design's **diffusion across the rest of the world**. The key move is to separate two things we usually lump together:

<div class="sc-split">
  <div class="sc-panel">
    <div class="head">Country of invention</div>
    <ul>
      <li>Where the idea or technology was first <em>created</em></li>
      <li>Impressive, but not what decides the global standard</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="sc-panel rsp">
    <div class="head">Country of adoption <small>the lead market</small></div>
    <ul>
      <li>Where a specific design is first <em>widely used</em></li>
      <li>Its choice of design becomes the template other countries copy</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

Why does this matter for management? Because a design can be invented in many places at once, but only one design usually "wins" globally - and the winner is typically the one that a **representative** market embraced first. Firms that read lead markets well can point their R&D at the place whose choices will echo everywhere, which **reduces duplicated R&D** (you stop developing five regional variants when one global design will travel) and is a major reason R&D **internationalises** in the first place - you set up abroad partly to sit inside the market that sets the tune.

### 1.1 The representativeness condition

There is one crucial catch. A lead market only works if it is **not idiosyncratic** - it must not be a peculiar, isolated market whose preferences are relevant *only to itself*. If a country's demand is weird and self-contained, the "lag markets" (the followers) will simply not follow, and the design dies at the border. Beise's classic illustration is the fax machine versus the teletypewriter, and the tug-of-war between demand conditions in the USA and France: the design that spread globally was the one adopted in a market whose needs *resembled* everyone else's, not the locally-optimised oddity.

<Aside type="note">
**Lead market vs lag market.** A **lag market** is simply a country that adopts *later*, following the lead market's design. The whole theory only holds if the lead market is representative enough that the lag markets recognise their own needs in it - otherwise there is no diffusion, just a local quirk.
</Aside>

### 1.2 The classic examples - and the shift underway

Historically, lead markets clustered in the advanced "Triad" economies, and different industries had different leaders:

| Industry | Classic lead market | Why it led |
|---|---|---|
| **Renewable energy** | Germany | Early, policy-backed mass adoption of solar/wind |
| **Computer / Internet** | USA | Deep early user base set the global design norms |
| **Automotive** | Germany | Demanding drivers and a dense supplier ecosystem |
| **Robotics** | Japan | Intense industrial uptake pulled the technology forward |

But the map is changing. **Emerging economies** are increasingly showing lead-market characteristics of their own - most visibly in **digital technology**, where large, **unsaturated markets** (think mobile payments or leapfrogged services) adopt new designs fast precisely because there is no entrenched old system to replace. A market that is hungry and un-committed can adopt faster than a rich, saturated one - which is why the next lead market for a digital design may well be in Asia or Africa rather than the Triad.

## 2 · Lead-market potential - the five advantages

How do you spot a would-be lead market *before* it leads? **Tiwari &amp; Herstatt** bundle the signals into **five advantages**. No single one is decisive; it is the *combination* that gives a country genuine lead-market potential.

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-chips">
    <span class="sc-chip">Demand advantage</span>
    <span class="sc-chip">Cost advantage</span>
    <span class="sc-chip">Transfer &amp; Export advantage</span>
    <span class="sc-chip">Technological advantage</span>
    <span class="sc-chip">Market-structure advantage</span>
  </div>
  <figcaption>The five advantages that together build <strong>lead-market potential</strong> (Tiwari &amp; Herstatt). Think of them as five gauges - a strong lead market reads high on most of them at once.</figcaption>
</div>

Here is what each one actually measures:

| Advantage | What it captures | Watch-outs & nuance |
|---|---|---|
| **Demand** | Per-capita income and the **anticipatory needs** of customers - buyers who want tomorrow's features today | Income matters in **both directions**: high income can pull premium designs, but *lower* income can pull frugal designs that then travel |
| **Cost** | Potential **economies of scale** (market size *and* expected growth) plus local **factor costs** | A big, fast-growing market lets a design reach cost-competitive volume quickly |
| **Transfer & Export** | How readily the design *travels*: international demonstration effects, similarity of local demand to foreign markets, presence of multinational and **"mobile" users**, export incentives, cross-national **policy convergence** | This is the representativeness condition in operational form - the more the country resembles others, the more it can lead |
| **Technological** | A **skilled workforce/professionals** and **access to open knowledge networks** | Feeds the ability to actually build and refine the design locally |
| **Market-structure** | Vigorous **competition**, a **mature financial sector**, and **competitive ancillary industries** (suppliers, services) | Rivalry and good financing force firms to keep improving the design |

<Aside type="caution">
**Demand runs both ways - don't forget the low end.** It is tempting to equate "lead market" with "rich market." But the per-capita-income factor is explicitly two-directional: a **lower-income** market with anticipatory needs can be the lead market for a *frugal* design that then diffuses upward into wealthy markets. That is the bridge straight back to reverse innovation from Chapter 4.
</Aside>

## 3 · The global innovation footprint - making each site count

Now flip from the *market* side to the *firm* side. A firm doing global innovation ends up with a **footprint** of R&D sites, and it has choices about how it sources that R&D in the first place - build it in-house or buy it in, keep it at home or place it abroad. Those **dimensions of R&D sourcing** (internal vs external, domestic vs foreign) set the stage. But the deeper management question is what each site is *for*.

**Doz &amp; Wilson's value diamond** answers exactly that: it maps **three roles** an R&D site can play. The discipline is to know which role a given site is playing - and to run it accordingly, because the selection logic, the payoff, and the failure mode are all different.

    <div class="sc-node amber">Substitution<span class="sub">same work, better conditions</span></div>
    <span class="arrow">→</span>
    <div class="sc-node violet">Complementarity<span class="sub">unique knowledge, combined</span></div>
    <span class="arrow">→</span>
    <div class="sc-node green">Discovery<span class="sub">forefront of change</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>The value diamond's three site roles (Doz &amp; Wilson). The arrow is not a ranking - it is a common <em>evolution</em> path: discovery sites often mature into complementary ones, and substitution sites can grow into complementary ones too.</figcaption>
</div>

### 3.1 Substitution - do the same work, but better placed

A **substitution** site does work the firm *could* do at home, but does it more **cost-effectively and productively** somewhere else. The logic is not "cheap labour" - it is finding a location where local conditions genuinely lift output per unit of effort.

<div class="sc-split">
  <div class="sc-panel">
    <div class="head">How you pick it & run it</div>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Select:</strong> local conditions that deliver real productivity gains - skills, regulations, ecosystem - plus a <strong>large talent pool</strong></li>
      <li><strong>Do:</strong> make it a genuine <em>centre of excellence</em>, well-integrated into the global network via shared processes, systems, connectivity and knowledge banks; build career paths so good people stay</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="sc-panel rsp">
    <div class="head">Payoff & trap</div>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Benefits:</strong> lower cost <em>and</em> higher quality; faster cycle times and time-to-market</li>
      <li><strong>Pitfall:</strong> don't confuse <strong>"low cost" with "low wage"</strong>; and don't just dump mundane, repetitive, no-value work there - that should be outsourced, not made into a site</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

Over time a well-run substitution site may **evolve into a complementary** one; a poorly-positioned one may have to be **downsized or closed**.

### 3.2 Complementarity - each site brings something unique

A **complementarity** site exists because it holds knowledge *no other site has*. Here value does not come from any one location doing more of the same - it comes from **combining distinct knowledge across sites** and from links to outside players.

<div class="sc-split">
  <div class="sc-panel">
    <div class="head">How you pick it & run it</div>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Select:</strong> map the knowledge innovation needs and find <em>where that knowledge actually lives</em> - those locations become your priority sites</li>
      <li><strong>Do:</strong> treat cross-site <strong>collaboration as a prerequisite</strong>, each site contributing unique knowledge; tap user networks and the wider ecosystem for value from external links; back it with strong senior-management sponsorship</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="sc-panel rsp">
    <div class="head">Payoff & trap</div>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Benefits:</strong> innovations drawn from a far greater diversity of knowledge and capabilities - market insights, technologies, methods</li>
      <li><strong>Pitfall:</strong> <strong>redundancy and duplication</strong> when sites aren't differentiated; and sites turning into isolated <strong>"mini-fiefdoms"</strong> that hoard rather than contribute to the common goal</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

Because knowledge moves, the knowledge map has to be **re-checked regularly** - which can mean relocating sites. GE's John F. Welch Technology Center in Bengaluru is the textbook example of a site that grew into a genuine complementary contributor.

### 3.3 Discovery - camp out where the future is arriving

A **discovery** site is planted in a **"harbinger" location** - a place at the *forefront of change*, a forerunner of what the rest of the world will face later. Its job is **accelerated learning**: rapid-cycle experimentation with new business models, services and technologies, aimed at **radical or architectural innovation** and at opening **new markets**.

<div class="sc-split">
  <div class="sc-panel">
    <div class="head">How you pick it & run it</div>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Select:</strong> harbinger locations and <em>accelerated learning environments</em> for fast experimentation - sometimes places whose <strong>regulatory regimes force alternative approaches</strong> you'd never try at home</li>
      <li><strong>Do:</strong> reach for unfamiliar, new knowledge; experiment boldly with models and technologies; and keep <strong>strong communication back to the business</strong> so the learning actually lands</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="sc-panel rsp">
    <div class="head">Payoff & trap</div>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Benefits:</strong> genuine exploration - new opportunities and fresh knowledge that feed radical innovation</li>
      <li><strong>Pitfall:</strong> value is <strong>hard to measure</strong>, so it's easy to <strong>lose patience</strong> and pull the plug; and if the site gets <strong>isolated from the business</strong>, its discoveries are simply lost</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

The natural life-cycle is that a discovery site which keeps contributing usable knowledge eventually **matures into a complementarity** site - the frontier becomes the mainstream, and yesterday's experiment becomes part of the network.

## 4 · Implications for firms

Put the two halves together - read the lead markets, then staff a footprint of purposeful sites - and a few clear management lessons fall out:

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="tiers">
    <div class="tier"><span class="t-label">Open up</span> Open the innovation <strong>value chain</strong> to global cooperation - stop treating R&D as a closed, home-country activity.</div>
    <div class="tier"><span class="t-label">Not just giants</span> This is <strong>not only for large multinationals</strong>; SMEs and start-ups can benefit too - but they need awareness that the option exists.</div>
    <div class="tier"><span class="t-label">Cheap to connect</span> <strong>Digital tools</strong> make real-time collaboration with global experts and users possible for very little cost.</div>
    <div class="tier"><span class="t-label">Master the hard parts</span> Firms must get good at <strong>knowledge management and protection</strong>, cross-site <strong>organisational processes</strong>, and <strong>intercultural teamwork</strong> - the things that quietly decide whether a footprint works.</div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Global innovation is now within reach of far more than the Triad's biggest firms - provided they can manage knowledge, process and culture across borders.</figcaption>
</div>

<Aside type="note">
**Why the "master the hard parts" line matters most.** The strategy of lead markets and value-diamond roles is the easy bit to say. What actually sinks global innovation is mundane: knowledge leaking (or not flowing), processes that don't join up across time zones, and teams that misread each other across cultures. That is precisely the coordination problem Chapter 6's transnational model is built to solve.
</Aside>

## Revision summary

<Aside type="tip">
**The one-minute recap**

- A **lead market** (Beise) is the country where a design is first **widely adopted** - adoption, not invention, triggers global **diffusion**. It only works if the market is **representative**, not idiosyncratic, so **lag markets** will follow.
- Lead markets cut **duplicated R&D** and drive **R&D internationalisation**. Classics: renewables → Germany, computer/internet → USA, automotive → Germany, robotics → Japan - but **emerging economies** (unsaturated digital markets) increasingly qualify.
- **Five lead-market advantages** (Tiwari &amp; Herstatt) combine to signal potential: **Demand** (income both ways, anticipatory needs), **Cost** (scale, factor costs), **Transfer &amp; Export** (demonstration effects, similarity, mobile users, policy convergence), **Technological** (skills, open knowledge networks), **Market-structure** (competition, mature finance, ancillary industries).
- The **value diamond** (Doz &amp; Wilson) gives every R&D site a job: **Substitution** (same work, better conditions - don't confuse low cost with low wage), **Complementarity** (unique knowledge combined - beware duplication and mini-fiefdoms), **Discovery** (frontier learning for radical innovation - beware unmeasurable value and isolation).
- **Implications:** open the value chain, include **SMEs and start-ups**, exploit cheap **digital collaboration**, and master **knowledge protection, cross-site process and intercultural teamwork**.
</Aside>
