So far we have treated innovation as something a firm does — mostly at home, in its own labs. This chapter breaks that assumption. Modern innovation is increasingly a *global* activity: R&D happens on several continents at once, knowledge is pulled in from wherever it lives, and — the twist most people don't expect — the cleverest products are sometimes designed for poor countries first and sold to rich ones afterwards. We'll cover **why** firms globalize their innovation, **how** the practice has evolved, **where** the world's knowledge now sits, and the striking idea of **reverse innovation**.

<Aside type="tip">
**The one line to remember:** globalizing innovation isn't just about building the same product a bit cheaper somewhere else. It means *opening up the innovation process itself* across national — and often organizational — boundaries, to develop products that are **better, faster and cheaper all at once**, using knowledge from wherever it happens to be.
</Aside>

## 1 · A motivating example — Mercedes-Benz R&D India

Start with a concrete puzzle. A quintessentially German engineering firm advertises hundreds of R&D and design jobs — and a large share of them are not in Stuttgart but in **Bangalore (Bengaluru), India**.

**Mercedes-Benz R&D India (MBRDI)** was founded in **1996** as a *captive unit* — a wholly-owned in-house R&D centre, not an outsourcing contractor — to support the company's research, IT and product-development work. It began with fairly basic tasks (CAD, CAE and IT programming) and steadily **moved up the value chain**: today it works on advanced vehicle engineering, control-unit software, head-unit software and critical engineering tools. It now employs **over 5,600 skilled engineers** and is **one of the largest development centres Mercedes-Benz runs outside Germany**.

Why would a company do this? Not simply to save money — a captive centre doing frontier engineering is a bet on **talent, capability and reach**, not just low wages. MBRDI is our doorway into the real question: what drives firms to spread R&D and innovation around the world at all?

## 2 · Why globalize? The BFC paradigm

The background pressure is easy to state. Peter Drucker warned decades ago that the world economy would stay "highly turbulent and highly competitive, prone to abrupt shifts", with **no single dominant economic power** and knowledge work rising in importance. In that environment, firms face a demand that used to feel contradictory — the **BFC paradigm**: develop products and services that are **B**etter, **F**aster and **C**heaper, all at the same time.

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-flow">
    <div class="sc-node green">Better<span class="sub">Quality</span></div>
    <div class="sc-node violet">Faster<span class="sub">Time</span></div>
    <div class="sc-node amber">Cheaper<span class="sub">Cost</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>The <strong>BFC paradigm</strong> — three goals that once seemed to trade off against each other must now be met together. Quality up, time-to-market down, cost down.</figcaption>
</div>

The old logic said you could have any *two* of quality, speed and cost — pick two, sacrifice the third. The BFC paradigm insists on all three. Why bother? Because getting there **improves the firm's competitive position** and **enhances profitability and stability**. And one of the most powerful ways to hit all three at once is to stop treating innovation as a single-country activity — to reach out for the best knowledge, talent and cost structures the world offers.

<Aside type="note">
**BFC echoes Blue Ocean.** Remember the value–cost trade-off from [Chapter 1](/learnings/mba/global-innovation-management/01-foundations-of-innovation-management/)? BFC is that same refusal to accept "better must cost more" — only here the lever is *global* knowledge rather than a new market space.
</Aside>

## 3 · Where in the process can globalization help?

To see *where* going global adds value, it helps to strip the innovation process down to three phases. (This is a deliberately simplified version — the full messy reality has many loops back.)

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-flow">
    <div class="sc-node amber">Conception<span class="sub">the fuzzy front end</span></div>
    <span class="arrow">→</span>
    <div class="sc-node violet">Implementation<span class="sub">build it</span></div>
    <span class="arrow">→</span>
    <div class="sc-node green">Marketing<span class="sub">take it to market</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>A simplified <strong>3-phase innovation process</strong>. Globalization can help in every phase — see the table below.</figcaption>
</div>

| Phase | What happens here | Where globalization helps |
|---|---|---|
| **Conception** | Requirement analysis, idea generation, idea evaluation, project planning | Tap ideas and unmet needs from other markets; access specialist know-how and lead-user insight abroad |
| **Implementation** | Development / construction, prototype development, pilot application, testing | Split development across time zones to shorten cycles; use lower-cost, high-skill engineering centres; test in tough local conditions |
| **Marketing** | Production, market launch and penetration (national *or* international) | Launch across many markets at once; adapt to local tastes, geography and regulation |

A Booz Allen Hamilton study made a sharp point here: the **high-leverage innovators** — the firms with the best overall performance — stand out **not by how much money they spend**, but by the **capabilities they demonstrate** in mastering the vital phases of the process. Globalizing innovation is one way to build those capabilities.

## 4 · What "Global Innovation" actually means

The word "global" gets thrown around loosely, so Tiwari gives it a precise meaning. **Global Innovation** is the *opening of the organizational innovation process across national — and in many cases across organizational — boundaries.*

Two things make this more than just "we sell abroad":

<div class="sc-split">
  <div class="sc-panel">
    <div class="head">More than product adaptation</div>
    <ul>
      <li>It is <em>not</em> merely tweaking an existing product for a foreign market</li>
      <li>It genuinely opens the <em>internal</em> innovation process — ideas, R&D, development — to outside contribution</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="sc-panel rsp">
    <div class="head">Beyond the "Triad"</div>
    <ul>
      <li>It goes past the old geographic concentration in the <strong>Triad</strong>: North America, Western Europe and Japan</li>
      <li>Knowledge and R&D now flow to and from major developing economies too</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

<Aside type="note">
**"Triad"** (a term from international-business theory) = the three traditional centres of economic and technological power: **North America, Western Europe and Japan**. For most of the 20th century, serious R&D happened almost entirely inside this triangle. The whole story of this chapter is how that stopped being true.
</Aside>

## 5 · How it all developed — from international business to global innovation

Global innovation didn't arrive overnight. It's the last step in a long evolution, each stage widening what a firm sends abroad — first goods, then R&D, then the innovation process itself.

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-flow">
    <div class="sc-node slate">Internationalization of Business<span class="sub">~1900 – 1990s</span></div>
    <span class="arrow">→</span>
    <div class="sc-node amber">Internationalization of R&D<span class="sub">~1900 – 1990s</span></div>
    <span class="arrow">→</span>
    <div class="sc-node violet">Globalization of R&D<span class="sub">1990s – ~2005</span></div>
    <span class="arrow">→</span>
    <div class="sc-node green">Globalization of Innovation<span class="sub">since ~2005</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>The evolution (Tiwari &amp; Herstatt). Firms first sold and produced abroad, then set up scattered R&D outposts, then genuinely <em>globalized</em> R&D, and finally opened the whole innovation process worldwide.</figcaption>
</div>

The decisive move is the **qualitative shift** from "internationalization of R&D" to "globalization of innovation" — from a Triad-centred world to one that actively draws on major developing economies. Firms make this shift because they increasingly need **global knowledge resources** to:

- **shorten development cycles** — hand work between time zones so development runs almost around the clock;
- **reduce development costs** — access high-skill engineering at lower cost;
- **develop products for differing customer tastes, geographic conditions or regulatory requirements** — you can't design for India from a desk in Germany;
- **gain access to important "lead markets"** — being present where an innovation is first adopted reduces risk and sharpens learning. (We give lead markets their own chapter next.)

<Aside type="caution">
**This is now a necessity, not a luxury.** For many firms — including small and medium-sized ones, not just giant multinationals — global innovation activity has become a *requirement* for staying competitive, not a nice-to-have side project.
</Aside>

## 6 · Where the world's knowledge now sits — S&T clusters

If knowledge is the raw material of innovation, the obvious question is: *where is it?* The answer has changed dramatically. **Science & Technology (S&T) clusters** — entire regions or cities dense with universities, brilliant scientists, R&D-intensive companies and prolific inventors — are the backbone of any national innovation ecosystem. It's the *collaboration* among those players, packed close together, that produces breakthroughs.

The **Global Innovation Index (GII) 2024** maps the top 100 such clusters, and the headline is blunt: **the world's five biggest S&T clusters are all in East Asia.** Knowledge creation is no longer confined to the Triad.

| Rank | Cluster | Economy |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Tokyo–Yokohama | Japan |
| 2 | Shenzhen–Hong Kong–Guangzhou | China / Hong Kong |
| 3 | Beijing | China |
| 4 | Seoul | Republic of Korea |
| 5 | Shanghai–Suzhou | China |
| 6 | San Jose–San Francisco (Silicon Valley) | United States |

To put the concentration in perspective: **Tokyo–Yokohama and Shenzhen–Hong Kong–Guangzhou together account for almost one in every five** international patent (PCT) applications filed globally. Other well-known clusters — Cambridge, Munich and Paris in Europe; Bengaluru in India — sit further down the same top-100 list. The centre of gravity of invention has genuinely moved east.

<Aside type="note">
**PCT** = Patent Cooperation Treaty, the route firms use to file one international patent application covering many countries at once. Counting PCT filings by location is a standard way to see *where* inventive work is actually happening.
</Aside>

### Two flows, not one

Globalization of R&D runs in **both directions** at once, and the patent data shows it clearly. Look at two mirror-image measures for 2018:

<div class="sc-split">
  <div class="sc-panel">
    <div class="head">Domestic firms' overseas R&D <small>inventions made abroad, owned at home</small></div>
    <ul>
      <li>Germany — 18.8%</li>
      <li>USA — 15.4%</li>
      <li>India — 8.1%</li>
      <li>China — 4.2%</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="sc-panel rsp">
    <div class="head">Foreign firms' domestic R&D <small>local inventions owned by foreigners</small></div>
    <ul>
      <li>India — 43.2%</li>
      <li>Germany — 18.3%</li>
      <li>USA — 13.2%</li>
      <li>China — 9.1%</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

Read the two panels together and the picture jumps out. German and US firms increasingly invent *abroad* (left). Meanwhile a striking **43%** of inventions made *in India* are owned by *foreign* firms (right) — exactly the MBRDI story, multiplied across many companies. R&D has become a genuinely two-way, borderless flow.

## 7 · A taxonomy of the globalization of technology

To keep all this straight, Archibugi and Michie offer a clean three-way taxonomy. It sorts global technology activity by *what* is being globalized and *who* does it.

<div class="tiers">
  <div class="tier">
    <div class="tier-h">Global <em>exploitation</em> of technology</div>
    <div class="tier-b"><strong>Who:</strong> individual firms — especially R&D-intensive ones based in small countries. <strong>What:</strong> taking technology developed at home and using / selling it worldwide to earn a return on the R&D.</div>
  </div>
  <div class="tier">
    <div class="tier-h">Global technological <em>collaboration</em></div>
    <div class="tier-b"><strong>Who:</strong> firms, governments and the public sector together. <strong>What:</strong> partnering across borders — joint ventures, alliances, shared research programmes — to create technology jointly.</div>
  </div>
  <div class="tier">
    <div class="tier-h">Global <em>generation</em> of technology</div>
    <div class="tier-b"><strong>Who:</strong> multinational enterprises (MNEs). <strong>What:</strong> actually <em>producing</em> new technology in several countries — the deepest form, where R&D itself is spread worldwide.</div>
  </div>
</div>

Two caveats worth remembering. First, this taxonomy is often relabelled the **"internationalization of innovation"** and has been widely reused. Second, globalizing innovation is **often — but not necessarily — linked with foreign direct investment (FDI)**: building a lab abroad usually means investing abroad, but not always (collaboration and licensing can globalize technology without an FDI stake).

### How real firms choose R&D locations

The taxonomy is abstract; the location logic is concrete. Two examples show the same instinct — *put R&D where the relevant knowledge and users are*.

| Firm | R&D-location logic (in their words) |
|---|---|
| **Sony** (Japan) | Has R&D sites around the world to do research and development "in environments closer to users while understanding their needs" — proximity to users drives placement. |
| **SAP** (Germany) | A global footprint of 20 **SAP Labs** in 17 countries; the guiding theme is *"applying local strengths to build global solutions"* — each lab contributes its local strength, best practices are shared across the network. |

SAP's phrase is the neat summary of the whole idea: **local strengths → global solutions**. A site is not just a cheaper pair of hands; it's a source of distinctive knowledge fed back into the whole organization.

## 8 · Reverse innovation — from East to West

Here is the part that overturns intuition. The traditional assumption is that innovation flows **downhill**: rich countries invent advanced products, then sell simplified, cheaper versions to poorer markets. **Reverse innovation** flips the direction. You innovate *for* an emerging market first — designing from scratch around its constraints — and then bring that product *back* to the rich world, where it turns out to have appeal too.

<Aside type="tip">
**The crucial distinction:** reverse innovation is **not a stripped-down version** of an existing premium product. Firms keep discovering they *cannot* simply sell a cut-down model in emerging markets — those customers have genuinely different needs. Reverse innovation means designing afresh around those needs, and the result is often good enough (and cheap enough) to disrupt the home market too.
</Aside>

### Example 1 — GE's MAC-i portable ECG

GE developed the **MAC-i** electrocardiograph (ECG — the machine that records the heart's electrical activity) in India, aimed at doctors in rural areas and small towns. It was designed around the hard realities of that setting:

<div class="sc-split">
  <div class="sc-panel">
    <div class="head">Designed for the reality</div>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Portable</strong> — light enough for a rural doctor to carry between clinics</li>
      <li><strong>Simple</strong> — few buttons, so a nurse or assistant with limited training can operate it</li>
      <li><strong>Robust</strong> — runs ~500 ECGs on one charge, because power supply in remote areas is erratic</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="sc-panel rsp">
    <div class="head">The price gap</div>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>GE MAC-i:</strong> about <strong>US$535</strong></li>
      <li><strong>GE MAC 5500</strong> (next range up): about <strong>US$10,000</strong></li>
      <li>Roughly <strong>10× cheaper</strong> — a difference in kind, not degree</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

Here's the reverse-innovation punchline: GE now sells many of these ECG instruments — **originally conceived for India** — successfully in **France**. A device built for erratic-power rural clinics found a market in a wealthy European country.

### Example 2 — GE's "Lullaby" baby warmer

GE went further and designed a **baby warmer, "Lullaby", in India (2009)** for the local market. The motivation was partly a stark welfare problem and partly a huge market:

| Design driver | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Welfare need** | Infant mortality in India ~55 per 1,000 births (vs ~6 in the US) |
| **Market size** | ~35 million births a year |
| **Ease of use** | Visually-coded control panel; intuitive for first-time users |
| **Robustness** | Built to survive heavy use, power cuts, heat and dust |
| **Efficiency** | ~60% less power on start-up |
| **Price** | ~US$3,000 — GE warmers in the US start at ~US$12,000 |

The result went global: Lullaby is now **sold in 62 countries**, including Brazil, Russia, Egypt, the UAE and Italy. A product designed for Indian hospitals became a worldwide line.

### Example 3 — Gillette Guard

The same thinking isn't limited to high-tech gear. P&G developed the **Gillette Guard** razor specifically for India — a genuinely re-thought, low-cost, robust design for local users and their needs, rather than a discounted version of a Western razor. It's a reminder that reverse and frugal innovation apply to everyday consumer goods just as much as to medical devices.

<Aside type="caution">
**Why incumbents should worry.** Reverse innovation isn't only an opportunity — it's a threat. **New competitors from China and India** are increasingly using their emerging-market products as a base to **diversify into industrialised markets**. A firm that dismisses a cheap emerging-market product as "not for us" may meet it again, upgraded, in its own home market. (This is the seam that connects straight to [frugal innovation](/learnings/mba/global-innovation-management/07-frugal-innovation-concept/).)
</Aside>

## 9 · The challenges — What? / How? / Where?

None of this is easy. Opening the innovation process across borders creates real management headaches. A clean way to organise them is by the three questions a manager keeps asking — **What are we doing? How do we run it? Where do we put it?**

<div class="tiers">
  <div class="tier">
    <div class="tier-h">What? — the substance</div>
    <div class="tier-b">
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Gaining and retaining global expertise &amp; know-how</strong> — both the people and the intellectual-property rights (IPRs) that protect what they create</li>
        <li><strong>Financial constraints</strong> as costs keep rising</li>
        <li><strong>Market uncertainties</strong> — foreign markets' peculiarities, size and demand are hard to read</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="tier">
    <div class="tier-h">How? — running it</div>
    <div class="tier-b">
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Intra-organizational process management</strong> — coordinating an innovation process that now spans continents and time zones</li>
        <li><strong>Forming and managing cooperation</strong> — building and sustaining partnerships and alliances</li>
        <li><strong>Intercultural conflicts</strong> — different working cultures colliding inside one project</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="tier">
    <div class="tier-h">Where? — placing it</div>
    <div class="tier-b">
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Identification of suitable locations</strong> — picking the right sites for the right knowledge (the subject of the next chapter's site-role framework)</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

The upshot for firms: they must **open their innovation value chains** to global cooperation, and this is **not just a big-MNE game** — SMEs and start-ups can benefit too, especially now that digital tools enable real-time collaboration with global experts and users at very low cost. But the prize only comes to those who master **knowledge protection, cross-border process management and intercultural teamwork**.

## Revision summary

<Aside type="tip">
**The one-minute recap**

- **Mercedes-Benz R&D India (MBRDI)** — a 1996 captive centre in Bengaluru, now 5,600+ engineers doing frontier work — shows globalizing R&D is about **talent and capability**, not just low wages.
- The **BFC paradigm** demands products that are **Better, Faster and Cheaper** at once (Quality, Time, Cost) — and global innovation is a key way to hit all three.
- Simplify the process to **Conception → Implementation → Marketing**; globalization adds value in every phase, and top innovators win on **capability, not spending**.
- **Global Innovation** (Tiwari) = opening the innovation process across national — and often organizational — boundaries, going *beyond* product adaptation and *beyond the Triad* (North America, Western Europe, Japan).
- The evolution ran **Internationalization of Business → of R&D → Globalization of R&D → Globalization of Innovation**; firms go global to shorten cycles, cut costs, adapt to local conditions and reach lead markets.
- The world's **top S&T clusters are now largely East Asian** (Tokyo–Yokohama, Shenzhen–HK–Guangzhou, Beijing, Seoul, Shanghai–Suzhou; then Silicon Valley) — knowledge creation has left the Triad, and R&D flows both ways (e.g. 43% of Indian inventions foreign-owned).
- Archibugi & Michie's taxonomy: global **exploitation** (firms), **collaboration** (firms + governments), **generation** (MNEs) — often, but not always, tied to **FDI**; firms like **Sony** and **SAP** place R&D where users and local strengths are.
- **Reverse innovation** (East → West) designs *for* emerging markets first — not a stripped-down product — then sells to rich ones: GE's **MAC-i** ECG (US$535 vs ~US$10,000), GE's **Lullaby** warmer (now in 62 countries), the **Gillette Guard**. New Chinese/Indian rivals use the same route to enter industrialised markets.
- Challenges sort into **What? / How? / Where?** — expertise & IP, finance and market uncertainty; process, cooperation and intercultural conflict; and choosing the right locations.
</Aside>
