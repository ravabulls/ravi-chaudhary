If innovation is the *what*, this chapter is the *where* and the *why now*. Before a firm can decide how to spread its research, launch products or chase new customers, it needs a clear picture of the world it is innovating **into** - a world that has been knitting itself together for decades, but that in the last few years started asking hard questions about whether that stitching should be undone. This chapter walks through what globalization actually is, what has been pushing it along, why people are now talking about *de*-globalization, and the slow, unstoppable **megatrends** that will decide what firms have to innovate for over the next fifteen-plus years.

<Aside type="tip">
**The one idea to hold onto:** globalization is *economic integration* - many formerly national economies fusing into one global economy, so that distance and borders matter less. But it is not just about trade; it is also social, political and cultural. And it is not a one-way street - it can slow, wobble, and partly reverse.
</Aside>

## 1 · What globalization actually means

The textbook definition of **economic globalization** is the integration of many formerly separate national economies into a single global economy, in a way that has substantially *shrunk the impact of distance and national borders*. Goods, money, people and ideas cross frontiers that used to stop them. When economists want to *measure* how globalized a country is, they lean on three economic indicators:

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-flow">
    <div class="sc-node amber">International trade<span class="sub">goods &amp; services across borders</span></div>
    <span class="arrow">→</span>
    <div class="sc-node green">Foreign direct investment<span class="sub">FDI - owning/building abroad</span></div>
    <span class="arrow">→</span>
    <div class="sc-node violet">Portfolio investment<span class="sub">cross-border shares &amp; bonds</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>The three economic indicators of globalization. <strong>FDI (foreign direct investment)</strong> means a firm building or buying a lasting stake in another country - a factory, a subsidiary - as opposed to just buying shares (that's portfolio investment).</figcaption>
</div>

But money flows are only half the story. Globalization also has **social** dimensions (international tourism, migration, cross-border friendships), **political** dimensions (international treaties, memberships, embassies) and **cultural** dimensions (the same films, brands and ideas everywhere). The best-known attempt to roll all of this into one number is the **KOF Globalisation Index**, produced in Switzerland, which scores countries on their economic, social and political openness.

A couple of results from the KOF Index worth remembering, because they are slightly counter-intuitive:

| Rank (2023 KOF Index) | Country | Takeaway |
|---|---|---|
| Top 15 | Small, open European economies | The *most* globalized places are often small - they have to trade to survive |
| 25th | **USA** | The biggest economy is not the most globalized - a huge home market means less need to look outward |
| 41st / 46th | Japan / Mexico | Large economies can still be relatively inward-facing |
| Outside top 50 | **China & India** | The two demographic giants score *low* - enormous internal markets, and plenty of remaining barriers |

<Aside type="note">
**Why do China and India rank so low?** It looks strange until you remember what the index measures: *openness relative to size*. A country with a billion-plus people can do an enormous amount of business *inside its own borders*, so trade and investment as a share of its economy stay modest. Being a giant is not the same as being globalized.
</Aside>

## 2 · The rise of the emerging economies

The centre of economic gravity has been moving. For most of the twentieth century a handful of **advanced (developed) economies** dominated global output. But the share of **emerging market and developing economies** in world GDP has climbed steadily for decades - and is expected to keep climbing. (In the IMF's split, that's roughly 39 advanced economies versus 154 emerging and developing ones - so the "rich club" was always the numerical minority.)

You can see the same shift in individual industries. Car production is the classic example: for years, output drained away from the old heartlands of Europe, North America and Japan toward developing-world producers - China above all. The interesting twist the lecture flags is that this may now be *changing again* as costs, politics and technology shift.

But the cleanest way to see the tilt is **FDI stock** - the accumulated stake that foreign investors hold *inside* a region. Track it from 1980 to 2022 and the developing world's slice roughly doubles:

| Region | 1980 share | 2000 share | 2020 share | 2022 share |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **World** (total) | 100% ($701 bn) | 100% ($7,381 bn) | 100% ($41,354 bn) | 100% ($44,253 bn) |
| **Developed world** | 58% | 78% | 71% | **66%** |
| **Developing world**\* | 42% | 22% | 29% | **34%** |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;- Africa | 6% | 2% | 2% | 2% |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;- Asia | 30% | 14% | 21% | **26%** |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;- Americas | 5% | 3% | 5% | 6% |

<div class="sc-fig">
  <figcaption>Inward FDI stock, current US dollars, per UNCTAD (2023); figures rounded. \*"Developing world" here also folds in the transition economies of Eastern Europe. Notice the <strong>U-shape</strong>: the developing share <em>fell</em> through the 1980s-90s as rich-world investment boomed, then <strong>surged back</strong> - Asia alone is now a quarter of the world total.</figcaption>
</div>

<Aside type="caution">
**Read the U-shape carefully.** The developing world's share dipped to 22% in 2000 *not* because it was shrinking, but because FDI into the developed world was exploding faster. The recent rebound to 34% is the real signal - and almost all of it is **Asia**, whose slice jumped from 21% to 26% in just two years. That is where the customers, the factories and increasingly the R&D are heading.
</Aside>

## 3 · What drives globalization

Why did the world integrate in the first place? The lecture groups the drivers into four buckets - a neat 2×2 of the *general climate*, the *supply side*, the *demand side*, and the *competitive pressure*. All four push in the same direction:

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-quad">
    <div class="q blue"><div class="qh">General conditions <small>the enabling climate</small></div><ul><li>Deregulation - free-trade rules via <strong>GATT / WTO</strong></li><li>Opening of formerly planned economies</li><li>Technological progress (transport, telecoms, IT)</li><li>More international cooperation between states</li></ul></div>
    <div class="q violet"><div class="qh">Supply conditions <small>the cost side</small></div><ul><li>Experience-curve economies - bigger volume, lower unit cost</li><li>High R&D expenditure to recover over a wider market</li><li>Shorter product life cycles - sell everywhere, fast</li></ul></div>
    <div class="q amber"><div class="qh">Demand conditions <small>the customer side</small></div><ul><li>Saturation of demand in the home market</li><li>Growing demand from emerging markets</li><li>Homogenization of demand - similar tastes worldwide (?!)</li></ul></div>
    <div class="q green"><div class="qh">Competitive conditions <small>the rivalry side</small></div><ul><li>Intensifying global competition</li><li>Imitation &amp; parallel developments abroad</li><li>The pull of <strong>first-mover advantage</strong></li></ul></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>The four driver groups. Read it as a chain: liberal rules and technology <em>make</em> globalization possible; scale economies and R&D costs <em>reward</em> it; saturated home markets and hungry emerging ones <em>demand</em> it; and rivals doing the same thing <em>force</em> it.</figcaption>
</div>

A few of these deserve a second look:

- **GATT / WTO** - the *General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade* and its successor the *World Trade Organization* are the rulebooks that steadily knocked down tariffs and quotas after 1947. Deregulation is the single biggest "general condition" enabler.
- **Experience-curve economies** - the more units you make, the cheaper each one gets (you learn). Selling globally lets you ride that curve far further than a national market allows.
- **Shorter product life cycles** - when a product is obsolete in eighteen months, you cannot afford to roll it out country-by-country over years; you launch worldwide to earn back the R&D before it dates.
- **Homogenization of demand** - the idea (Levitt's old thesis) that tastes converge, so one global product fits all. The lecture's own "(?!?)" is a healthy warning: it is only *partly* true, and Chapters 5-7 on local adaptation and frugal innovation push back hard on it.

## 4 · The de-globalization debate - is the tide turning?

For most of my lifetime the arrow pointed one way: more trade, more integration, more global value chains. Then came a run of shocks - trade wars, Brexit, and above all **Covid-19** - that made serious people ask out loud whether globalization had peaked. The *Wall Street Journal* and *The Economist* were running headlines like "Will Covid kill globalisation?" The honest answer is: it bent, it did not break - but the questions are real. The lecture frames the debate around four sharp prompts:

<div class="sc-split">
  <div class="sc-panel">
    <div class="head">The case for de-globalization</div>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Covid shock</strong> - lockdowns snapped supply chains and exposed how fragile "just-in-time, from far away" really is</li>
      <li><strong>"World's factory" wobble</strong> - even before Covid, rising costs and tighter regulation pushed factories from China toward <strong>Vietnam &amp; Malaysia</strong>; prolonged lockdowns then dented the business world's trust</li>
      <li><strong>Geopolitics</strong> - tariffs, sanctions and a push to "re-shore" or "friend-shore" strategic production back home</li>
      <li><strong>Resilience over efficiency</strong> - firms now willing to pay more for supply chains that don't collapse</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="sc-panel rsp">
    <div class="head">The case that it's overstated</div>
    <ul>
      <li>Connectedness indices (e.g. <strong>DHL's Global Connectedness Index</strong>) kept showing trade and data flows resilient, even through the pandemic</li>
      <li>Value chains are <em>sticky</em> - decades of investment, skills and supplier networks can't be relocated overnight</li>
      <li>Emerging-market demand keeps growing - the customers are still abroad</li>
      <li>What's happening is more a <em>re-wiring</em> (diversifying away from single-country risk) than a genuine retreat</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

Two of the discussion prompts are worth chewing on because they cut to the strategic core:

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-flow">
    <div class="sc-node slate">Is the <strong>G-7</strong> still relevant?<span class="sub">seven rich democracies as "global governance"</span></div>
    <span class="arrow">→</span>
    <div class="sc-node soft">…when China, India &amp; others sit outside it<span class="sub">and now out-produce several members</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>The <strong>G-7</strong> (US, Japan, Germany, UK, France, Italy, Canada) was built when those economies <em>were</em> the world. As economic weight shifts to the emerging giants, the question is whether a club that excludes them can still "govern" the global economy at all.</figcaption>
</div>

And the sharpest test case of all - **how dependent is a firm like Apple on its global value chain, and could it unwind that quickly?** The honest answer is *no, not quickly*. Apple's product is designed in California but assembled through a vast, finely-tuned network concentrated in China, drawing components from dozens of countries. That network took twenty years and enormous supplier investment to build; the specialised tooling, skilled labour and logistics simply do not exist at that scale anywhere else *yet*. A firm can *diversify* - add capacity in India or Vietnam, dual-source key parts - but "reduce dependence rapidly" runs straight into the stickiness that makes value chains so hard to move. That tension between **efficiency** (concentrate where it's cheapest) and **resilience** (spread the risk) is the defining supply-chain question of the decade.

<Aside type="note">
**My one-line read on the debate:** globalization isn't dying, it's *de-risking*. Firms aren't abandoning global chains - they're trading a little efficiency for a lot of resilience, spreading bets across more countries so that no single lockdown, tariff or earthquake can stop the line.
</Aside>

## 5 · Global challenges → global goals

Zoom out from trade, and the world faces a set of shared problems that no single country can solve alone - the backdrop against which *all* future innovation happens:

| Challenge | The squeeze | Why it matters for firms |
|---|---|---|
| **Climate change** | Warming, extreme weather, decarbonization pressure | Regulation, carbon costs, and demand for clean tech |
| **Resource scarcity** | Finite water, minerals, energy, rare earths | Material costs, circular-economy pressure, supply risk |
| **Growing population** | Billions more people to feed, house and employ | Huge new demand - but only if it can be served affordably |
| **Feeding everyone** | Producing more food on the same (or less) land | Agri-tech, food innovation, sustainable production |

The lecture's neat reframe is that these are not only problems - they are **goals**. The United Nations turned exactly this list into the **Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)** - 17 global targets (no poverty, clean water, climate action, and so on) agreed for 2030. Flipping "problem" into "goal" is not just optimism; for an innovation manager, every one of those goals is also a **market** - a place where a genuinely better, affordable solution can create enormous value.

## 6 · Trends vs megatrends

Now to the engine of the second half of this chapter. Not every change matters equally, so it helps to be precise:

<div class="sc-split">
  <div class="sc-panel">
    <div class="head">Trend</div>
    <ul>
      <li>A recognizable change in the societal environment</li>
      <li>Can be short-lived, local, or niche</li>
      <li>A fashion, a fad, a shift in one market</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="sc-panel rsp">
    <div class="head">Megatrend</div>
    <ul>
      <li>A <strong>long-term, comprehensive transformation</strong> of society itself</li>
      <li>Reshapes politics, economics <em>and</em> social life together</li>
      <li>Slow, deep, and almost impossible to reverse</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

The standard (Z-punkt) definition says a megatrend differs from an ordinary trend on **three dimensions** - and all three have to hold:

<div class="sc-fig">
  <table class="matrix">
    <thead>
      <tr><th>Dimension</th><th>What it means</th><th>The test</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td><strong>Time horizon</strong></td><td>It has been observable for decades and rests on measurable, empirical indicators</td><td>Projectable into the future for <strong>at least 15 years</strong></td></tr>
      <tr><td><strong>Range</strong></td><td>Not confined to one region or one group</td><td><strong>Comprehensive &amp; supra-regional</strong> - it shows up across borders</td></tr>
      <tr><td><strong>Impact</strong></td><td>Multidimensional upheaval of all social subsystems - political, social, economic</td><td>Deep effect on the behaviour &amp; strategy of <strong>every actor</strong> - governments, individuals and firms</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>The three tests of a megatrend. The point of the strict definition is planning: because a megatrend is projectable ~15+ years out, a firm can actually <em>build strategy around it</em> - unlike a fad, which is gone before the product ships.</figcaption>
</div>

## 7 · The megatrends of today

So what are the megatrends a global innovation manager has to plan for right now? Four stand out in the lecture, with two cross-cutting shifts running underneath them all:

<div class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-quad">
    <div class="q blue"><div class="qh">Ageing societies <small>demographics</small></div><ul><li>Rich countries are getting old fast - shrinking workforces, more retirees</li><li>Demand shifts toward health, care, accessibility</li></ul></div>
    <div class="q violet"><div class="qh">Digital transformation <small>technology</small></div><ul><li>Data, AI, connectivity and automation reshaping every industry</li><li>New business models, new skills, new competitors</li></ul></div>
    <div class="q amber"><div class="qh">New emerging powerhouses <small>economic gravity</small></div><ul><li><strong>China &amp; India</strong> as innovation centres, not just cheap labour</li><li>Vast new middle-class markets - and new rivals</li></ul></div>
    <div class="q green"><div class="qh">Ecological sustainability <small>the planet</small></div><ul><li>No longer optional - a hard constraint on how firms operate</li><li>Circular economy, clean energy, decarbonized supply chains</li></ul></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Today's four megatrends. Underneath them run two more shifts the lecture highlights: a likely <strong>move of economic gravity</strong> toward Asia, and broad <strong>demographic change</strong> - young, growing populations in the developing world alongside ageing in the rich one.</figcaption>
</div>

Two of these interlock in a way worth spelling out. The **shift in economic gravity** and **demographic change** are really the same story from two angles: the rich world is *ageing and shrinking* while much of the developing world is *young and growing*. That means the future's workers **and** the future's customers increasingly live in the same places - the emerging powerhouses. A firm that keeps designing only for wealthy, ageing home markets is aiming at the part of the map that is contracting.

<Aside type="tip">
**Megatrends are the opposite of hype.** By definition they move slowly and are visible for decades - which is exactly why they are so easy to *under*-react to. The competitor who took ageing, digital, Asia and sustainability seriously ten years early is the one eating your lunch today.
</Aside>

## 8 · So what - implications for innovation management

Pull it together and the strategic message is direct: these shifts change both **what** firms must innovate and **where** they must do it.

<div class="sc-fig">
  <table class="matrix">
    <thead>
      <tr><th>The shift</th><th>What it changes for innovation</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td>Emerging economies rising</td><td><strong>Where</strong> the growth is - innovate <em>for</em> and increasingly <em>in</em> the developing world, not just export to it</td></tr>
      <tr><td>De-risking of value chains</td><td>Design for <strong>resilience</strong>, not only lowest cost - flexible, multi-country sourcing and production</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Ageing societies</td><td>New needs - health, care, accessible &amp; simple products for older users</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Digital transformation</td><td>Digital <strong>is</strong> the product now - data-driven services, platforms, AI-enabled everything</td></tr>
      <tr><td>New powerhouses (China, India)</td><td>Innovate <em>with</em> these markets - they set requirements and increasingly supply the technology</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Sustainability as necessity</td><td>Green is a <strong>constraint and an opportunity</strong> - circular design, clean tech, the SDGs as markets</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>Every megatrend is also an innovation brief. The firms that thrive read these shifts as demand signals - and organise their global R&D to answer them.</figcaption>
</div>

That last point - organising R&D *globally* to answer these shifts - is exactly where the next chapter picks up. Once you accept that the customers, the talent and the growth are spreading across the world, the obvious question becomes: should your innovation stay at home, or should it spread out too?

## Revision summary

<Aside type="tip">
**The one-minute recap**

- **Globalization = economic integration** - formerly national economies fusing into one, so distance and borders matter less. Measured by trade, **FDI** and portfolio investment (the **KOF Index**) - plus social, political and cultural dimensions. Big markets like the USA, China and India rank surprisingly *low* because openness is measured relative to size.
- **Emerging economies are rising** - their share of world GDP keeps climbing, and **FDI stock** in the developing world roughly doubled from ~22% (2000) to **34% (2022)**, driven above all by **Asia**.
- **Four driver groups** push globalization: *general* (GATT/WTO, opening economies, technology), *supply* (experience curve, high R&D, short life cycles), *demand* (home saturation, emerging-market demand, homogenization), *competitive* (global rivalry, imitation, first-mover advantage).
- The **de-globalization debate** (Covid, the "world's factory" wobble, G-7 relevance, Apple's sticky value chain) is real but overstated - it's **de-risking**, not retreat: trading a little efficiency for resilience.
- **Global challenges = global goals** - climate, resources, population and food, reframed as the UN **SDGs**, are also markets.
- A **megatrend** (vs a trend) is long-term, comprehensive and deep on three tests - **time horizon** (~15+ years), **range** (supra-regional), **impact** (all actors). Today's: **ageing societies, digital transformation, new powerhouses (China & India), sustainability** - plus shifting economic gravity and demographic change. Each one is an innovation brief for *what* and *where* to innovate.
</Aside>
