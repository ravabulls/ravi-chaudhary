*Google Project Management Certificate · Course 6: Applying Project Management in the Real World*

---

Courses 1 to 5 taught the concepts: the four phases of the project life cycle, goals and scope, budgets and schedules, risk, quality, communication, and the Agile and Scrum methodologies. This capstone course teaches almost nothing new. Instead it drops all of it into one continuous scenario and asks whether the concepts can actually be applied when the information arrives the way it does in real life, scattered across a proposal, a stack of meeting transcripts, email threads and a page of team bios.

The scenario is **Sauce and Spoon**, a small restaurant group piloting tabletop ordering tablets at two of its five locations. The course follows **Peta**, the group's newly hired first in-house project manager, through the whole life cycle. She has five years of project management experience and no restaurant experience at all, which is the point the instructor keeps returning to: strong project management skills usually transfer without subject matter expertise. Dan, the instructor, is a program manager at Google working on responsible practices for AI and machine learning, and he came to that role from teaching maths and coordinating sponsorship for a football club.

Week 1 covers the initiation phase end to end. The output is a finished **project charter** plus a **stakeholder analysis**, built up across five activities, and the skills practised alongside them are analysing documents for project details, negotiating scope, and influencing people who do not report to you.

<Aside type="note">
**Where this sits:** the course calls the documents it produces **artifacts**, and recommends keeping every template and completed version in one folder, because they double as portfolio evidence in a job interview. The concepts themselves (charter, scope, SMART, stakeholder power and interest) were all defined in earlier courses; this page records how they behave when applied to a messy scenario. Activities are described inline in the section where they occur, never as downloads.

## The Sauce and Spoon scenario

Sauce and Spoon is a local restaurant chain built on scratch-to-table cuisine and partnerships with local farmers and suppliers. Its mission, as the proposal states it, is to enrich the community: fresh quick food for working families, customer needs first, an efficient and profitable business model that lets the group keep growing, and support for local businesses through sourcing.

### What the project proposal says

The proposal was written by **Deanna Coleman**, the Director of Operations, and it is the primary document Peta starts from. Its structure is executive summary, opportunity statement, project overview, proposed next steps and timeline, and the company OKRs.

<div class="sc-chips">
  <span class="sc-chip green">Annual sales growth of 28 percent</span>
  <span class="sc-chip amber">Order wait time up 20 percent</span>
  <span class="sc-chip amber">Front-of-house turnover of 50 percent</span>
  <span class="sc-chip slate">Profit margin flat year over year</span>
</div>

The opportunity statement is unusually candid. In its first three years the group beat revenue goals without losing quality, but it burned its staff out, lost half its front-of-house people, made expensive human errors in ticketing and barely onboarded new employees. Today there are more locations and more sales, yet no more efficient procedures: orders take longer to arrive, negative reviews cite delay in service, and despite rising sales the margin has not moved.

The proposed remedy is a guest-facing digital menu and point-of-sale system, in the form of an integrated tablet at each table, giving guests a seamless ordering experience and the restaurant a more reliable ticketing system. The stated next steps are simply to scope the project, write a charter, and establish a pilot plan.

### The pilot

| Decision | What was chosen | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Locations** | Sauce and Spoon **North** and **Downtown**, two of five | Each has the right staff size and guest volume to test the concept |
| **Section** | The **bar area** only, at both sites | Bar guests want a faster experience; one section keeps training and problems contained |
| **Features needed** | Menu item add-on suggestions plus coupons | Supports the company-wide goal of increasing product mix |
| **Timing** | Launch at the start of Q2, ideally sooner | January and February are the quiet months |
| **Measurement** | Through the end of Q2, meaning end of June | Gives a full quarter of pilot data before any rollout decision |

Two pieces of restaurant vocabulary Peta has to ask about, both of which become project metrics. **Table turn time** is how long a party occupies a table, including the time to reset it for the next party: currently about 95 minutes in most sections and 80 minutes at the bar. **Comping** means not charging, and the current policy is to remake any meal a guest reports as wrong, which is where much of the food waste comes from. The other two terms that recur are **FOH** (front of house, servers and hosts) and **BOH** (back of house, kitchen staff, bussers and runners).

## The 5 Ws and the initiation guiding questions

The phase reading is a checklist compiled by Google project managers for exactly this situation: a new project, a new domain, and stakeholders who have not thought in project terms.

<div class="sc-chips">
  <span class="sc-chip amber">What will be done</span>
  <span class="sc-chip amber">Why undertake it</span>
  <span class="sc-chip violet">Who benefits</span>
  <span class="sc-chip green">When is it due</span>
  <span class="sc-chip slate">Where will it happen</span>
</div>

**What** are the actual steps. **Why** names the problem being solved and how the deliverables help. **Who** asks who really benefits: customers, stakeholders or employees. **When** is the deadline, if there is one, since not all projects have one. **Where** means locally, internationally or across many sites.

Two tips go with it: ask **three stakeholders from three different areas** to describe the project to you, then synthesise those conversations into a single project summary. If three people describe three different projects, that is the misalignment surfacing early.

### Questions for each charter section

| Area | Guiding questions from the reading |
| --- | --- |
| **Goals** | What are the success metrics? What do team members expect from the project? What do stakeholders want from it? |
| **Scope** | Who is the project delivered to, and who is the end user? What is its size and complexity? What is the timeline, the budget, the resources required? |
| **Costs** | What are the biggest risks? What money, time, people and supplies will it take, and where do they come from? What is the cost of **not** solving the problem? |
| **Benefits** | What is the biggest problem being solved, and how does the project solve it? How do you define success or completion? What are the direct and indirect benefits to users and the organisation? |
| **Stakeholders** | What are your priorities and goals, and how does this project align with them? What role would you like to play? Who else should I reach out to? Here is how I plan to keep you informed, does that work? |

The scope tips are the ones that matter most later: put scope in writing at the start and get every stakeholder to sign off, refer back to what was agreed whenever someone suggests adding more, challenge anything that looks like **scope creep** by naming it as such and asking whether stakeholders agree, and if stakeholders disagree on scope, facilitate a conversation towards common ground rather than deciding for them.

The stakeholder tips add two practical moves: read the organisational chart of your company and of any supplier you work with, researching titles you do not recognise, and for a senior stakeholder, approach their administrative business partner or someone on their team first to learn what role that person may actually want before asking for an introduction.

## The project charter

<Aside type="note">
**Project charter** - a formal document that clearly defines the project and outlines the details needed to reach its goals. The project manager writes it during initiation, the first phase of the life cycle.
</Aside>

The reading describes four things it does. It organises vital project information in a **skimmable** format so busy senior stakeholders have visibility and a chance to give feedback. It ensures everyone agrees what success is. It is a **living document**, continually updated, serving as the central source of truth. And it is shared with stakeholders who must **approve** it before the project can advance to planning.

### The six sections

| Section | What it holds |
| --- | --- |
| **1. Project summary** | A high-level overview of the project and its aims. One to three sentences, brief and to the point |
| **2. Project goals** | The desired results of the project, agreed between stakeholders and the project manager. They answer why we are doing this |
| **3. Deliverables** | The specific tasks and tangible or intangible outcomes that let the team meet the goals |
| **4. Scope and exclusion** | The boundaries, split into in-scope and out-of-scope. Details that do not contribute to the goals are out of scope |
| **5. Benefits and costs** | The expected gains set against what must be spent to realise them |
| **6. Appendix** | Additional content at the end of the document, used here to time stamp misalignments and their resolutions |

Charters vary a lot between projects and companies, but most contain some variation of these, and whatever the form, they lay out goals, highlight benefits and keep stakeholders aligned. A charter may also carry sections such as budget, cost and success metrics.

### Activity: draft a project charter

The first activity is the blank template, the proposal and one meeting transcript. The template is a single document with the headings **Project Charter: [Title]**, **DATE**, then Project Summary, Project Goals, Deliverables, Scope and Exclusion (split into In-Scope and Out-of-Scope), Benefits and Costs (split into Benefits and Costs), and Appendix.

The brief asks for a project name and date, a summary of one to three sentences, **4 to 8 goals** and **5 to 9 deliverables**, drawn only from the supporting materials. The instruction that makes it hard is that some goals are stated explicitly and others are only suggested or implied, so the materials have to be read for complaints as well as for requests. A generative AI tool is allowed for a first draft of the summary or for brainstorming goals, with the reminder to use your own judgement to finalise it.

| In my draft | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **Name** | Sauce and Spoon Tabletop Menu Tablet Pilot Rollout |
| **Summary** | A pilot of tabletop tablets in the bar section at North and Downtown, to speed up service, increase product mix and generate operational data supporting annual growth goals, measured through the end of Q2 |
| **Explicit goals** | Increase product mix by raising average check from $65 to $75; cut table turn time by 30 minutes; raise daily guest counts by 10 percent; cut food waste by 25 percent; increase appetizer sales |
| **Implied goals** | Reduce negative reviews about service delays; reduce front-of-house burnout and turnover |
| **Deliverables** | Tablets installed in both bar areas, a chosen vendor package with add-ons and coupons, integration with POS and host software, a staff training plan and materials, defined metric tracking, and a pilot evaluation report at the end of Q2 |

## The charter as an alignment tool

<Aside type="note">
**Alignment** - reaching agreement between two or more parties.
<br />**Misalignment** - stakeholders not agreeing on the details of the project. It can arise between the project manager and any stakeholder at any stage, and is a common cause of project failure.
</Aside>

Whenever you communicate project details, think about the audience and what matters to them. For a charter the audience is the stakeholders, so the questions to ask are: who are they, which details matter most to them, and which details might they disagree with. Then check the charter actually contains those details before presenting it.

Stakeholders are generally not involved in day-to-day execution, which is why initiation is the moment to write down what everyone expects. It is also the cheapest moment to change anything: once the project moves into planning and execution, a substantive change can mean undoing completed work. Early on, do not be afraid to adjust.

The instructor's worked example of misalignment is two stakeholders on this same project, one imagining the tablets eventually automating ordering completely, the other wanting them to improve order accuracy. The response is to facilitate a discussion between the two and confirm which goal the project is actually pursuing.

### Documenting misalignments

Record the feedback received, then document each misalignment **and** its resolution so the team can refer back to the decision later. The mechanism is an **appendix** with a **time stamp**, the date and sometimes the time when the entry was added.

### Activity: revise your charter

The second activity supplies two email threads and one short meeting transcript, and asks for 6 to 10 goals, 5 to 9 deliverables, and 1 to 2 misalignments in the appendix with the decision that followed each. The guiding questions for the appendix are a useful template in themselves: who are the stakeholders, when do they disagree, who is the decision-maker, how was the misalignment clarified, and what was the final decision.

The three source documents produce three distinct situations:

| Source | Disagreement | Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Deanna's email on extra goals** | She wants a plan to reallocate FOH payroll to hire kitchen staff in the charter now. Peta objects that there is no data yet on tablet impact on revenue | **Unresolved.** Kept out of scope, revisited once pilot data exists. Interim idea: monitor BOH bandwidth and open two part-time line cook roles |
| **The appetizer sales thread** | Gilly says two thirds of her after-4pm parties already order appetizers so tablets will change little, and suggests dropping the goal. Alex, whose dinner crowd orders appetizers at about 20 percent of tables, proposes 20 percent growth | **Resolved** by splitting the target per location: 10 percent at North, 20 percent at Downtown, 15 percent average |
| **The guest wait time meeting** | Peta argues the goal duplicates the table turn time goal. Gilly notes wait time also depends on how busy the restaurant is | **Resolved.** Dropped as a separate goal, with Peta undertaking to explain to Deanna how shorter turn times reduce wait times |

The same meeting is where the measurement window gets fixed: piloting in April means measuring through the end of June.

## SMART goals

Some goals are broad during initiation because not all details are known, and that is acceptable, but specificity should be added wherever possible, because clear goals prevent misalignment and sharpen the understanding of scope, budget and timeline. The recurring real-world problem is a stakeholder who says they want more customers or more units sold without saying how many, of what kind, or by when.

<Aside type="note">
**Benchmarking** - evaluating success against a standard. When a goal is hard to quantify, research how others in the industry measure the same thing, for example by searching for how restaurants measure success.
</Aside>

Benchmarking the restaurant industry surfaces three metrics the course names: **table turnover rate**, the average time a guest spends at the table; **prime cost**, labour cost plus the total cost of goods such as food and drink; and **average check amount**, the average spend per guest on a meal. Most industries have their own equivalents, technology included.

The relevance test is worked through on this project: because one of Sauce and Spoon's broader objectives is fresh, quick food for working families, a goal such as cutting customer checkout time by 10 percent on average in the first six weeks after implementation is relevant, since it serves the larger company aim.

### Activity: update your charter goals

The third activity asks for the goals to be analysed against all five SMART criteria, with the deficient parts highlighted, then **3 to 5 goals** rewritten in full. Its own worked example is a goal that starts as increase gift card sales by the end of the year and becomes increase gift card sales by 15 percent by the end of the year by offering a 25 percent discount to returning customers, so the rewrite adds both the measure and the method.

A useful concession in the brief: where a detail genuinely is not known yet, write **TBD** into the goal rather than dropping the criterion, because a goal written in SMART language forces the team to come back and fill the gap.

My five rewritten goals, all time-bound to the end of Q2:

<ul>
<li>Raise the average check total from $65 to $75 using tablet upsell prompts and coupons on appetizers and specialty beverages, supporting the company-wide product mix goal.</li>
<li>Increase appetizer sales by 15 percent on average across the pilot, 10 percent at North and 20 percent at Downtown, via add-on suggestions and coupons.</li>
<li>Cut average bar table turn time by 30 minutes, from roughly 80 minutes to roughly 50, by letting guests order and pay at the tablet.</li>
<li>Raise average daily guest counts at the two pilot sites by 10 percent, driven by the faster turnover.</li>
<li>Cut food waste by 25 percent by capturing guest order modifications directly on the tablet and reducing comped or re-fired meals.</li>
</ul>

The non-numeric goals stay in the charter as **supporting goals** rather than being deleted: fewer negative reviews about service delays, less front-of-house burnout and turnover, and clean baseline versus pilot data to inform the rollout decision.

## Scope, benefits and costs

Scope, benefits and cost are the second alignment conversation, after vision and goals. The reasoning is that stakeholders reading the charter may never see the detailed plans that come later, so consensus on the critical elements has to be reached now, while the details are still soft. If a particular benefit matters to a key stakeholder, emphasise that benefit in the charter.

### In-scope and out-of-scope

**Scope** is the boundaries of the project, for instance the number of restaurants in the pilot. Anything that does not contribute to the project goals is out of scope. The three questions are: which details are stakeholders already aligned on and therefore in scope, which elements do they disagree about, and which details should be designated out of scope for this particular project.

### Benefits and costs

<Aside type="note">
**Benefits** - the expected gains of a project. They can be direct monetary gains or indirect, such as better customer engagement or satisfaction.
<br />**Costs** - the money spent on project tasks plus the price of things like time, resources and labour. Costs are evaluated and managed through a **budget**, an estimate of the money allocated to complete the project.
</Aside>

Benefits are usually already written down in the business case or project proposal, so that is where to look first. Two further points: a list of benefits often reveals project goals that were missed, and a list of costs lets stakeholders weigh the gains against the money needed to realise them. Build the cost list by working with stakeholders to get estimates on labour, materials and anything else the project will make the business pay for. The benefits should outweigh the costs, which is nearly always true at the moment a project is proposed; putting both in one place is what makes that judgement visible.

The final charter splits both ways. Direct benefits are the five numeric goals. Indirect benefits are fewer service-delay complaints, better customer satisfaction, reduced front-of-house burnout, clean operational data for the rollout decision, better integration between tablets, POS and host software, and a refreshed digital menu presence. The costs came from the chosen vendor, Terrific Tablets:

| Cost line | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Training materials and fees | $10,000 |
| Hardware and software implementation across locations | $30,000 |
| Maintenance, IT fees through end of year | $5,000 |
| Updated website and menu design fee | $5,000 |
| Other customisation fees | $550 |
| **Total** | **$50,550** |

Indirect costs sit underneath as prose rather than numbers: staff training time and the short-term productivity dip during rollout, project manager and stakeholder time through the end of Q2, and possible interim kitchen staffing.

### A practitioner's view of initiation

A Google interviewee describes the recurring initiation problem as getting alignment between key stakeholders, plus attracting good project managers onto the project rather than assigning them, on the principle that attraction beats promotion. To refine success criteria he works through roughly twenty questions: understanding the environment, the pitfalls and risks in the ecosystem, survey interviews asking people what they see as the problem, and quantitative questions to establish what is actually measurable.

His three causes of project failure are worth memorising: **poor project definition**, **disorganisation** because the project lead never established a framework to manage the work, and **too many people**, where everyone wants to work on the project and nobody is accountable. Where two sponsors are apples and oranges, the fix is many meetings and brokered compromise.

## Stakeholder analysis and the power-interest grid

<Aside type="note">
**Stakeholder analysis** - a visual representation of all the stakeholders. It captures who they are, what their priorities are and how they prefer to communicate, and it shows how strongly each one's needs or goals should be prioritised.
<br />**Influence** - how much power a stakeholder has and how much their actions affect the project outcome.
<br />**Interest** - how much the stakeholder's own needs will be affected by the project's operations and outcomes.
</Aside>

The reason the analysis comes before the negotiations is practical: almost every part of a project needs input and agreement from several stakeholders, and they will not all value the same input or agree on the same things, so a project manager has to be a capable negotiator. The aspects commonly negotiated are **scope**, **costs and benefits**, **timeline**, **success criteria**, **roles and responsibilities**, and **resources**. Good early alignment prevents some of those negotiations from ever happening.

### The four quadrants

### Stakeholder management tips

The accompanying reading defines **stakeholder management** as maintaining good relationships with the people who have the most influence on your work, and notes that without it a project is less likely to succeed.

<ul>
<li><strong>Identify everyone at the start.</strong> Early involvement sets clear expectations, responsibilities and boundaries, gives people time to voice concerns, and creates a sense of ownership that makes them more likely to contribute and remove barriers.</li>
<li><strong>Keep the project vision clear.</strong> The vision describes the need the project fulfils. Because stakeholders apply pressure to add requirements, shorten timelines or cut resources, agreement on the vision, and specifically on what done looks like, protects everyone. Include highly influential stakeholders in strategic planning.</li>
<li><strong>Equip stakeholders with user-friendly resources.</strong> A one-pager giving an overview, or a weekly status report with the latest information and links to the main artifacts, plus access to the documentation itself.</li>
<li><strong>Learn what they care about and why,</strong> by asking about their most important priorities and goals, the role they want, and how the project supports them.</li>
<li><strong>Adjust communication frequency and approach</strong> to each role and preference, and say so explicitly rather than assuming.</li>
<li><strong>Enlist senior stakeholders</strong> when needed, and ask who else you should be talking to.</li>
<li><strong>Bring problems to stakeholders</strong> once they have a vested interest: how would you handle this, what solutions come to mind?</li>
</ul>

## Negotiating scope with stakeholders

Persuasion and negotiation are framed as constructive tools that improve communication, clarify wants and needs and produce workable solutions, and being good at them raises your influence. The key posture is to treat the other party as colleagues and peers rather than opponents, which is another reason for doing the stakeholder analysis first: it makes them people with their own jobs and pressures who also want the project to succeed.

<Aside type="note">
**Mutual benefit** - a solution in which every party gains some benefit or advantage. The aim is an outcome that maximises benefits, minimises losses and is fair to all.
</Aside>

The instructor's example is a disagreement over headcount: you want five people, a stakeholder wants three. Mutual benefit might be installing automated software to absorb some of the work, or adjusting the timeline and expectations so five people are not needed. The stakeholder keeps the smaller team, the work still gets done. Brainstorm every option that meets the criteria first, so that in the negotiation you can present several alternatives rather than one.

Trade-offs matter as much as satisfying stakeholders, and making them requires knowing the project's priorities across scope, timeline and budget. If a deadline is fixed, negotiate any scope change that threatens it; if the requirements are fixed, negotiate budget or timeline instead. The **triple constraint model**, the iron triangle from an earlier course, is the tool for deciding whether a request is acceptable and what it will cost elsewhere.

### The tips reading

<Steps>

1. **Understand motivations** before the meeting. They may be budgetary, interpersonal such as wanting more time, or tied to personal career goals.

2. **Set the scene.** Open by reflecting on why you are meeting, remind everyone why the project exists, and affirm the shared goal.

3. **Listen first.** Hear them out before presenting your own view; acknowledging their perspective makes your suggestions easier to accept later.

4. **Ask questions to define goals.** Push for specific, measurable detail rather than subjective language, for example cutting newsletter signup time by at least 30 percent.

5. **Explain the why before the what.** Give the reasons and the value first; people who understand where you are coming from are likelier to grant the request.

6. **Do not oversell.** State your case, then withdraw slightly and let people process. Silence here is respect, and a long pause means you have prompted thought.

7. **Be creative.** Looking for alternative solutions converts a heavy negotiation into a team effort, and there is often more than one answer.

8. **Do not make it personal.** Focus on what is good for the project, and reframe with objective facts if personal considerations creep in.

9. **Seek a win-win.** Work out what would satisfy the other side, then find a version that also satisfies you. One party may compromise more, but a mutually beneficial agreement is always the target.

</Steps>

### Reaching a win-win

The follow-up reading adds three practices, applicable to vendors, contractors and suppliers as much as to internal stakeholders. **Share information** rather than withholding it: open lines where each party names its worries and preferences work better, for instance saying up front that a previous supplier delivered poor quality so expectations on quality are clear. **Ask questions and listen actively**, so both sides have the information a good agreement needs. **Propose multiple options**, because a single offer invites the other party to treat it as take it or leave it; if every proposal is rejected, ask which one they liked best, since that points towards a workable solution.

### A scope change in the wild

A program manager at YouTube tells the cautionary version. A scope change can arrive at any time and usually arrives just before launch. On an app measuring body weight percentages, the CEO, focused on the launch PR, demanded a change to the graphs at the last minute. The team asked the developers for an estimate, which pushed well past the launch date, so they produced several proposals and negotiated the outcome: launch as built, then ship the change about two weeks later in a second release.

Two lessons he draws. **Stack rank** a last-minute request against everything else at its priority level. And nothing is free: when scope comes in, go back and identify what gets pushed out to make room.

## Influencing without authority

The final piece of the week is the negotiation Peta is actually facing. Omar, having reviewed the charter, emails to say it matches his vision but he wants the rollout expanded to every dining area rather than just the bar, and he will sign off once the charter is revised. Peta replies that she will explore it but cannot promise the current budget or schedule will support an expansion that size, and that she is concerned about maintaining customer satisfaction if the project scales too fast. Deanna then presses her privately: Omar rarely gets involved, so when he does it matters.

Peta's response is to gather evidence rather than to argue or to capitulate. She convenes the general managers, the kitchen managers, the consultant and the executive chef and runs a brainstorm, stating openly that she has reasons to push back but wants input, and that if the change is not feasible she wants a thoughtful rationale for refusing.

| Voice | Position |
| --- | --- |
| **Larissa**, Kitchen Manager Downtown | Initially in favour given the revenue goals, then persuaded that starting small and expanding once the effort and data are understood makes more sense |
| **Gilly**, GM North | Confident her restaurant could handle it and could guide Zane through it |
| **Zane**, Kitchen Manager North | Open to expansion with Gilly's guidance, if it makes sense once everything else is considered |
| **Alex**, GM Downtown | Hesitant. Worked hard to restore staff morale and service quality, and a small problem in a big rollout could be hard to recover from |
| **Seydou**, consultant | Advises against, despite standing to sell more tablets. A bar rollout is lower risk, and letting the kitchen scale up gradually matters because guests with tablets order more |
| **Carter**, Executive Chef | Against. He already has concerns about kitchen staffing with the bar rollout alone, and thinks the rollout has to be incremental |

Peta's summary back to the room is the negotiation position in one paragraph: an expanded rollout would give more accurate metrics and much larger profits if it succeeded, but would require pushing back the timeline, buying more tablets and wiring, and hiring more back-of-house staff, and the benefits may not outweigh the risks. Staying with the bar keeps customer satisfaction high and the project inside its original scope, lets the kitchen scale gradually, and lets any problems be handled discreetly. Carter's closing warning is the reason the next step is about influence rather than argument: Omar and Deanna have both been pushing hard on growth and a digital presence, and he has already tried making his case without success.

<Aside type="note">
**Coalition** - a temporary alliance or partnering of individuals or groups to achieve a common purpose or engage in a joint activity. Two or more people advocating together exert more influence than either acting alone.
</Aside>

A coalition applies all four steps at once. It raises credibility by showing that others support the goal, its members help find common ground and supply evidence, and it allows an emotional connection by including someone with a good relationship with the stakeholder or a deep enough understanding of both the person and the goal to make that connection.

An effective coalition balances **power** and **interest**: people whose vested interest and subject expertise advance the goal, plus people with enough organisational power to get things done. This is exactly what the power-interest grid was built for, which is why the stakeholder analysis is the tool the course recommends for choosing coalition members.

The ask itself, by email, in person or by phone, has four parts:

<ul>
<li>State clearly the issue you are trying to resolve.</li>
<li>Explain which aspect of the project is being negotiated.</li>
<li>Ask whether they would consider supporting your position, and say what that position is.</li>
<li>Refer to the specific source of power or interest you identified for them.</li>
</ul>

The model sentence given is about timing: the launch timing will affect business hours, and your experience managing restaurant projects year-round would help explain why that factor needs reconsidering. It tells the person that you value them, why the issue affects them, and why you think they specifically can help.

## Revision summary

<Aside type="tip">
**One-minute recap**

- **The capstone** applies Courses 1 to 5 to one scenario: **Sauce and Spoon**, a five-location restaurant group piloting tabletop ordering tablets in the **bar section** of its **North** and **Downtown** sites, launching at the start of Q2 and measured to the end of Q2. Peta is the group's first in-house project manager, experienced but new to restaurants.
- **The source documents** are a project proposal (sales up 28 percent, order times up 20 percent, 50 percent front-of-house turnover, flat margin), meeting transcripts, email threads and team bios. Goals must be read out of them, some explicit and some only implied.
- **The 5 Ws**: what, why, who, when, where. Tip: ask three stakeholders from three areas to describe the project, then synthesise one summary.
- **Project charter** = a formal, skimmable, living document that defines the project, aligns stakeholders on what success is, and must be approved before planning starts. Six sections: **summary, goals, deliverables, scope and exclusion, benefits and costs, appendix**.
- **Alignment** is agreement between parties; **misalignment** is a common cause of project failure. Initiation is the cheapest time to change anything. Record each misalignment and its resolution in a **time-stamped appendix**.
- **SMART**: specific, measurable, attainable, relevant, time-bound. Hunt subjective words like bigger, better, faster. Use **benchmarking** when a goal resists measurement; restaurant metrics include table turnover rate, prime cost and average check amount. Relevance is judged against company OKRs. Use **TBD** rather than dropping a criterion.
- **Scope** is the project's boundaries; anything not contributing to the goals is **out of scope**. Define it in writing, get sign-off, refer back to it, and name suspected **scope creep** openly.
- **Benefits** are expected gains, direct or indirect, usually already in the proposal; **costs** are money plus time, resources and labour, managed via a **budget**. Benefits should outweigh costs, and listing both together makes that visible. The pilot quote totalled $50,550.
- **Stakeholder analysis** maps **power** against **interest**: manage closely (high power, high interest), keep satisfied (high power, low interest), show consideration (low power, high interest), monitor (low power, low interest). Omar sits in keep satisfied, Deanna in manage closely, the kitchen managers and the consultant in show consideration, the non-pilot general manager in monitor.
- **Negotiation** aims at **mutual benefit** and a **win-win**. Understand motivations, set the scene, listen first, ask for measurable goals, explain why before what, do not oversell, be creative, keep it impersonal. Share information, listen actively, propose multiple options. Use the **triple constraint** to judge requests, stack rank late changes, and remember nothing comes for free.
- **Influencing**: Conger's four steps are establish credibility, frame for common ground, provide evidence, connect emotionally. A **coalition** is a temporary alliance that applies all four, and it should mix power with interest, chosen using the stakeholder analysis.
- **The live negotiation**: Omar wants the rollout expanded to all dining areas. Peta's evidence-gathering brainstorm produces the counter-case, and the expansion stays an open appendix item pending a feasibility comparison.
</Aside>
