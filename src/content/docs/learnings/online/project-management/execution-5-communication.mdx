Team management was the previous module; this one is its natural partner, because none of it works without strong communication. During execution the project manager is the person who connects the team to the information they need, and the module is about the daily craft of that: which channel to use, how to write an email people actually act on, and how to run a meeting that was worth calling.

The framing is that a PM is a hub for incoming and outgoing information. You clarify goals, action items, progress and updates, you track who needs to hear what and when, and you carry that forward consistently and coherently so everyone knows the current state of the project and what happens next. Consistency matters more than eloquence here.

The module is heavily practical. Two of its activities are writing a **meeting agenda** and writing an **invitation email**, so the agenda and the email get the most detailed treatment, alongside the four elements of an effective meeting, the accessibility guidance that makes meetings genuinely open to everyone, and the four meeting types you will keep running.

## The project manager as the communication hub

Teams communicate in a myriad of ways, and the PM sits in the middle of all of them. Meetings and check-ins are the obvious channel, but **project documents are communication hubs too** - schedules, trackers, meeting notes, the project plan. That makes three things your responsibility to state clearly:

<ul>
<li>How those documents get used</li>
<li>Who has access to them</li>
<li>How often they get updated</li>
</ul>

On top of that you field emails, instant messages and meetings, and all of it is what actually drives the project forward.

### Say it more than once, and in more than one way

People take information in differently: some learn by listening, some by watching, some by doing. So important information should be communicated **multiple times and in multiple ways**, which lets each person receive it in a format they can digest. Your team already has a lot of tasks competing for attention, so be proactive and reinforce the things that matter rather than assuming one announcement landed. Nobody should end up out of the loop.

## Choosing the channel

<div class="sc-chips">
  <span class="sc-chip amber">Email and messaging</span>
  <span class="sc-chip violet">In-person meetings</span>
  <span class="sc-chip green">Video conferencing</span>
  <span class="sc-chip slate">Work management and collaboration tools</span>
</div>

Your organisation may already have these in place, or a new project may let you pick, so it pays to know what is out there.

| Channel | Best for | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Email** | The standard for business communication, and the record of it | Everyone gets a flood of it, so streamline how much you send and make each one easy to triage |
| **Instant messaging** (Google Chat, Slack) | A quick question, a short update, nudging someone to check their inbox, clearing up a miscommunication fast | Distraction and informality; sensitive or confidential material leaking into chat; messages are hard to search for later |
| **Virtual meetings** (Google Meet, Zoom) | Real-time interaction as remote work becomes standard; boosts morale and creates a sense of team when you cannot be together | Needs the same structure as an in-person meeting to be worth the time |
| **Work management and collaboration tools** (Google Drive, Asana, Smartsheet) | Two-way information sharing, progress tracking, budgets, charts, contracts and licences, keeping stakeholders informed | Access control - be deliberate about who needs it and who does not |

The particular strength of work management tools is that communication sits **inside the context of the work**. Instead of writing an email and linking out to the relevant information, the task, the project detail and the timeline are already in one place for reference and decision making, and updates happen in real time. Team members can post their own progress without an extra meeting or phone call, which is what lets a remote team feel like it is working in the same office. Encourage people to check in and update regularly to keep that feeling alive.

Instant messaging deserves a judgement call rather than a habit. It is faster, but **ask people how they prefer to be reached** - plenty will still want email. With experience you learn which channel suits which person and which message.

<Aside type="caution">
**Confidentiality.** Using, accessing or sharing confidential or need-to-know information externally - financial and business data, user data, details about products and services - is likely prohibited unless you have explicit permission. When you set up shared work management tools, be mindful of who wants access versus who actually needs it. Data protection is part of your job.
</Aside>

## Writing effective emails

Email has been the main business channel for a long time, and it is also easy to misunderstand or ignore. Four principles fix most of that.

</figure>

### State what you want clearly

You are writing because you want something from the reader - an answer, a decision, agreement, a meeting. Make that unmissable:

<ul>
<li>Put the request in the <strong>subject line</strong>, and say there if it is urgent or needs a reply</li>
<li>State the request within the <strong>first two paragraphs</strong></li>
<li>Name the specific <strong>call to action</strong>: reply, review, RSVP</li>
<li>Write short, clear sentences for the detail, and say what you need from the recipient and by when</li>
<li>Define your terms; avoid acronyms and jargon the reader may not know</li>
</ul>

### Keep the content concise

Make every word work. Summarise what you need to convey and delete anything that does not contribute to the goal. The target is a **question-less, self-standing** email - one carrying enough information to stand alone, so the reader is left with no doubt about what you want or when. Know the audience while you do it: executives and other busy leaders often will not read past a few sentences or click through to an external link, so tailor the length to who is receiving it. If there really is a lot to share, put it in a document, link or attach that, and summarise in the email what you want them to focus on.

### Structure the writing

Structure is the visual flow of the message. A well-structured email hands over the critical information fast and lets the reader skim the explanation or skip it entirely.

| Device | Why it helps |
| --- | --- |
| **Bullets** | Break up the visual flow. If there is more than one of anything, bullet it, and open each bullet with a strong action verb |
| **Labels** | Guide the reader to the most important information |
| **Hyperlinks** | Give direct access to detail instead of bloating the email with it |
| **Strong topic sentences** | Put the main idea of each paragraph first |

### Check grammar, punctuation and spelling

Turn on the grammar and spelling suggestions in your email application, and correct everything before sending. Errors quietly cost you credibility.

### The worked example

The module reworks an invitation to an annual team building retreat. The **weak version** buried the registration link at the bottom, omitted the date and location entirely, piled in so many links that the reader was overwhelmed, used no bullets or labels, and contained spelling and punctuation mistakes.

The **strong version** keeps the same subject line - *Annual Team Building Retreat - Register Now!* - and then opens with a clear, concise description of the event with the dates and the hotel stated up front. The opening paragraph is followed by a bold label, *Sign up now!*, and three bulleted hyperlinks that each say exactly what the reader will get: register for the retreat, book a hotel room, sign up for lakeside activities. Grammar, spelling and punctuation are clean.

<Aside type="tip">
That contrast is the invitation-email activity in miniature: **what, when and where in the first lines; one obvious primary action; labelled and bulleted links; nothing else.**
</Aside>

### Tone and recipients

Language choices matter. Avoid slang, acronyms and text-message shortcuts, and write in a tone appropriate to the person receiving it. The general default is **friendly, motivating, clear and specific**. Choose your recipients carefully, and above all be conscious of *why* you are sending it at all.

### Using generative AI to draft emails

AI can take some of the administrative load off email so you can spend more time on strategic work, provided you use it responsibly.

<ul>
<li><strong>Check your organisation's AI policy first</strong>, especially where sensitive information is involved</li>
<li><strong>Use the prompting framework:</strong> specify the task, provide the necessary context, include references as examples of the style, tone and format you want, evaluate the output, and iterate rather than accepting the first attempt</li>
<li><strong>Generate templates</strong> for emails you send often, such as project updates, so you keep your own writing and decision making in the loop</li>
<li><strong>Customise for the audience</strong> - the output is a draft, not a send</li>
<li><strong>Review everything for accuracy.</strong> You are the human in the loop and the final judge of accuracy, tone and appropriateness</li>
</ul>

## What makes a meeting effective

Meetings differ in size, purpose and format, but effective ones share four elements.

<div class="sc-quad">
<div class="q amber">
<div class="qh">Structured</div>
<ul>
<li>Starts and ends on time</li>
<li>Attendees carefully selected</li>
<li>Topics prioritised on an agenda</li>
<li>A designated note taker</li>
</ul>
</div>
<div class="q violet">
<div class="qh">Intentional</div>
<ul>
<li>A clearly stated purpose and expectations</li>
<li>Stated in the agenda and the invite</li>
<li>Pre-reading sent in advance</li>
<li>Everyone knows why they are there</li>
</ul>
</div>
<div class="q green">
<div class="qh">Collaborative</div>
<ul>
<li>Not a wall of presentations</li>
<li>A shared digital meeting document</li>
<li>Comments welcome in any format</li>
<li>Objectives of the session made clear</li>
</ul>
</div>
<div class="q slate">
<div class="qh">Inclusive</div>
<ul>
<li>A moderator or facilitator</li>
<li>Space left for quieter participants</li>
<li>Accessible materials and tools</li>
<li>Dial-in and camera-off options</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>

### Structured

Managing the time and the audience tells participants that they are valued. If someone's presence is not necessary, do not invite them - share the notes instead. Invite people who can **contribute to the discussion** and anyone who will be **directly affected** by the topics.

<Aside type="note">
**Timeboxing** - setting a time limit for the discussion of a topic, with a buffer of a few minutes so the agenda is not over-packed. It forces the important items to get the attention they deserve.
</Aside>

If there are several topics, consider a few shorter meetings focused on one or two each rather than one sprawling session. After the first few meetings, review the pacing: some topics need more time, others less. And if you finish early, end early - everybody likes their time back.

The **note taker** can be you or another attendee, and their existence is what lets anyone refer back to what was discussed. Be explicit about how, when and to whom the outcomes get shared.

### Intentional

The purpose is the reason you are meeting and what you want to achieve: making a decision, assigning tasks, proposing or inventing an idea, reviewing last month's sales and setting next month's goals, choosing coordinators for an event, having a supervisor prioritise a list of tasks. It may change from meeting to meeting or stay the same. A well-designed agenda increases the group's ability to address problems and prevents wasted time, because it:

<ul>
<li>Sets clear expectations for what must happen <strong>before</strong> and <strong>during</strong> the meeting</li>
<li>Helps attendees prepare</li>
<li>Keeps everyone focused on the right topics</li>
<li>Clarifies the expectations and goals</li>
</ul>

Where input is needed, **send pre-reading in advance** so people arrive ready to participate. Whether the meeting is formal or informal, and whether it has three attendees or hundreds, state a clear and thoughtful purpose and aim to achieve it within the time allotted.

### Collaborative

Collaboration is people working together to produce or create something, and a meeting can be collaborative even when its purpose is one person sharing information. Three practices do most of the work: keep the agenda from being wall-to-wall presentations where participants are talked at; say plainly whether the session is for a decision or for information and discussion; and keep a **shared digital meeting document** that people can write comments and thoughts into directly, telling them the notes will be shared, which encourages active listening and real-time participation. Then respect each person's preferred communication style - make clear they are welcome to respond out loud, in the chat, in the meeting notes or in any other format you offer.

### Inclusive

<Aside type="note">
**Inclusivity** - the practice or policy of including people who might otherwise be excluded or marginalised. Done well, it produces excellent collaboration because every participant's contribution is valued.
</Aside>

Appoint a **moderator or facilitator** for both in-person and virtual meetings. They guide the session and help participants ask questions in real time while someone else presents, so the presenter can stay on their topic while the moderator watches the participants and signals when people can chime in. Deliberately leave room for those who have stayed quiet: at the close of a topic or before the meeting ends, go round the room and ask each person to comment on a particular question - a **final flight check** before everyone takes off.

For virtual meetings, remember that internet access varies. Provide a **phone dial-in** option, which the common tools all support, and tell participants it is fine to turn cameras off to improve their connection.

## Inclusive and accessible meetings in practice

Meetings can be intimidating, so inclusion needs **procedures that are consistent and predictable** rather than good intentions.

<ul>
<li><strong>Formalise check-ins.</strong> Ask each person consistently for an update on their work or how they are doing. Keep the questions open-ended and vary them over time with humour, analogies or finish-the-sentence prompts, such as a highlight of my week or something I have gratitude for. Your own modelling of professionalism, vulnerability and empathy sets the tone.</li>
<li><strong>Give full attention.</strong> Listen without interrupting. Eye contact and turning towards the speaker help them feel safe; head shaking, looking away or checking your phone does the opposite.</li>
<li><strong>Help everyone be heard.</strong> Solicit ideas and ask questions. If someone is interrupted, redirect attention back and invite them to finish. If someone has not spoken, ask what they think. Speaking up is daunting for many people, particularly in virtual meetings, for anyone who is not in the majority in the room, and for entry-level colleagues. Warn people in advance that they will be asked to share so they can prepare, and gather input after the meeting from those who did not speak.</li>
<li><strong>Make different perspectives safe.</strong> Open by explicitly encouraging competing viewpoints and try to surface at least three points of view on a contested issue, then follow up afterwards asking for further thoughts.</li>
<li><strong>Use images that reflect the world.</strong> In slides and handouts, choose images showing diversity of race, gender, age, ability, cultural background, religion and geography.</li>
</ul>

<Aside type="note">
**Accessible** - easily used, accessed or adapted for use by people experiencing disabilities. In a meeting it means nobody is excluded from participating or from understanding what is shared. Plan for visual impairments and blindness, hearing loss and deafness, mobility disabilities, and neurological disorders.
</Aside>

| Group | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| **Visual impairments** | Slides in a large font of at least 22 point, high-contrast colours, alternative text for every image, graphic and table, and meaningful graphics described aloud. Share an accessible electronic version of the deck ahead of time. Printed handouts: at least 18 point, black on white matte paper, a simple font with no compressed type or italics, and 1.25 to double line spacing |
| **Deaf or hard of hearing** | Face the person you are speaking to, since some are reading your speech. Speak clearly at a moderate pace and volume without exaggerating or shouting, and allow time to respond. Ask for clarification if you do not understand. Put everything you say on the slides, and caption your videos |
| **Mobility disabilities** | Ample circulation space so mobility devices can pass through, accessible seating spread around the room, and half-round seating for presentations so everyone faces the speaker |
| **Neurological disorders** | Provide an agenda or task list so people have time to absorb the content and expectations, enable closed captioning on the call platform, record the meeting and share the recording with everyone, use simple page layouts, break up long text with images and graphs, and avoid moving or flickering content and background audio that cannot be switched off |

The payoff is not abstract: inclusive, accessible meetings improve engagement, productivity and morale.

## The productive meeting checklist

An internal study at Google found that productive meetings share three things: **active participation from attendees**, **a clear and concise agenda that is followed throughout**, and **the correct attendees** - people who can contribute to the goal.

<Steps>

1. **Before.** Prepare an agenda stating the purpose and goals, and share it. Invite only those who can help reach the goals, make their roles and responsibilities explicit, and mark non-essential people as optional. Across time zones, share the burden by rotating recurring meeting times. Evaluate whether the meeting is needed at all and cancel it if the content could be an email. Schedule shorter meetings, because meetings expand to fill the time allotted. Then set aside time to prepare yourself: read the materials, review the agenda, come ready to participate.

2. **During.** State the goals at the start and stick to the agenda so it does not get derailed; for a recurring meeting, review the previous meeting's action items to keep accountability alive. Ask people to put phones and laptops away and silence notifications. Practise and demonstrate **active listening**, verbally with responses such as *that makes sense, tell us more* and non-verbally with nodding and eye contact. Give everyone a chance to speak, remote participants included, and use open-ended questions - *what does everyone think?* rather than *does everyone agree?* Start with a light personal question to help people relax. Capture key points, decisions and action items, and assign each action item to a person.

3. **After.** Recap the key decisions, action items, timelines and notes and send them to participants. Schedule any follow-up meetings with the relevant context attached. Reassess whether the recurring meetings are needed at all and whether they can be less frequent.

</Steps>

<Aside type="tip">
If you are new to the company or team, learn their existing meeting practices and work within them before making major changes. And the underlying economics: time saved by more productive meetings goes straight back into project tasks.
</Aside>

## The four types of project meeting

| Meeting | Purpose | Who attends | Character |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Project kick-off** | The official start of the project, aligning the team's understanding of the goals with the actual plans and procedures | The team, plus senior management and key stakeholders for buy-in and alignment | One-off, at the beginning |
| **Status update** | Align the team on updates, progress, challenges and next steps; give team and stakeholders visibility into performance and task progress | The team, with reports shared onwards | Regular and recurring, with a relatively fixed agenda and time |
| **Stakeholder review** | Win and sustain buy-in and support; surface stakeholder challenges and adjust to resolve them | Stakeholders, one-to-one or in groups | Generally more formal, recurring or one-off |
| **Project review (retrospective)** | Review how the project or phase unfolded: lessons learned, what is going well, what to keep doing, what to improve, and celebrating the success | The project team | At the end of a project or phase |

### The status meeting agenda

This is the most common meeting type, and it works because the agenda barely changes. One of the PM's key responsibilities is to know the project's status at any moment and to make sure others are up to speed or know where to find the latest information.

<figure class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-flow col">
    <div class="sc-node amber">Task updates<span class="sub">status of the most urgent tasks, how many done, how many open</span></div>
    <div class="sc-node violet">Schedule status<span class="sub">behind, ahead, or on pace with projections</span></div>
    <div class="sc-node green">Budget status<span class="sub">plus anything new hitting the bottom line</span></div>
    <div class="sc-node slate">Current and anticipated issues<span class="sub">changes, risks, resource and vendor issues, quality and scope</span></div>
    <div class="sc-node soft">Action items<span class="sub">every one with an owner and a due date</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Raising issues on a recurring basis means nobody gets caught off guard and solutions get discussed together.</figcaption>
</figure>

<Aside type="note">
**Action item** - a task on the list that needs to be completed. Assigning action items is how you wrap up a meeting and keep the project moving, and **every action item has an owner and a due date**.
</Aside>

Status meetings also create the moment to recognise milestones, share information and raise concerns with the team. Their frequency depends on the project's complexity, the number of team members, and how much information the sponsor or client needs, and you should not be afraid to change the cadence as the project progresses.

### Getting stakeholder meetings right

Stakeholders each bring their own tools, know-how and expertise, and the stakeholder meeting is where those contributions get outlined and used. Start by understanding their challenges or problems, then respond and adjust to resolve them.

**One-to-one** meetings let you go deeper on the details that particular person cares about. **Group** meetings are for engaging several at once; if you have many stakeholders, focus the meeting on the most influential ones - the senior manager from each group you need to engage, for example - and keep everyone else informed by email or meeting notes.

Most stakeholder meetings stick to critical information only. Always be ready with a short overall project status update of two to five minutes to open. Beyond that, the reasons to meet are to **seek out and listen to feedback**, or to **make a decision or resolve a major issue**, which usually means senior leaders and the project sponsor and covers go or no-go decisions, choices between options, and sign-off on investments. Because these meetings are formal, prepare reading materials and documents in advance so people arrive in the right mindset.

Frequency depends on why the stakeholder is involved at all: whether they are advisory, such as a consultant in a RACI, or simply someone to inform and keep in the loop; and whether their involvement centres on one activity or runs long-term. Decide case by case.

## After the meeting is where projects are won

What happens after a meeting is often the most important part. The familiar failure is a great live conversation followed by everyone going their separate ways with no clarity on what each person should do next.

Generative AI helps here. A prompt such as *summarise the following meeting notes in a succinct, two-paragraph email that identifies the key points and action items, with a friendly opening line and a warm sign-off*, followed by the raw notes, turns disorganised jottings into a clean summary with the action items pulled out - valuable precisely because action items are easily lost in the middle of notes and cause real problems when missed.

Two caveats. AI cannot read your mind, so your **notes have to be complete enough** to contain everything important; if capturing good notes is hard, some video conferencing software will transcribe the meeting for you. And **specify the format** to match your communication objective: a short email summary for the people who attended, but a longer recap if some people missed the meeting and need to get fully up to speed.
