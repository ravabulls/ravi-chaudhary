## Communicating a project problem

Every project has problems, and communicating them is part of the project manager's job. Most are small enough to settle inside the immediate project team. Occasionally one has to be **escalated** to a senior stakeholder, together with a proposed solution, so they can give input and guidance on the next step.

The core principle: a stakeholder should never have to open several project documents or chase multiple email threads to work out what the problem is. Pulling the relevant pieces together is the project manager's responsibility, not theirs.

<Aside type="note">
**Synthesising** - gathering information from several sources, such as emails, presentations, meeting notes and documents, and using those points to form your own analysis. It is a skill project managers use constantly: leading meetings, writing documentation, emailing the team. Potential employers may want proof you can do it.
</Aside>

### The test question

When deciding what belongs in a one to two sentence overview, ask: **how can I communicate a decision in a way that makes it easy for them to decide?**

Sending a link to the project plan and pointing at the overdue rows fails that test, because it gives no understanding of what the problem actually is.

### The worked example from the video

Five tasks in the plan are overdue because of supplier delays, and the slippage may reach the final deliverable. Solutions have already been explored. The summary given:

> A number of tasks have run past their due date because of supplier issues, so we recommend hiring a second supplier to hit the deliverable date. Otherwise we will need to push the launch date back.

That gives the stakeholder three usable options: agree with the solution, disagree with it, or offer one of their own. Any of the three counts as success, because the project manager's role was to communicate the problem and propose a solution, not to be right.

### Chris on problem solving

Chris is a **program manager at Google working on Search**, building features for millions of users across many surfaces, languages and information needs. His view is blunt: problem solving is the single most important thing program managers do, because it *is* the job.

<div class="sc-chips">
  <span class="sc-chip amber">Scope problems - work moving out of scope, or scope growing or shrinking</span>
  <span class="sc-chip violet">Budget problems - too little funding, or too much</span>
  <span class="sc-chip green">People problems - too few, too many, or the wrong skill sets</span>
  <span class="sc-chip slate">Timeline problems</span>
</div>

His loop is four moves: **identify** the problem, **build a framework** for it from your tools, processes and methodologies, **propose a solution**, and **get buy-in** for it. The only way to get buy-in is to put something organised and principled in front of people.

<ul>
<li><strong>Always hunt the root cause.</strong> What you are experiencing is usually a red herring, an outcome of something underneath. Debugging the underlying systemic, process, tooling or technical issue is the first step, not the last.</li>
<li><strong>The decision is the outcome.</strong> Once you understand the problem well enough, assembling an objective plan from those inputs is how you actually reach a decision.</li>
<li><strong>The artifacts are not the job.</strong> Charters, scope documents, meeting notes and trackers feel like the job, but they are mechanisms and tools. The real job is running scope and programmes, convincing people, driving organisational change and solving strategic initiatives.</li>
<li><strong>Build the skill anywhere.</strong> If you have not yet had big problems to work on, hobbies, building a piece of software for yourself or a friend, or a passion in another industry all supply real problems to solve. Every industry and business has them.</li>
</ul>

## Objectives and key results

OKRs were introduced earlier in the programme; this week revisits them specifically as a lever for getting a problem taken seriously.

<Aside type="note">
**Objectives and key results (OKRs)** - a tool for organisation-wide goal setting that pairs a goal with a metric to produce a measurable outcome.
<br />**Objective** - what needs to be achieved. Close to a goal.
<br />**Key result** - how an organisation, team or individual will tangibly measure success against that objective.
</Aside>

| Sauce and Spoon example | |
| --- | --- |
| **Objective** | Prioritise customer needs and wants |
| **Key result** | Address feedback from customer reviews within 24 hours |

### Two jobs OKRs do

<div class="sc-split">
  <div class="sc-panel eff">
    <div class="head">Testing whether a project deserves to exist</div>
    <ul>
      <li>If a project and its goals contribute to the organisation's wider OKRs, that is a good sign it is relevant and worth the time and money</li>
      <li>At Google, every project, large or small, aims to contribute tangibly to organisation-wide OKRs</li>
      <li>Struggling to explain how a project helps reach an OKR is a strong signal to re-evaluate the project altogether</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="sc-panel rsp">
    <div class="head">Getting a problem onto a senior agenda</div>
    <ul>
      <li>Naming the specific OKRs a problem threatens makes clear why it must be addressed</li>
      <li>It also explains why the problem is worth that person's attention at all</li>
      <li>Senior stakeholders have a great deal to focus on beyond your project, so an OKR link is what catches limited attention</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

OKRs work as a **shared language** across an organisation, which is exactly why they travel well upwards.

## Emailing a senior stakeholder

If an issue is big enough to escalate, it is an issue you want resolved quickly, so email is the tool: fast to send, and able to ask for a specific decision. The risk is being ignored.

<Steps>

1. **Start from what matters to them.** Senior stakeholders are usually more interested in a problem's potential impact on the organisation than on a single project. Make that impact clear **within the first two sentences**.

2. **Write a subject line that states the topic and the action.** Words such as *urgent*, *timely*, *decision needed* or *please review* tell a person receiving many emails a day both what this is and what you want them to do.

3. **Keep the body brief.** Outline the problem, explain how it hits organisational goals, and state the decision you need in order to proceed. Concretely: one or two sentences summarising the problem, plus one sentence on the OKR impact.

4. **Attach or link anything they need to decide**, rather than making them ask for it.

5. **Proofread.** Misspellings, grammatical errors and inaccurate hyperlinks all cost credibility. Use the spellcheck and grammar tools available to you.

</Steps>

### Laura on communicating with executives

Laura is an **executive productivity advisor at Google**, coaching executives one to one on time management, meeting management, effective email, communication and organisation. Her material is the counterpart to the email best practices: how to shape communication around a particular person.

<ul>
<li><strong>Concise for an executive, detailed for a teammate.</strong> When summarising for an executive, isolate the absolutely important information they need to see.</li>
<li><strong>Do the homework before you write.</strong> Ask their executive assistant, or someone who has worked with them, what their preferred communication style is, what kinds of presentation they like, and what information they typically need in order to decide. That preparation matters most when your window with them is short.</li>
<li><strong>Expect stakeholders to differ.</strong> She worked with two managers on one project: one was talkative, loved brainstorming and wanted frequent meetings to hash out every detail; the other was the complete opposite. The same information and the same decision have to be tailored per person.</li>
<li><strong>Anticipate the five questions.</strong> Before presenting, work out the five questions the stakeholder is likely to ask and keep that detail ready in an appendix, so their time is used well.</li>
<li><strong>Arrive with proposed solutions.</strong> Not what should I do, but I think we should do A, though B and C are also options, what do you think. It gives them a starting point and shows you have done the background work and understand the problem.</li>
<li><strong>Put a TLDR at the top.</strong> Too long, did not read: the one sentence they need from the email. Variants that do the same work are an update on project A, need decision, action requested, or deadline by. It tells them what is coming before they read it.</li>
<li><strong>Make the body scannable.</strong> Bullets, bolding and highlighting for what must stand out, the ask reiterated at the end, deadlines stated, links and attachments included, so someone skimming or re-reading later still has everything needed to reply.</li>
</ul>

## Why closing a project properly matters

<Aside type="note">
**Project closing** - making sure the team completes all project work, executing any remaining project management processes, and obtaining stakeholder recognition that the project is complete.
</Aside>

### The closing checklist

<ul>
<li>Conduct administrative closure of the <strong>procurement</strong> process.</li>
<li>Run a <strong>formal closing process</strong> after the final project phase or milestone.</li>
<li>Complete and present an <strong>impact report</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>Document acceptance</strong> from all stakeholders, confirming they are happy with the deliverables and outcomes.</li>
<li>Formally <strong>disband and thank</strong> the project team.</li>
</ul>

### Retrospective tips from the reading

The retrospective itself was Week 3's material, but the closing reading adds four facilitation tips worth carrying into the final one.

| Tip | Why it works |
| --- | --- |
| Create a safe space for people to share experiences and feedback | Honest input only arrives where it is safe to give |
| Model the behaviour and responses you want from the team | The facilitator sets the tone before anyone else speaks |
| Phrase questions non-confrontationally | Instead of what went wrong and what went well, ask what we should **start, stop and continue** |
| Remind the team of the milestones they reached | Recalling the whole arc of the project sparks far more discussion than starting from a blank page |

## The closeout report

Before a project manager can call a project complete, there is a closeout report to write. It is a compilation exercise as much as a reflection: every link and every document gathered in one place, a practice the course calls **good project hygiene**.

### What it does

<ul>
<li><strong>Confirms the project is done</strong> and summarises deliverables, success metrics, feedback, lessons learned and next steps.</li>
<li><strong>Serves as a reference document for the organisation</strong>, so that a follow-up or similar project starts with the artifacts already assembled.</li>
<li><strong>Reflects on the team's performance</strong> and helps the team verify that every task was actually completed.</li>
<li><strong>Finalises the team's efforts</strong> so people can move on to new projects and tasks, with everyone satisfied with the work done.</li>
<li><strong>Increases the impact of the work</strong> by communicating it to people who were not closely involved.</li>
</ul>

### Who it is for

A closeout report is a document created by project managers **for project managers**: future project managers, and anyone interested in the project's elements and artifacts. The reading describes it as a blueprint of what the team did, how they did it and what they delivered, including an evaluation of the quality of the work and of performance against budget and schedule.

The bar the video sets: someone entirely unfamiliar with the project should be able to read it and come away understanding what the project was, why it was done and how well it went. In this course it is also framed as a **portfolio piece** that can stand alone in front of a potential employer, demonstrating the ability to synthesise and communicate information clearly.

### The sections

| Section | What goes in it |
| --- | --- |
| **Project summary** | The objectives, or put another way, the desired result for the project |
| **Methodology** | Which approach the team used: Waterfall, Agile, Lean, a combination, or something else |
| **Results: performance baseline** | Actual against planned schedule, cost and scope, with a notes column for explaining discrepancies |
| **Key accomplishments and outcomes** | What was achieved, as bullets |
| **Lessons learned** | What the team now knows that it did not before |
| **Next steps** | What happens after this project |
| **Project documentation archive** | Links to the proposal, charter, plan, evaluation findings and other artifacts |

The template supplies guiding questions under each section to push for enough detail, with the invitation to go further still. The purpose throughout is to **compile and archive** the most important aspects of the project.

### The executive summary

<Aside type="note">
**Executive summary** - a few sentences to a paragraph describing a project's purpose and outcome, giving an overview of the main points of a larger report. It is written for stakeholders who may not have time to read the whole thing.
</Aside>

The test to apply: if an executive had time to read nothing but this, would they understand the project highlights? It should answer how effectively the project was delivered and what was learned from it, while being neither overloaded with detail nor so general as to be vague.

Two pieces of practical advice. First, review the **SMART goals, the business case and the project charter** before writing, because they point at the aspects that matter most and are usually tied to the key accomplishments. Second, **write the executive summary last**: draft the results, what worked and next steps slides first, in detail and with graphs and images, and the highlights become easy to lift out.

Three elements belong in it:

| Element | The question it answers |
| --- | --- |
| **Project vision** | What was the purpose, and what need is the project fulfilling? |
| **Key accomplishments** | Which activities, tasks and milestones produced the success? What are the main highlights, what value was added, did profitability improve? |
| **Lessons learned** | What could be improved, and how will future processes change for the better? |

### The savings app example

The video builds a summary for an imaginary app that automatically moves money from a user's checking account into a designated savings account twice a month, with the amount set by an algorithm using the checking balance at the time of withdrawal plus other variables such as deposit frequency.

<ul>
<li><strong>Vision</strong> - help users get ahead financially through an algorithm-based automatic deposit system that pulls money into savings.</li>
<li><strong>Key accomplishments</strong> - in the run-up to launch, 1,000 beta users saved over 300,000 dollars in six months, which proved the use case and the need.</li>
<li><strong>Financial highlight</strong> - at 3 dollars per user per month, 1,000 users over six months netted 18,000 dollars.</li>
<li><strong>Lessons learned</strong> - beta testers mainly wanted more frequent updates about when their money was being transferred, so that goes into the update.</li>
</ul>

## Celebrating the team

The last item on the closing checklist is to formally disband and thank the team, and the reading treats celebration as a real project management task rather than a nicety. Celebrations help a team feel recognised and rewarded for the work, and the form should suit the project and the company.

<div class="sc-chips">
  <span class="sc-chip amber">Publicise the successful outcome inside the company</span>
  <span class="sc-chip violet">Request a speaking slot at a team or company all-hands to spotlight the project and the team</span>
  <span class="sc-chip green">Organise a celebration event for the project team</span>
  <span class="sc-chip slate">Recognise individual contributions through awards or superlatives</span>
  <span class="sc-chip soft">Use the employee recognition programmes your organisation already runs</span>
</div>

Documenting and organising the project's components serves the same end from the other direction: it provides **visibility and accountability**, and project team members and senior stakeholders reference and contribute to project documents throughout the work, not only at the end.

## The personal closing report

The final activity applies the same closing discipline to the learner rather than the project. The course calls it **personal closing reporting**: reflecting on completing the certificate exactly as you would reflect on a delivered project, using the retrospective and closeout habits built through the programme.

### What the video asks for

<Steps>

1. **List your key accomplishments.** Look back to the start of the programme: what challenges did you overcome? Concepts you doubted you would ever understand and then did. Difficulties in your personal life you worked around. Fitting the course in alongside a full-time job counts as a major win.

2. **Reflect on lessons learned.** A busy week where a lesson got less attention than it deserved. Discovering which parts you enjoyed, such as stakeholder management, and which you enjoyed less, such as budgeting and procurement.

3. **Decide next steps for the career.** Contacting recruiting companies, asking a current manager for more responsibility, refreshing a resume, setting a target of applying to a number of project management jobs per week.

4. **Put the goals on a timeline** as if they were part of a project you were managing, because project management applies to everyday life as much as to work.

5. **Write your own executive summary** last: your experience of the programme as a whole, your successes, and how you plan to advance in project management.

The framing at the end is that the report is a **project artifact** to keep and look back on, and that finishing the certificate deserves the same treatment you would give a team at the end of a project: celebrate the successes, then keep growing and improving.

## Revision summary

<Aside type="tip">
**One-minute recap**

- **Escalating a problem**: most problems stay in the team; occasionally one goes up to a senior stakeholder with a proposed solution. **Synthesise** from emails, notes, presentations and documents into a coherent summary, because stakeholders should not have to read several documents to understand an issue. Test: how can I communicate a decision in a way that makes it easy for them to decide? Example: tasks are overdue because of supplier issues, so hire a second supplier or push the launch date. The stakeholder can then agree, disagree, or propose their own.
- **Chris, program manager on Google Search**: problem solving is the job. Problems are scope, budget, people or timeline. Identify, build a framework, propose, get buy-in. Always debug the **root cause**, because the symptom is usually a red herring. Charters and trackers are mechanisms, not the job. Build the skill on hobbies and side projects.
- **OKRs**: organisation-wide goal setting pairing a goal with a metric. **Objective** is what to achieve, **key result** is how success is measured. Example: prioritise customer needs, measured by addressing review feedback within 24 hours. They test whether a project deserves to exist, and they are how a problem earns senior attention.
- **Email best practices**: lead with organisational impact in the first two sentences, write a subject line stating topic and required action (urgent, please review, decision needed), keep the body to the problem plus the OKR impact plus the decision needed, attach what they need, proofread.
- **Laura, executive productivity advisor**: keep it concise for executives, research their preferred style in advance, tailor per stakeholder, prepare answers to the five likely questions in an appendix, always bring proposed solutions, open with a **TLDR**, use bullets and bolding, restate the ask and the deadline at the end.
- **Why closing matters**: a thorough close assures that all work is complete, all agreed processes are executed, and everyone formally recognises the project is done. Checklist: close procurement administratively, run a formal closing process, present an impact report, document stakeholder acceptance, disband and thank the team.
- **Retrospective tips**: safe space, model the behaviour, ask what to **start, stop and continue** instead of what went wrong, and remind the team of the milestones reached.
- **Closeout report**: written by project managers for project managers, a blueprint of what was done, how and what was delivered, evaluating quality, budget and schedule. Sections: summary, methodology, performance baseline, key accomplishments, lessons learned, next steps, documentation archive. The **performance baseline** compares planned against actual schedule, cost and scope with a notes column. Compiling every link in one place is **good project hygiene**. It should stand alone as a work sample.
- **Impact report**: for senior stakeholders and sponsors not in the day-to-day, usually a deck. Structure: **executive summary, results, what worked, next steps**. Report impact to improve the service, motivate people, build credibility and share lessons, then amplify it beyond the presentation.
- **Executive summary**: a few sentences to a paragraph covering **project vision, key accomplishments and lessons learned**. Write it last, after the rest of the deck. Check it against SMART goals, the business case and the charter.
- **Sauce and Spoon outcome**: after post-pilot fixes to navigation, waitstaff pacing, payment messaging and a pre-service tablet checklist, the pilot hit its goals: check total up from 65 to 75 dollars, turn time down 30 minutes, guest count up 10 percent overall and 20 percent Downtown, food waste down 25 percent, appetiser sales up 15 percent, checkout at one minute or less, technical issues under 5 percent weekly. Order accuracy stayed short of 98 percent. Launched on 23 April as planned, roughly on budget, with scope far larger than planned. Handover went to Molly Edwards for the next rollouts.
- **Personal closing report**: the same closing technique applied to your own progress. Template sections are executive summary, key accomplishments, lessons learned, next steps, and goals at 1 month, 6 months, 1 year and 5 years. Keep it as a personal project artifact.
</Aside>
