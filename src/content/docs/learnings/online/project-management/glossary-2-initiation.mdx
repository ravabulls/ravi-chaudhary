This is the whole vocabulary of Course 2 as study notes for revision, so I can scan it in a few minutes before a quiz. Every definition is one or two sentences, grouped alphabetically, with a pointer wherever two terms are easy to mix up.

<Aside type="tip">
Read down the letters once for coverage, then go back, cover the right-hand column, and try to define each bolded term from memory. The parentheses flag the pairs that quizzes love to contrast, so give those a second pass.
</Aside>

## A

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Adoption** | A success metric for how readily customers take up a product or service and fit it into their routine without friction. (Adoption is take-up; engagement is what happens afterwards.) |
| **Asana** | A work management platform for planning projects, assigning tasks, automating routine steps, tracking progress, and keeping stakeholders in the loop from one place. |

## B

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Benchmark** | A reference point, usually past performance or industry data, used to judge whether a target is realistic. |
| **Benefits** | The gains a project is expected to produce, whether countable (revenue, time saved) or not (morale, brand perception). |
| **Budget** | The estimated amount of money the project will need to reach completion; one of the three resource buckets alongside people and materials. |
| **Business case** | The justification for running the project at all: the problem or opportunity behind it and the value it is expected to return. |

## C

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Collaboration tools** | Lightweight tools such as email and chat, used for quick check-ins, questions, and everyday conversation about the work. |
| **Conditional formatting** | A spreadsheet feature that colours cells automatically based on their contents, for example red for not started and green for complete. |
| **Cost-benefit analysis** | Adding up the value a project is expected to create and setting it against what the project will cost in money, so leaders can confirm the gains beat the spend. |
| **Customers** | The people or organisation that receives value from the landed project, and usually the ones paying for it. (The customer buys; the user is whoever actually uses the result.) |

## D

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Data validation** | A spreadsheet feature that puts dropdown lists into cells so everyone picks the same wording instead of inventing their own. |
| **Deliverable** | A concrete output produced or handed over at the end of a task, event, or process. (The goal is the outcome you want; a deliverable is one of the things you produce to get there.) |
| **Docs** | A digital word processing application, the usual home for charters, agendas, plans, and meeting notes. |

## E

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Engagement** | A success metric for how often and how meaningfully customers keep interacting with the product over time. (Contrast with adoption, which only measures whether they started.) |

## F

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Function** | A spreadsheet feature that builds formulas for you, so data can be calculated and summarised automatically: sums, averages, dates, percent complete. |

## G

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Gantt chart** | A horizontal bar chart that lays tasks out across a timeline, making it obvious who owns each piece of work and when it is due. |

## H

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Header** | The top cell or cells of a spreadsheet column, labelling what that column holds. |

## I

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Influence** | How much power a stakeholder holds and how strongly their actions can move the project's outcome; the vertical axis of the power grid. (Interest is the horizontal axis, and the two are constantly confused.) |
| **Initiation** | The first phase of the project life cycle, ahead of planning, executing, and closing, where goals, scope, deliverables, success criteria, stakeholders, and resources all get defined. |
| **In-scope** | Work that sits inside the agreed boundaries and moves the project toward its goal. (The mirror of out-of-scope.) |
| **Intangible benefits** | Gains that resist being put into numbers, such as employee morale, customer satisfaction, or stronger brand recognition. |
| **Intangible costs** | Losses that resist being put into numbers, such as damaged morale or reputational harm. (Same idea as intangible benefits, on the other side of the ledger.) |
| **Interest** | How much a stakeholder's own needs are affected by the project's operations and outcomes; the horizontal axis of the power grid. (Interest is about being affected, influence is about holding power.) |

## K

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Key results** | The measurable, time-bound outcomes in an OKR that prove the objective has been met, usually two or three and deliberately ambitious. (In practice a key result is a SMART goal sitting under an objective.) |
| **Key stakeholders** | The people carrying both high influence and high interest, also called key players; they occupy the manage-closely corner of the power grid. |

## L

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Land** | To measure whether the project genuinely achieved what it set out to, judged against the success criteria agreed at the outset. (Launching is handover; landing is the proof it worked.) |
| **Launch** | Delivering the finished result of the project to the client or user. (Stopping here is the classic launch-and-forget mistake.) |

## M

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Materials** | The items the work depends on, both physical (hardware, supplies) and not (software licences, training). |
| **Metrics** | The numbers or figures you measure with, chosen so they reflect the goal honestly rather than just being easy to count. |

## O

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Objective** | The half of an OKR that states what is to be achieved, written to be aspirational and motivating. (The objective inspires; the key results measure.) |
| **Objectives and key results (OKRs)** | A goal-setting method that pairs one aspirational objective with a few measurable key results. (Against SMART goals: OKRs add a strategic objective, use several metrics, run on a quarterly cadence, and are meant to stretch rather than be comfortably attainable.) |
| **Out-of-scope** | Work that falls outside the agreed boundaries and does not serve the goal; letting it in adds time, cost, and risk. |

## P

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Pivot table** | A spreadsheet analysis tool that condenses a large table and exposes the relationships between data points, so patterns become readable. |
| **Power grid** | The two-by-two stakeholder analysis grid plotting influence against interest, producing four strategies: manage closely, meet their needs, show consideration, monitor. |
| **Primary stakeholders** | People affected directly by the project and who benefit directly from its success, typically the sponsor, the customer, team members, and senior leaders. (Secondary stakeholders are only touched indirectly.) |
| **Productivity tools** | Everyday software for producing the artefacts of a project: word processors, spreadsheets, and presentation apps. |
| **Project charter** | The document that pins down all the key details of the project and, once the sponsor and stakeholders approve it, grants the authority to move into planning. (The proposal persuades people to start; the charter defines what was agreed and stays a living reference throughout.) |
| **Project goal** | The outcome the project is meant to deliver, meaning what you have been asked to achieve. (The goal is the destination; the deliverables are what you produce on the way there.) |
| **Project manager** | The person who plans, organises, and oversees the whole project, and who is accountable for how the team and the work perform. |
| **Project proposal** | The document written at the very start of initiation to persuade the organisation that the project is worth doing. (Comes before the charter, is usually authored by a senior leader, and is used only at that early stage.) |
| **Project sponsor** | The person accountable for the project who ensures it delivers the business benefits everyone agreed on; they fund it, release resources, and sign the charter. |

## R

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **RACI chart** | A grid setting each task against each role to show who is Responsible for doing the work, Accountable for the outcome and its approval, Consulted for input, and Informed of the result. (Responsible does it; Accountable owns and approves it, and there is only ever one per task; Consulted gives two-way feedback; Informed simply receives the news.) |
| **Resources** | Everything a project runs on: the budget, the people, the materials, and anything else at your disposal. |
| **Return on investment (ROI)** | A metric that expresses the gain from a project relative to what it cost, so competing options can be compared on the same footing. |

## S

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Scheduling and work management software** | Tools for assigning tasks across several teammates and for tracking and visualising progress; they earn their keep on bigger projects with many tasks and a large team. |
| **Scope** | The agreed boundary of the project: what is included and, just as importantly, what is not. |
| **Scope creep** | Uncontrolled change or growth in scope at any point after the project has begun, arriving from outside (the customer or the market) or from inside (the team adding unrequested polish). |
| **Scope management** | Agreeing in advance how proposed changes will be evaluated, accepted or refused, and then carried out. (Scope is the boundary, creep is the boundary slipping, and management is the process that governs both.) |
| **Secondary stakeholders** | People touched only indirectly by the project's success, such as contractors, partner organisations, investors, or a legal contact. (Primary stakeholders are affected directly.) |
| **Silo** | A situation where the knowledge and the responsibility for a task rest on one person alone, turning them into a single point of failure. |
| **Slides** | Google's digital presentation application, the usual home for stakeholder updates and project decks. |
| **SMART goals** | A checklist for testing whether a goal is well defined: Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Relevant, Time-bound. (Unlike an OKR, a SMART goal is one self-contained statement with a single metric, and it is supposed to be attainable rather than a stretch.) |
| **Spreadsheet** | A tool for organising, transforming, visualising, and manipulating information; the project manager's workhorse for timelines, budgets, trackers, and charts. |
| **Stakeholder analysis** | Mapping out every stakeholder visually, rating each one's interest and influence, and deciding how to involve them; also called stakeholder mapping. |
| **Stakeholder buy-in** | Drawing stakeholders into the decision-making so there is broad agreement and support, which is what keeps a project from being deprioritised or starved of resources. |
| **Stakeholders** | Anyone invested in the project, interested in it, or affected by how it turns out. |
| **Stakes** | The parts of a business, situation, or project that stand to be lost if things go wrong; holding a stake is precisely what makes someone a stakeholder. |
| **Steering committee** | The most senior decision-making body on a project, with the authority to change the budget and approve updates to timeline or scope; the project manager briefs it but does not sit on it. |
| **Success criteria** | The specific, measurable standards used to judge how well the project reached its goals, agreed and documented before the work starts. |

## T

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Team members** | The people who actually build the deliverables, as opposed to those who fund, approve or receive them. They rank as primary stakeholders. |
| **Tools** | Software and instruments a team leans on to keep work visible and coordinated, from task boards and schedules to budget sheets and shared documents. |
| **Triple constraint** | The three heaviest restrictions on any project: scope, time, and cost. Move one and at least one of the others has to give, with quality suffering when they fall out of balance. |
