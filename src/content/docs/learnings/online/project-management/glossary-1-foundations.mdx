This is the full vocabulary of Course 1, written out in my own words rather than the course's, so I can look a term up in seconds instead of hunting back through four module pages. Each entry is one or two sentences, and where two terms are easy to mix up I have flagged the contrast in brackets.

<Aside type="tip">
How to use this: skim the whole list once, then drill the entries that carry a bracketed contrast note. Those pairs - project vs program, classic vs matrix, Agile vs Waterfall, Lean vs Six Sigma - are exactly what the quizzes lean on.
</Aside>

## A

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Agile** | A flexible way of running projects where phases overlap and work is delivered in short repeating cycles, so the team can absorb feedback as it arrives. (Contrast with Waterfall, which closes each phase before opening the next.) |
| **Authority** | The formal power to make project decisions that carry weight across the organization. Project managers often hold very little of it, which is why influence matters so much. |

## B

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Barrier** | Anything standing in the way of progress, from a missing approval to an unavailable person. Clearing barriers is a core part of the PM's job during execution. |
| **Buzzword** | A word or phrase that is fashionable in an industry or a moment in time. Worth knowing for the job hunt, since searching the right buzzword surfaces the right postings. |

## C

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **C-Suite** | The chief-level executives of a company, such as the CEO, CFO, and COO. |
| **Change agent** | Someone inside the organization who drives transformation by pushing it to work more effectively and keep developing. |
| **Change management** | The work of getting a finished project actually adopted by the people who have to live with it. (Delivering the thing is not the finish line; adoption is.) |
| **Classic structure** | An organization arranged top-down, where everyone reports up a single chain of command. (Also called functional; contrast with matrix.) |
| **Closing** | The last phase of the life cycle, where the work is confirmed complete, signed off, reviewed for lessons, handed over, and celebrated. |
| **Contract work** | Work done for a company by people who are not its employees, taken on project by project. |
| **Corporate governance** | The company-wide framework of rules and oversight through which an organization pursues its goals. (Broader than project governance, which covers a single project.) |
| **Cross-functional team** | A team pulled from different departments and skill sets, assembled around one project rather than one function. |
| **Culture mapping** | A tool for laying out a company's values, norms, and everyday behavior, so you can see in advance how a change is likely to land. |

## D

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Delegation** | Handing each task to the person or resource best placed to finish it, instead of absorbing everything yourself. |
| **Deliverable** | A concrete outcome or piece of work the project has to produce, for example a trained support team or 500 collected signatures. |
| **DMAIC** | Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control: the five-step improvement cycle at the heart of Six Sigma and Lean Six Sigma. |

## E

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Effective communication** | Being open and early with plans and information, so nobody on the project has to guess where things stand. |
| **Escalation paths** | The agreed routes for raising a risk or problem to the person who can act on it, and doing so while there is still time to act. |
| **Executing** | The phase in which the team carries out the planned tasks while the project manager tracks progress, removes blockers, and adapts the plan. |

## F

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Feedback mechanism** | Any instrument used to collect input from stakeholders, a survey being the standard example. |
| **Floating task** | A task that could slip its delivery date without harming the timeline or the project's overall success. (Contrast with work on the critical path, where any slip pushes everything.) |
| **Flowchart** | A diagram that maps a process one step at a time, making a project's development path visible at a glance. |
| **Functional manager** | Whoever runs a standing department and controls its staff and budget, so a project manager usually has to borrow people from them rather than command them. |
| **Functional organizations** | Companies divided into departments by function, such as finance, marketing, or engineering. Also known as classic organizations. |

## G

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Governance** | The management framework that determines how decisions get made and who is accountable and responsible for what. |

## I

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Influencing without authority** | Getting teammates to deliver their assigned work when you are not their manager, relying on relationships, clarity, and persuasion instead of formal power. |
| **Initiation** | The opening phase of a project, where goals, deliverables, budget, resources, and people are identified and approval is secured. |
| **Internship** | A short placement that buys hands-on experience in an industry, often the way into a first project role. |
| **Interpersonal skills** | The behaviors that make working with other people go well: communicating, negotiating, defusing conflict, and reading what motivates someone. These are what let you influence without authority. |
| **Iterative** | Working in a way where phases and tasks overlap or repeat rather than queue up one behind another. (Contrast with linear.) |

## K

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Kanban** | An Agile approach and tool that makes work status visible, using a board or chart where cards move across columns as they progress. |

## L

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Lean** | A methodology built around stripping waste out of an operation so that every remaining step adds value. |
| **Lean Six Sigma** | The blend of its two parent methodologies, used when a project has to save money, lift quality, and move through its processes quickly all at once. |
| **Linear** | A project structure in which each phase or task must be finished before the next one can begin. (Waterfall is the classic linear method.) |

## M

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Matrix structure** | A grid-shaped hybrid organization where people have a direct manager to report to and also answer to stakeholders from other departments or programs. (Contrast with classic, which has one reporting line only.) |
| **Mission** | A statement that pins down what an organization does, who it does it for, and why. |

## O

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Organizational culture** | The shared values, history, and habits that give a company its personality and decide how work really gets done. |
| **Organizational structure** | The way a company arranges its people, departments, and reporting lines. |
| **Ownership** | The feeling that a person is genuinely empowered to take responsibility for getting their own tasks finished. |

## P

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Planning** | Building and maintaining the schedules, timelines, budgets, and documentation that make a project trackable, using whatever productivity tools and processes fit. |
| **Program manager** | A project manager who runs several related projects at once for a given product, team, or program. (At Google this title is common even for people doing ordinary project management.) |
| **Project** | A temporary, one-off effort with a defined start and finish that produces a unique set of deliverables. (Contrast with operations, which is ongoing and repeating, and with a program, which bundles related projects together.) |
| **Project governance** | The framework that sets how decisions get made inside a single project. |
| **Project life cycle** | The shared structure every project runs through: initiate the project, make a plan, execute and complete tasks, then close it. |
| **Project management** | Applying knowledge, skills, tools, and techniques to the work of a project so that its requirements are met. |
| **Project management methodology** | A defined set of guiding principles and processes for carrying a project through its life cycle, such as Waterfall, Scrum, or Lean Six Sigma. |
| **Project management office** | An internal group, usually shortened to PMO, that defines and maintains project management standards for the whole organization. |
| **Project manager** | The person who shepherds a project from start to finish, guiding the team with organizational and interpersonal skills the whole way. |
| **Project task** | A single activity that has to be completed inside a set window by the project manager, the team, or a stakeholder. |

## R

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Reporting chart** | A diagram of the people and groups in an organization showing who reports to whom. |
| **Resource availability** | Knowing how to get hold of the people, equipment, and budget a project needs, and when they can be had. |
| **Resources** | Everything a project draws on to get finished: people, equipment, software, vendors, budget, and physical space. |
| **Retrospective** | A meeting or workshop with the team after a project or phase, held to capture what worked and what to run differently next time. |
| **Risk** | An event that might happen and would affect the project if it did. (A risk is still potential; a barrier is already in the way.) |

## S

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Scrum** | An Agile framework in which a small cross-functional team, guided by a Scrum Master, builds complex work collaboratively across short sprints with an agreed list of deliverables. |
| **Six Sigma** | A methodology that cuts variation out of the work by making sure the same quality process is followed every single time. (Lean removes waste; Six Sigma removes variation.) |
| **Sprint** | A fixed, short stretch of time in Agile, typically one to four weeks, in which the team completes a set list of deliverables. |
| **Stakeholder** | Anyone with an interest in the project or affected by whether it succeeds. |
| **Steering committees** | A senior group that sets an organization's priorities and steers the general course of its operations. |

## T

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Transferable skill** | An ability that carries from one job or career path to another, such as organizing, budgeting, or leading a group. |

## U

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Urgency** | Getting the team to feel that the project matters now, and to see which specific actions will move it forward. |

## V

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Values** | The principles that describe how people in an organization are expected to behave. |

## W

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Waterfall** | A methodology in which phases run one after another in a fixed sequence, best suited to work whose requirements are unlikely to change. (Contrast with Agile, which loops and expects change.) |
