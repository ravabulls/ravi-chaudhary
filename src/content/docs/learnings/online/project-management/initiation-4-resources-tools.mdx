Having set the **goals**, **scope**, and **stakeholders**, the last piece of initiation is the practical one: what the project *runs on*. This module is about three linked things - the **resources** a project needs, the **documentation** that pins the plan down (above all the **project charter**), and the **tools** that keep the work organised.

<Aside type="note">
**Where this sits:** resources, charter, and tools are still *initiation* work - they're decided before planning begins, so the team starts with funding, people, and a shared source of truth already in place.
</Aside>

---

## Project resources

<Aside type="note">
**Project resources** - everything (and everyone) a project depends on to get done. Three buckets: **budget**, **people**, and **materials**.
</Aside>

The three are **interdependent**: the team can't work without materials, and you can't buy materials without a budget. Miss one and the others stall.

| Resource | What it covers | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Budget** | The money the project needs - team cost, outside services/vendors, purchased materials, marketing | Hidden costs: taxes, fees, overtime; keep it aligned to scope |
| **People** | Everyone doing the work - you, internal teammates, and external specialists with skills you lack in-house | Get real commitment on the *hours* each task takes; avoid understaffing |
| **Materials** | Tangible items (lumber, hard drives, computers) *and* intangible ones (software licences, cloud storage, training) | Account for everything up front so nothing surprises the budget |

**Why plan resources early (during initiation).** Figuring out resources before the work starts is what lets everyone actually do their job. Leave it late and you risk two classic failures:

<div class="sc-chips">
  <span class="sc-chip amber">Understaffing → progress stalls, timeline slips</span>
  <span class="sc-chip amber">Underbudgeting → can't buy materials, hire vendors, or cover overtime</span>
</div>

<Aside type="tip">
How to find your resources: **interview** stakeholders and likely team members about what they think they'll need. They often name materials you hadn't budgeted for, or point you to a person whose expertise makes them an asset.
</Aside>

## Documentation - and why it matters

A big part of the job is **guiding decisions** - and there are far too many (goals, deliverables, people, costs) for one person to hold in their head. Documentation makes those decisions visible to everyone, not just the project manager.

| Documentation gives you | Why it helps |
| --- | --- |
| **Transparency** | Everyone can see goals, scope, deliverables, stakeholders, and resources |
| **A reference point** | People who join mid-project can catch up without re-asking |
| **Discovery** | Writing it down surfaces tasks, timelines, or costs you hadn't considered |
| **A historical record** | Reviewed at close, so lessons carry into future projects |

## Proposal vs charter

Two early documents are easy to confuse. They answer different questions at different moments.

| | Project **proposal** | Project **charter** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Purpose** | *Persuade* stakeholders the project should begin | *Define* the project and its key details |
| **When** | Very start of initiation | End of initiation (before planning) |
| **Usually written by** | A senior organisational leader | The project manager |
| **Lifespan** | Used only at the early stage | A **living document** referenced all the way through |

## The project charter

<Aside type="note">
**Project charter** - a formal document that clearly defines the project and outlines the details needed to reach its goals. Think of it as the project's **compass**: you steer by it for the whole life cycle.
</Aside>

**What the charter does for you:**

- Shows the **benefits outweigh the costs** (it carries the cost-benefit analysis - value created, money saved, time required).
- Gets everyone to **agree on the value and the details** before planning starts.
- Once the **sponsor and stakeholders approve it**, you have the **authority to proceed**.
- Is **living** - you review and refine it as the project evolves.

### What goes in a charter

Charters vary by organisation, project, and audience (a marketing exec cares about brand impact; a CTO cares about engineering cost). Most pull from this menu:

| Section | Section |
| --- | --- |
| Introduction / summary | Success criteria |
| Goals / objectives | Key deliverables / requirements |
| Business case (benefits & costs) | Budget |
| Project team | Schedule / milestones |
| Scope (in and out) | Constraints, assumptions, risks |
| Stakeholders | OKRs · Approvals |

<Aside type="tip">
Match detail to the project: a **short, simple** charter suits small, low-complexity projects; for complex ones, keep the charter lean and push extra analysis into an **appendix**. Build it *with* your team and stakeholders so it captures their real concerns, and let the **business case** (the reason for doing the project) steer what you include.
</Aside>

### A worked mini-charter

A condensed example, using the running "Plant Pals" office-plant service (goal: grow revenue 5%):

| Field | Example entry |
| --- | --- |
| **Summary** | Offer high-volume customers small, low-maintenance office plants |
| **Goal (SMART)** | Increase revenue 5% by launching an office-plant service for top clients by year end |
| **Deliverables** | Ship 1,000 plants to 100 customers; launch an orders/support website |
| **Business case** | Top-requested service; improves customer satisfaction and retention |
| **Benefits vs costs** | Benefits: satisfaction + revenue. Costs: sourcing, website, marketing - est. **$250,000** |
| **In scope / out of scope** | In: deliver small plants to last year's top clients. Out: plant care after delivery |
| **Success criteria** | +5% revenue by year end; 95% customer-satisfaction rate three months after launch |

<Aside type="caution">
Benefits should **always outweigh cost** - that comparison is the charter's whole argument. Real projects use a far more detailed cost-benefit analysis than a teaching example.
</Aside>

### Drafting a charter with a gen-AI tool

A gen-AI assistant (e.g. Gemini) can produce a **first draft** fast and save time - but the output is a starting point, not a finished charter.

<Steps>

1. **Feed it real detail.** Prompt like *"Act as a project manager and build a project charter from the information below; add placeholders for anything missing,"* then paste your project description, business need, deliverables, stakeholders, and success criteria. The more you give, the more complete the draft.

2. **Evaluate the output.** Read closely, don't skim - it may include things you didn't intend, or bullets that need expanding into full sentences. *The AI does the heavy lifting; you still do the heavy thinking.*

3. **Iterate.** Refine the prompt: ask for less technical language, shorter/longer sections, a specific number of pages, or "what important information is missing?" Share an existing template or an **example** (the *References* idea) to steer format.

</Steps>

<Aside type="tip">
This maps onto a prompt framework: **E**valuate the result and **I**terate by adjusting wording, giving **R**eferences/examples, and breaking instructions into shorter sentences.
</Aside>

## Tools

<Aside type="note">
**Tools** - aids that make it easier to manage resources and organise work: track tasks, deliverables, and milestones, manage budgets, build charts, and keep everyone informed.
</Aside>

Effective **communication** and **tracking** are the bulk of a PM's day, and tools power both. Crucially, information sharing goes **both ways** - teammates update you on progress without extra meetings, which raises **visibility and transparency** for the whole team, stakeholders included. (Picture the alternative: project details on a whiteboard everyone must walk over to read.)

### Straightforward vs sophisticated

| | Straightforward | Sophisticated |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Examples** | Docs, spreadsheets, email, chat | Scheduling / work-management software (e.g. Asana) |
| **Best for** | Small projects, few tasks/people | Big scope, many tasks, larger teams |
| **Trade-off** | Fast to adopt | Powerful, but the team needs time to learn it |

<Aside type="caution">
Sometimes the choice isn't yours - if the organisation has standardised on a tool, you work with what you're given. Stay **flexible**.
</Aside>

### Types of tool

| Type | What it's for | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Scheduling / work management** | Assign tasks, track progress, visualise team throughput | Asana, Monday.com, Basecamp, Trello, Jira, Smartsheet |
| **Productivity** | Create project artefacts - charters, agendas, plans, RACI charts, decks | Docs/Word, Sheets/Excel, Slides/PowerPoint |
| **Collaboration** | Quick check-ins and questions | Email, chat |

<Aside type="tip">
Work-management software shines at spotting trouble: if a team closes 50 tasks one week and 3 the next, the tool makes the drop obvious so you know to check in. Most tools share the same core features (task tracking, assignment) - learn **one deeply** and you adapt to others quickly.
</Aside>

### Spreadsheets are the versatile workhorse

Sheets/Excel do far more than lists. Handy features for PM:

| Feature | Use |
| --- | --- |
| **Sharing permissions** | Edit / comment / view-only when you share |
| **Freeze rows/columns** | Keep header titles visible while scrolling |
| **Conditional formatting** | Auto-colour status: Not started (red), In progress (orange), Complete (green) |
| **Dropdown lists (data validation)** | Force consistent entries ("done" vs "complete") |
| **Sort & filter** | Order by due date; show only your own tasks |
| **Pivot tables / charts** | Summarise data and present it to stakeholders |
| **Functions** | Auto-calc sums, averages, dates, % complete |

Common templates worth reusing: a **Project Timeline** (whole life cycle), a **Project Tracking** sheet (budget, deliverables, costs), and a **Gantt chart** (tasks over time with dependencies).

### Introducing a new tool to a team

People resist change, especially mid-project. Roll a new tool out deliberately:

<Steps>

1. **Discuss it early and often** - no one should feel blindsided; give the team time to prepare.

2. **Ask key stakeholders for feedback** - on features and priorities; incorporate it into next steps.

3. **Involve them in demos** - let them trial the tool; pull in key users to test before rollout, which also surfaces in-house experts for later training.

4. **Ensure it's fully functional and accessible** before introducing it - a buggy tool kills adoption.

5. **Set up training** as needed - different people have different comfort levels; good first impressions raise adoption.

6. **Plan a transition** when replacing a tool - run both in parallel for a while, then **sunset** (retire) the old one; expect a temporary dip in productivity.

</Steps>

<Aside type="tip">
**For interviews & your career:** be ready to name tools you've used and *how*. No experience with a specific tool? Map a real-life example (planning a house move with a spreadsheet for budget and schedule). Building a personal **tool tracker** - a sheet listing each tool's description, link, features, cost, and your experience level - is a good way to learn the landscape and show employers you understand the industry.
</Aside>
