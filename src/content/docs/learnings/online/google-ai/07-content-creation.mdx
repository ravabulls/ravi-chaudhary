The course opens with a small, well-chosen demonstration of its own premise. *I could try to describe a dream I had last night - or better yet, I could just show you.* Sometimes words on a screen or a verbal description are not enough to bring an idea to life.

Most people would happily make their work stand out with good visuals. The reason they do not is stated plainly: **this can be out of reach if you're not a designer.** That is the gap the course is about - not making designers faster, but removing the requirement to be one.

<Aside type="note">
**The claim:** with Gemini you can create and transform content **across media types**. Build marketing materials or images for a website. Take a report and turn it into a presentation. Create a video from a prompt. All of it rests on one capability - **multimodality**.
</Aside>

## Multimodality

### The definition, and why it is more than a feature list

**Modalities** are different types of format: text, image, audio, video. **Multimodality** is the ability to work across them - and the course's definition of the useful part is precise: you can **make a request in one format and get an output in a different format**.

The fundamentals course adds the second half of the argument, which is about input rather than output: multimodality lets you give Gemini **more detailed and richer context when prompting**. Instead of telling it what you want using only words, you enrich the instruction with images, video and audio. Its three examples - a video from a single sentence, an email drafted from pictures of your products, an entire presentation built from audio recordings - are chosen to show both directions at once.

## Nano Banana Pro

### What the lab uses

The image lab runs on **Nano Banana Pro**, described as an image-generation and editing model available in the Gemini app, which you can prompt **with text or with images** to create and edit high-quality visuals.

Three stated gains from the lab:

| Gain | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Visual storytelling** | Creating custom imagery that matches your specific vision without complex graphic design software |
| **Multimodal fluency** | The nuances of prompting for images - **specifying style, lighting and composition** - and of using images themselves to guide AI |
| **Streamlined workflow** | Generating, refining and combining creative elements such as copy and visuals **within a single chat** |

And three role-specific applications: a social media image for a product launch if you work in marketing; visuals to make a slide deck more engaging if you work in sales; visual aids for a lesson plan if you teach.

<Aside type="caution">
**Access, as the course states it.** This lab requires **Google AI Pro**, for higher access to Google's most capable models. Learners enrolled in the certificate are eligible for a **complimentary three-month trial**, which has to be activated before starting. The lab is **optional** - not required to progress through the course or earn the certificate - but strongly recommended.
</Aside>

<Aside type="note">
**Beyond the recording - prompting an image so the result is usable.** The lab teaches this and leaves no transcript, and it is the most learnable skill in the course because image prompts respond to structure in a way text prompts do not. A usable image prompt names six things, roughly in this order:

| Slot | Example | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Subject** | *a mid-sized container ship at a quay* | Be concrete. *A logistics scene* produces a stock-photo average |
| **Action or state** | *being loaded, two cranes in motion* | Stops the default static, centred composition |
| **Setting and light** | *northern European port, overcast late afternoon, flat diffuse light* | Light does more for believability than any other word in the prompt |
| **Composition and framing** | *wide shot, low angle, ship occupying the right two-thirds* | The single most neglected slot, and the one that makes an image look designed rather than generated |
| **Style** | *editorial photography, 35mm, shallow depth of field* - or *flat vector illustration, two colours* | Name a medium, not a mood. *Professional* means nothing |
| **What it is for** | *leave clear space in the upper left for a headline* | Negative space, aspect ratio, and whether anything has to sit on top of it |

Four things that separate people who get what they wanted from people who give up:

- **Iterate on the image, not the prompt.** Nano Banana Pro edits as well as generates: *"same image, change the light to early morning"*, *"remove the figure on the left"*. Rewriting the prompt from scratch throws away everything that was already right.
- **Use an image as part of the prompt.** A rough sketch, a screenshot of a layout, or a photograph of the actual product constrains the output far more precisely than adjectives. This is the *using images to guide AI* half of multimodal fluency.
- **Text in images used to be the giveaway and no longer is.** Nano Banana Pro is specifically built for **precise text rendering** and 4K output, which is what makes it usable for anything with a word on it - a poster, a banner, a slide. Still read every character before you ship it.
- **Ask for consistency explicitly** when you need a set. *"Same character, same colour palette, same illustration style, now in a different scene."* Series consistency is the hardest thing to get and the thing a brand actually needs.
</Aside>

## Video, and combining models

The course's text-to-video claim is that you can create a video **with just a prompt**, and its demonstration includes taking an image and turning it into a video.

The app-building course adds the idea that makes this more than a novelty. One of the Google practitioners describes **combining models**: generate an image, bring it to life with **Veo**, then create a story around it with Gemini and pass the video back for more. His phrase - *chain all these things* - and the reassuring part: in AI Studio you do not need to know how to chain them. *You just need to describe your idea.* Say *"I have the story, I want to imagine the storyboard, then I want to animate that storyboard"* and it ties the models together.

<Aside type="note">
**Beyond the recording - the current video models, and what they cost.** The course says *Veo* without a version. As of writing:

| Model | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Veo 3.1** | Cinematic video generation with creative controls and **natively synchronised audio** - the audio is generated with the video rather than added after |
| **Gemini Omni Flash** | Video generation, editing and **interpolation** with integrated audio - the one to reach for when you are modifying existing footage rather than creating from nothing |
| **Nano Banana 2** | The faster, production-scale image model, for volume |
| **Nano Banana Pro** | The professional one: 4K, precise text rendering |

The commercial fact the certificate never mentions, and which bites immediately if you move from the app to the API: **image, video and music models have no free tier at all.** Text, speech and embedding models have one; generated media does not. Inside the Gemini app this is hidden behind the AI Pro subscription, which is why the lab requires it. The moment you build something that generates images for other people, every image is a line on a bill. See [the Gemini API chapter](/learnings/online/google-ai/11-gemini-api/).
</Aside>

<Aside type="tip">
**Beyond the recording - the realistic verdict on generated video.** Generated video is the most impressive and least immediately useful thing in this course, and it is worth being clear about why so the disappointment arrives before the work does.

**What it is genuinely good for now:** short establishing shots, abstract or illustrative backgrounds, product-in-motion for a few seconds, animated versions of a still you already have, and - the honest best use - **storyboards and pitch material**, where the point is to show an idea rather than to ship the final asset. The [deployment course's](/learnings/online/google-ai/10-app-deployment/) own demo app is a storyboard generator, which is a telling choice.

**What it is still bad at:** continuity across shots, anything with legible dialogue or specific hand movements, consistent faces, and any duration past a few seconds without visible drift. It is also slow and expensive relative to everything else here.

**So the pattern that works** is the one the practitioner describes: generate the still you want first, get it right, *then* animate it. Starting from text and hoping for a finished video inverts the control you have.
</Aside>

## Format transformation

The capability with the lowest glamour and the highest hit rate. Start with something in a Google Doc - *your messy notes* - or a PDF, a spreadsheet, an audio file or another format, and direct AI to turn it into something else entirely.

<Aside type="tip">
**Beyond the recording - the transformations that earn their place.** Not all pairings are equally worth doing. These are the ones that reliably save real time:

| From | To | Why it works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Meeting recording or notes** | Action items with owners and dates | The classic, and still the best. Mentioned in the fundamentals course by a practitioner who dreaded writing exactly this email |
| **Long report or deck** | A one-page brief for one named person | Forces the model to select rather than compress |
| **Spreadsheet** | The three charts that matter, plus what each shows | Saves the worst part of analysis, which is deciding what to plot |
| **Your own messy notes** | A presentation outline with speaker notes | The course's own example, and the outline is the hard part |
| **A document** | Five questions it provokes | Turns a read into a conversation, and surfaces what is missing |
| **A written process** | A checklist someone else can follow | Where the gaps in your own understanding become visible |

The one to be careful with is **report into infographic**, which the course itself flags: if you are **representing something as fact**, double-check that AI got it right. A number that is wrong in a paragraph is an error; the same number wrong in a chart on a slide behind you is a different kind of problem, because nobody reads a chart sceptically.
</Aside>

## Your role, and the limits

The course closes on the same division of labour as every other course in the certificate, with content-specific wording. AI is a **thought partner, not your replacement**. Three instructions:

<Steps>

1. **Review everything it produces** and make sure it matches your perspective or your brand.

2. **If you are representing something as fact** - turning a report into an infographic, for instance - **double-check that AI got it right**.

3. **Exercise judgment** by asking whether you are using AI in a way that is appropriate **given the context and the audience**.

</Steps>

The promised end state: quickly prototype, create engaging content, and get feedback and pointers about your work.

<Aside type="caution">
**Beyond the recording - the three questions about generated media that the course does not raise.** Images and video differ from text in that they get published, and publication brings obligations that a draft email does not.

**Can you use it commercially?** Generally yes for output of the major paid services, and the providers typically assert you may use what you generate - but this is a **terms-of-service question, not a settled legal one**, and the terms differ between a free tier and a paid plan. Check the terms for the specific tool and tier you used, and keep a note of which tool produced which asset. That note is the [accountability trail](/learnings/online/google-ai/03-responsible-ai/) in its most literal form.

**Did you generate something that belongs to someone else?** The real risk is not abstract copyright but the concrete things models reproduce when asked: **trademarks, logos, recognisable brand elements, and the likeness of real people**. A model asked for *"a sports shoe advertisement"* may well give you something that strongly resembles a brand you do not own. Look at what you generated before it goes anywhere, and never generate a real person's likeness for anything that implies they said or did something.

**Do you have to label it?** In the EU, since **2 August 2026**, Article 50 of the AI Act requires AI-generated content to be marked in machine-readable form, and requires deepfakes to be labelled. **SynthID** watermarking handles the machine-readable part for content generated by participating Google models - but, as covered in [responsible AI](/learnings/online/google-ai/03-responsible-ai/), a watermark is not a disclosure. The visible label is still yours to add, and the *T* in ACT is now a legal duty in the EU for the cases the Article covers, not only good practice.

One smaller thing, worth building into the habit because nobody else will: **write the alt text**. A generated image published without it is inaccessible, and the model that made the image will write a better description of it than you will.
</Aside>
