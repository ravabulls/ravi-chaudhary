*Google AI Professional Certificate - Course 4: AI for Writing and Communicating*

---

The course opens on an email you have probably sent. *"HEY, TEAM!"* in capitals, an emoji, and - if you are that person - a corny dad joke to keep them reading.

Now imagine the same email going to your CEO. Or to a new client. The CEO probably needs a quick bullet-point summary. The client needs reassurance and detailed solutions. Same information, three completely different pieces of writing, and without help, producing all three could take hours.

The course is clear that the cost of getting this wrong is not aesthetic. **If someone misunderstands your message or your tone, you can lose their trust** - and that leads to more questions, more meetings and more reviews. Things get slow, inefficient and draining fast.

<Aside type="note">
**The framing:** you are doing more than sharing information, you are **trying to achieve an objective**. The principles of effective communication - knowing your audience, delivering the message well - are called timeless and unchanged. What is new is being able to deliver on those principles in a different way.
</Aside>

## The five elements that stay yours

The course's structure here is its best contribution: a list of what AI specifically cannot take over, with a reason for each.

| Element | What it is | Why only you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Purpose and intent** | Defining the *why* - what you want the message to **do** | Do you want to persuade, to inform, to de-escalate, or to inspire? Nothing in the text tells AI which |
| **Understanding your audience** | Who is receiving this and what they care about | Only you know your leadership's priorities, your client's needs and your team's mindset - and AI has no access to the **context and history of your relationship** with them |
| **Authenticity and voice** | That it sounds like you | You are the final editor who makes sure the message matches your personal or company brand and values |
| **Accountability** | Owning the impact | You are responsible for the impact your words may have and any consequences they bring, **so read every word** |
| **Strategic judgment** | Choosing the vehicle | Email or phone call? And - the course says this explicitly - in some cases **no message at all** |

That last row is the one worth dwelling on. A tool that makes writing nearly free creates a bias toward writing, and *send nothing* stays a legitimate option that no drafting assistant will ever suggest.

<Aside type="caution">
**What the course deliberately does not list.** *Ghostwriter* is not among AI's responsibilities, and the reasoning is given: relying on it completely results in communication that is **missing important context or just doesn't feel authentic**. The intended use is to speed up the editing process and help bring your voice to life - give your wild ideas structure, get you past the blank page - so that you spend **less time struggling with how to say something and more time improving what you want to say**.
</Aside>

The outcomes the course promises from doing this well are specific and modest, which makes them more credible than they would be otherwise: your emails actually get read and people follow through; your communication is clearer, reducing back-and-forth and getting to quicker decisions; and your perspective is better understood by teammates and clients, so you are more effective at informing or changing their opinions.

<Aside type="note">
**Beyond the recording - what Canvas is.** The course names it without explaining it. **Canvas** is a side-by-side workspace inside the Gemini app: the chat stays on one side, and the document being produced lives on the other, where it persists and can be edited directly rather than being regenerated in the chat stream.

Why that matters for this course specifically:

- **You can edit the draft yourself and then ask for changes to your edited version**, rather than re-prompting and getting a wholly new draft that has lost the good sentence you liked.
- **It is built for variants.** Asking for the same message at three lengths or in three tones produces something you can compare, which is the actual task in this course.
- **It survives the chat.** A long conversation of iterations does not bury the document.

It also handles code, which is why it reappears in [data analysis](/learnings/online/google-ai/08-data-analysis/) for interactive forecasts.
</Aside>

<Aside type="tip">
**Beyond the recording - the audience problem, solved in one prompt.** The course sets up the three-audiences problem beautifully and then leaves the mechanics to a lab. The move that does the work is asking for all versions **at once, from one brief**, so the substance stays identical and only the framing changes:

> *Here is what happened: [the facts, written plainly and once]. Write three versions. (1) For my CEO: five bullets, no preamble, lead with the impact on the quarter. (2) For the client: warm, acknowledges the disruption, names the specific remedy and the date. (3) For my team: direct, says what changes for them on Monday, no corporate language. Keep the facts identical across all three.*

The final instruction is the one that matters. Generated separately, three messages drift apart, and someone will eventually put two of them side by side.
</Aside>

## Sharing disappointing news

The course lists this among the scenarios worth brainstorming and then, like much of this hour, hands it to an activity. It is the hardest writing task most people face at work, so it is worth completing.

<Aside type="note">
**Beyond the recording - the structure, and where AI helps.** Bad-news messages fail in a predictable way: they bury the news under context, so the reader scans for what went wrong, does not find it, and arrives at it already anxious. The order that works runs against the instinct:

<Steps>

1. **The news, in the first sentence.** *"The launch is moving to 14 November."* Not the background, not the appreciation of everyone's hard work.

2. **The why, briefly.** Two sentences. Enough to be respectful of their intelligence, not enough to read as a defence.

3. **What you are doing about it**, with a date and a name attached. This is the part that determines whether you look in control.

4. **What it means for them specifically.** The only part most readers actually need, and the part most often missing.

5. **The channel for questions.** Reply, call, or a slot already booked.

</Steps>

AI is genuinely good at two things here and bad at a third:

- **Good at the draft**, because the structure above is a form and forms are what it does well.
- **Very good as a reader.** *"Read this as the client who was promised October. What is the first thing you feel, and what is the first question you ask?"* That is the course's refinement-and-feedback job pointed at the thing that actually matters.
- **Bad at the decision.** Whether to send this at all, whether it should be a phone call first, and who hears it before it is written down are **strategic judgment** from the list above. A drafting tool will always produce the draft.
</Aside>

## Tone, voice and translation

### Tone

Among the refinement jobs: instantly rephrase something to make it clearer, and **adjust tone of voice from formal to casual**.

<Aside type="tip">
**Beyond the recording - tone words that actually change the output.** *Professional* is the least useful instruction in this area, because almost everything in the training data is professional. The adjustments that produce a visible difference are concrete:

| Instead of | Ask for |
| --- | --- |
| *More professional* | *Remove every intensifier and adverb. Shorter sentences.* |
| *Friendlier* | *Second person. Contractions. One sentence of genuine acknowledgement, no flattery.* |
| *More confident* | *Delete every hedge - no "I think", "perhaps", "it seems". State things.* |
| *More concise* | *Cut it to 40 percent. Keep every fact. Lose every transition.* |
| *Less corporate* | *No nouns made from verbs. No "leverage", "utilise", "going forward". Say who does what.* |

The pattern: name the **edit**, not the feeling. A model can execute *delete the hedges*; it can only guess at *more confident*.
</Aside>

### Voice

The course makes authenticity your job and does not say how to do it, which leaves the most common complaint about AI writing unaddressed: it does not sound like you.

<Aside type="caution">
**Beyond the recording - getting your own voice out of it, and the tells to remove.** Two things work, in this order.

**Give it samples, not adjectives.** Paste three things you actually wrote - ideally the ones you were happiest with - and say *"match the voice in these, including the sentence length and how much I hedge."* This is [few-shot prompting](/learnings/online/google-ai/02-prompting/) applied to style, and it beats any description of your voice you could write.

**Then edit out the tells.** Current models have recognisable habits, and readers have learnt them. The ones worth hunting:

- **Tricolon everywhere.** Three-part lists in every paragraph: *clear, concise and compelling.* One is rhetoric; four in a row is a signature.
- **The "it's not X, it's Y" construction**, used as a substitute for an argument.
- **Openings that restate the question** before answering it.
- **Uniform paragraph length**, every one three or four sentences, which is the most reliable tell of all and the least noticed.
- **Hedged conclusions** that review the considerations and recommend nothing.
- **Closing paragraphs that summarise what was just said** in a short message that did not need a summary.

The reason to care is not stylistic pride. It is the course's own point about trust: writing that reads as generated reads as not-written-for-me, and the reader discounts it accordingly. **Read every word** - the accountability row - turns out to be the practical fix as well as the ethical one.
</Aside>

### Translation

A practitioner makes a case for what they call one of the most underrated AI features. Their parents primarily speak Korean and they mostly speak English, and they use translation tools constantly and find them genuinely useful. But LLMs, in some cases, do a **much better job conveying the tone and the spirit** of what the person was trying to say, and expressing it in a language the other person can understand.

The distinction is between translating **words** and translating **intent**, and it is the difference between a message that is accurate and a message that lands.

<Aside type="note">
**Beyond the recording - what to specify when the languages have forms English does not.** The practitioner's point generalises, but it needs one addition for anyone writing in a language with grammatical formality - which is most of them, and certainly German.

A model asked to translate into German must choose between **du** and **Sie**, and it will choose, with no idea which is right. The same applies to Korean speech levels, Japanese keigo, French *tu*/*vous* and Spanish *tú*/*usted*. Getting it wrong is not a small stylistic miss; addressing a new business contact as *du* reads as presumptuous, and addressing a close colleague as *Sie* reads as cold or sarcastic.

So specify it, along with the three other things that do not survive a literal translation:

- **The form of address.** *"Use Sie. This is a first email to a potential client."*
- **The register.** Business German is noticeably more formal and more nominal than business English; an English email translated at face value often reads as oddly casual.
- **What to leave in English.** Technical and business vocabulary is frequently borrowed, and translating it produces something nobody says. *"Keep the technical terms in English."*
- **The intent, stated.** *"The point of this message is to decline politely without closing the door."* Give the model the objective and it translates toward it rather than toward the words.

Then do the obvious thing, which is to **read it in the target language before sending it.** If you cannot, the course's own principle applies: accountability does not transfer to the tool.
</Aside>

## Practice and simulation with Gemini Live

### What the demo shows

The best-documented activity in this course is a product marketer on the Gemini app team demonstrating **Gemini Live** for high-stakes conversations - sales pitches, presentations, difficult one-on-ones.

Two things about it to note first. **Gemini Live needs the mobile app**, so this is a phone exercise. And the interaction is different in kind from everything else in the certificate: *when we use Gemini Live, we're not prompting, we're having a natural, free-flowing conversation.*

The setup, which is the part worth copying:

<Steps>

1. **Declare the exercise.** *"Hey Gemini, I need to make an important sales call. Let's role-play."*

2. **Give it the scenario and the character, then hand it the role.** *"I'm a salesperson for a logistics company called Cardboard Caravan. My goal is to book a meeting with the logistics manager at Terry's Trousers, who recently submitted an inquiry on our website. I call and you answer the phone."*

3. **Then actually do it.** The transcript runs a full discovery conversation - the manager explains they want to streamline shipping and reduce costs after delays and inefficiencies, the salesperson acknowledges, references other companies in that position, and asks for a brief follow-up meeting. It ends with a meeting next week and a case study to send.

4. **Ask for feedback afterwards.** *"Provide feedback on my approach, including strengths, weaknesses, and suggestions for improvement."*

</Steps>

The reaction to the feedback in the demo is an unguarded *"huh, wow"* - which is the most informative moment in the hour, because it suggests the feedback was better than expected.

### The second use

The same practitioner uses Live for something else: talking through an out-of-the-box concept for a product launch, having Gemini help flesh it out, and then **synthesising the conversation into an action plan** with clearly outlined next steps to share with leadership. Her phrase for it: a great way to go from idea to execution.

And a third practitioner describes a weekly ritual built the same way. Starting a new role, with a week full of one-on-ones and new cross-functional stakeholders, she took notes through the week, then at the end of it pulled up Gemini Live, **talked through all those notes**, uploaded documents where necessary, and worked with Gemini to produce a succinct, actionable end-of-week wrap-up with the **open questions** she had, which she sent to her new manager. Her account of the value is two-sided: it let the manager track her progress, and it showed *her* where there were opportunities to add value and what she still did not know.

<Aside type="tip">
**Beyond the recording - how to run a roleplay that is actually hard.** The demo's roleplay goes well, which makes it a poor rehearsal. A cooperative counterpart teaches you nothing, and the reason most people try this once and stop is that it feels too easy to be useful. Fix it by specifying difficulty:

- **Set the disposition explicitly.** *"You are sceptical, you have been burnt by a vendor before, and you have eleven minutes. Do not be helpful."*
- **Give it a hidden objective.** *"Your actual goal is to get a discount without committing to anything. Do not state this."* Now you are practising against a real negotiating position.
- **Ask it to interrupt.** Models are relentlessly polite and will let you finish every sentence, which no difficult counterpart does.
- **Run the same scene twice** and change only your opening. The difference in where the conversation goes is the lesson.
- **Then ask the right feedback question.** Not *"how did I do"* but: *"Where did I lose you? Which single sentence of mine most reduced your willingness to meet? What did I fail to ask?"*

The scenario this is most valuable for is the one nobody practises: the conversation you are **dreading**. Declining a request from someone senior, raising a problem you should have raised a month ago, asking for something you are not sure you deserve. Three minutes of saying it badly to a phone is worth more than an hour of composing it in your head - and it is the one application in this certificate with no output to show anyone, which is probably why it gets skipped.
</Aside>

## Revision summary

<Aside type="tip">
**One-minute recap**

- **The opening:** *"HEY, TEAM!"* with an emoji works for close teammates and fails for your CEO or a new client. The CEO wants a bullet summary; the client wants reassurance and detailed solutions. Doing all three by hand takes hours.
- **The real cost:** misunderstood message or tone loses trust, which produces more questions, more meetings and more reviews. You are not sharing information, you are **achieving an objective**.
- **Five elements that stay yours:** **purpose and intent** (persuade, inform, de-escalate, inspire); **understanding your audience** (AI has no access to the history of your relationship with them); **authenticity and voice**; **accountability** - you own the impact, so **read every word**; **strategic judgment** - email or call, and sometimes **no message at all**.
- **Ghostwriter is explicitly not on AI's list.** Relying on it fully produces writing missing context or lacking authenticity. Use it to spend **less time on how to say something and more on what you want to say**.
- **AI's four jobs:** **brainstorming** (how to position a service, how to share bad news); **rapid drafting and variation** via **Canvas** (emails, one-pagers, talking points, tailored per audience); **refinement and polish** (rephrase, shift formal to casual, feedback as you iterate); **practice and simulation** with **Gemini Live**.
- **Beyond the recording - Canvas** is a side-by-side workspace where the document persists and you can edit it directly, then ask for changes to *your* version rather than regenerating from scratch. Built for comparing variants.
- **Beyond the recording - the audience prompt:** state the facts once, then ask for all three versions in one request with *"keep the facts identical across all three"*. Separately generated messages drift apart and someone will compare them.
- **Beyond the recording - bad news structure:** the news in sentence one, a brief why, what you are doing about it with a date and a name, **what it means for them specifically**, then the channel for questions. AI drafts it well and reads it even better (*"read this as the client who was promised October"*), but whether to send it at all is judgment.
- **Beyond the recording - tone:** name the **edit**, not the feeling. *Delete every hedge* beats *more confident*; *cut to 40 percent, keep every fact* beats *more concise*.
- **Beyond the recording - voice:** paste three things you actually wrote and ask it to match them. Then remove the tells: three-part lists everywhere, *it's not X it's Y*, openings that restate the question, uniform paragraph length, hedged conclusions, and summaries of short messages.

- **Gemini Live roleplay** needs the mobile app, and is conversation rather than prompting. Declare the exercise, give the scenario and hand over the role, run it, then ask for **strengths, weaknesses and suggestions**.

Set a sceptical disposition, give it a hidden objective, ask it to interrupt, run the scene twice changing only your opening, and ask *"where did I lose you, which sentence hurt most, what did I fail to ask?"* Best used on the conversation you are dreading.
