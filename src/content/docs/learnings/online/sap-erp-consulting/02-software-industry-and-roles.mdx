Two things in one chapter: the *mindset* I need to work in tech, and the *map* of who does what. The map matters because an SAP consultant sits at its crossroads - I end up talking to almost everyone on it.

## The mindset for working in tech

I'm not just learning a tool, I'm stepping into a whole industry. That reframe helps: one hard afternoon is one room in a very large building, not the whole building.

<Aside type="tip" title="Three habits that actually matter">
- **Stay curious, expect to get it wrong.** Things breaking, cryptic messages, "worked yesterday, not today" - that's the normal texture of the work, not a verdict on me.
- **Comfort with not-knowing.** Nobody holds the field in their head.
- **Everyone looks things up.** Professionals search all day. The real skill is *finding* the answer fast, not *memorising* it. That takes the pressure off.
</Aside>

### Learning it the self-led way

This is a blended course: a few live sessions plus a lot of self-paced hours. The live time is where I get unstuck and get feedback - but most of the progress happens alone, so a routine is what makes it real.

<Aside type="note" title="What keeps me going">
Motivation is a feeling - real, useful, unreliable. The pair that replaces it:
- **Discipline** = showing up to the study block because it's on the calendar, not because I feel like it.
- **Dedication** = the specific reason I keep the discipline (the *why*).

They prop each other up. And **study actively** - build it, explain it out loud, recall from memory before checking. Passive re-reading feels like learning but isn't; the effort *is* the learning.
</Aside>

## Getting unstuck - the #1 beginner skill

The single most valuable early skill isn't knowing answers, it's knowing what to do the moment something won't work. It's a named, reusable routine - same four moves, in order, every time.

<Steps>

1. **Restart it.** Turn it off and on, or close and reopen. Clears a confused state and fixes a genuinely large share of everyday glitches - for free. Try this first.

2. **Read the message.** Don't click the error away in a panic. I don't need every word - I hunt for the plain-language clue: a file name, `not found`, `no internet connection`, `permission denied`. Partial understanding usually points straight at the cause.

3. **Search well.** Turn the problem into a *specific* search: the **program's name + the exact words of the message**. If the first try flops, change the words and search again.

4. **Ask a person.** When restart / read / search aren't enough, asking is the smart move, not a failure - as long as I give enough to work with (template below).

</Steps>

<Aside type="tip" title="Ask a question that gets answered fast">
Fill these in *before* sending. Four lines and a helper can usually spot it immediately:

1. **What I was trying to do**
2. **What I expected to happen**
3. **What actually happened** (+ the exact error, quoted word-for-word)
4. **What I've already tried**

Same shape scales into the job: a good bug report to my team is exactly these four lines. Post in a shared channel over a private DM - someone else with the same question learns too.
</Aside>

<Aside type="caution" title="Traps to un-learn">
"Restarting is a silly non-solution" - no, it genuinely works. · "Asking = I'm behind" - no, the people who ask early stay on track. · "A vague question is fine, they'll know what I mean" - a helper can't read my screen or mind; specific gets a useful answer far faster.
</Aside>

<Aside type="note">
Keep three words apart: a **field** is a broad area (security, design); a **role** is a job inside it (security analyst, UX designer); a **title** is whatever a company prints on the badge - and titles vary wildly. Learn the *role behind the title*.
</Aside>

## The map of tech roles

Grouped into families, one line each. The goal is recognition, not memorising every box - roles blur and people wear several hats in real life.

### Building - software, data, AI

| Role | What they do |
|---|---|
| **Front-end developer** | Builds the parts you see and touch - screens, buttons (the "dining room") |
| **Back-end developer** | Builds the hidden machinery - logic, storing data, making things happen (the "kitchen") |
| **Full-stack developer** | Works across both front and back |
| **Software engineer** | ≈ developer, with stress on discipline & maintainable code, not just code that works today |
| **Data analyst / engineer / scientist** | Analyst answers questions from data · engineer builds the pipelines · scientist finds patterns & predictions |
| **AI / ML** | Builds systems that learn patterns from data rather than following fixed rules - leans heavily on data roles |

<Aside type="note">
Under almost all of it sit **databases** - the organised stores where information lives. Nearly every system depends on one. This matters most in the enterprise/SAP world below.
</Aside>

### Design & experience - no coding required

| Role | What they do |
|---|---|
| **UX (User Experience)** | How the product *works* - is it clear, findable, does the flow make sense? Good UX is invisible; you only notice bad UX |
| **UI (User Interface)** | How it *looks* - colours, spacing, buttons, fonts, visual polish |
| **Product designer** | Where UX + UI meet: something both usable and attractive, then handed to developers to build |

### Run & keep safe - ops, security, infrastructure

| Role | What they do |
|---|---|
| **Sysadmin** | Looks after servers & systems - configured, updated, working |
| **Networking** | Keeps everything connected - data flow between machines, offices, the internet |
| **Cloud / DevOps / platform** | Runs systems on rented computing; builds pipelines to release software reliably |
| **IT support / help desk** | Front line when a laptop won't cooperate |
| **Cybersecurity** | Protects systems & data - **defensive** (build protections, watch), **offensive** (break in *with permission* to find holes first), **governance** (rules & compliance). Touches every other field |

<Aside type="tip">
Ops & security are judged by things *not* happening. Building = power, lifts, plumbing. Security = locks, guards, alarms. You only notice either when one fails.
</Aside>

### Business, product & quality - where career-changers often fit

| Role | What they do |
|---|---|
| **Product manager (PM)** | Decides *what* to build and *why*, and in what order - from user needs + business goals |
| **Business analyst (BA)** | Translates business problems into requirements the tech team can act on - big in the enterprise world |
| **Project / program manager** | Keeps the work organised, coordinated, on schedule |
| **Tech marketing** | Brings the product to its audience - digital marketing, SEO, growth, content |
| **QA / testing** | Catches problems before users do; deliberately tries to break things |
| **Technical writer** | Makes complex things understandable - guides, manuals, help pages |

<Aside type="caution" title="Two pairs I keep confusing">
**PM vs project manager:** product manager decides *what* & *why*; project manager keeps the building *organised & on time*. · **UX vs UI:** how it *works* vs how it *looks* - partners, not the same, and a product needs both.
</Aside>

## Where enterprise software & the SAP path fit

**Enterprise software** runs the operations of a large organisation (not one personal user) - its finance, staff records, supply chain, all at once and at scale. Large, stable, well-paid corner of the industry.

<Aside type="note" title="ERP in plain words">
**ERP = Enterprise Resource Planning.** One big connected system a whole company runs on - finance, logistics, HR, and procurement (buying) sharing the *same* information instead of each keeping separate records. Like one shared board on the kitchen wall everyone reads from and writes to, instead of everyone keeping their own private list that never agrees.

Every ERP sits on a **database** - every record, transaction and update lives there. An ERP is only as good as the database beneath it, which is why data is handled so carefully in this world.
</Aside>

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="SAP">
One of the world's leading makers of enterprise software; its systems run a large share of major companies. Learning SAP = learning the systems a great deal of the corporate world depends on - which is why the skill is in steady demand.
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="ABAP">
SAP's own programming language, used to build and customise behaviour *inside* SAP systems. Where general programming might be taught with JavaScript, the SAP world has ABAP for its own systems.
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Implementation consultant">
The role this pathway leads toward - sits right at the crossroads, bridging *what the business needs* and *what the system can do*.
  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

The key point: an SAP role is **not** an isolated corner. Implementing an ERP touches nearly the whole map at once.

<Aside type="tip">
So the whole role-map is really a map of my *future colleagues*. And a handy instinct falls out of it: I can often guess who's responsible without being told. App confusing → design (UX). Leaks private data → security. Crashes under load → infrastructure. Feature does the wrong thing → the developer who built it or the tester who missed it.
</Aside>
