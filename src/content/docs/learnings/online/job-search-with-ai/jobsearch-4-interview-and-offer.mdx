The three earlier modules built the raw material: what you have to offer, a resume and profile that say it clearly, and a way of applying and networking that gets those documents in front of people. An interview invitation is where all of that stops being paper and becomes a conversation.

The course is blunt about what preparation actually means. It is **not memorising answers**. It is anticipating what is likely to be asked, understanding the company and the role well enough to guess what this particular interviewer needs to hear, and then rehearsing until your own stories come out conversationally. Preparation is framed as a strategic advantage, because the effort is visible in the room and most candidates do not put it in.

The module also flags where its advice comes from: consultations with many employers and external partners, not only Google's own hiring practice, so it is meant to read as broader industry guidance. Two working recruiters appear on camera to say what they personally listen for.

## The three areas to work on

Everything in the module hangs off three words, and they map to before, before again, and during.

<div class="sc-chips">

  <span class="sc-chip violet">Practice - say the answers out loud, more than once</span>
  <span class="sc-chip green">Communication - be natural, be curious, make it a conversation</span>

### Preparation

Research the company, its position in its industry, the specific job you applied for, and the interviewer if you know who it will be. The course's suggested route is **Gemini Deep Research**: tell it you have an interview coming up for a named position at a named company, ask for a report on the company and its priorities, then keep asking follow-up questions off the back of it.

Once the research is done, the real work is **mapping your skills onto the company's needs**. The course contrasts two answers to the question of why you would be a good fit:

Alongside the tailored material, have the reliable classics ready. The module names *what are your biggest strengths and weaknesses* and *tell me about a time you overcame a challenge* as questions that travel across almost every job. Preparing examples and anecdotes in advance is what lets you be at ease and articulate on the day.

### Practice

Say the possible responses **aloud**, with a friend or on your own. Identify the stories you want to tell, then tell them repeatedly until they sound conversational rather than recited.

### Communication

Speak naturally and authentically, and let your personality show, because that openness is what makes a candidate memorable and connects you to the interviewer. When the moment is right, ask thoughtful questions of your own so the exchange flows both ways. An interview is also your chance to learn about them.

The course gives a worked example of turning their answer into your evidence. Ask how they measure success in the role. If the answer is total sales and customer satisfaction, do not simply say *cool*. Pick it up: in volunteer work you found the two were interconnected, and you sold out the annual fundraiser three years running, partly because attendees returned, with over 70 percent of the tickets you sold going to repeat attendees.

<Aside type="tip">
**Take the interview even if you are unsure about the fit.** There is no substitute for live practice. If it goes well, note what worked so you can repeat it. If it does not, write down what to improve next time.
</Aside>

## The four interview formats

Knowing what to expect removes a lot of pressure, and the experience varies widely by role. The course separates three formats defined by the **type** of question from one defined by its **format**, and warns that interviewers often combine them: behavioural questions followed by technical ones in the same conversation is normal.

<div class="sc-quad">
<div class="q amber">
<div class="qh">Behavioural</div>
<p>Used across nearly all industries and levels, entry to executive. Tests teamwork, problem-solving, communication and adaptability by asking what you did before.</p>
</div>
<div class="q violet">
<div class="qh">Technical</div>
<p>Assesses technical competency. Common in computer engineering, computer science and IT. Expect specific questions and possibly a challenge to complete.</p>
</div>
<div class="q green">
<div class="qh">Case study</div>
<p>A real-world business problem to analyse and solve. Common in consulting, product management and finance, where analysis and communication are the job.</p>
</div>
<div class="q slate">
<div class="qh">Panel</div>
<p>A format, not a question type. Several interviewers at once, and the questions inside it can be behavioural, technical or a mix.</p>
</div>
</div>

### Behavioural interviews

A customer service interviewer might ask about a time you handled a difficult customer, and your answer shows how you stayed calm and reached a resolution. Three preparation moves:

<ul>
<li><strong>Prepare and tailor your stories.</strong> Brainstorm past experiences from work, academic projects or extracurricular activities that demonstrate the skills the role needs, then read the job description carefully and pick the examples that align with its responsibilities and likely competencies.</li>
<li><strong>Structure clearly and emphasise your role.</strong> Use STAR to frame the experience, and use I statements throughout so your individual actions, contributions and impact are unmistakable.</li>
<li><strong>Be authentic and resourceful.</strong> Never invent an example; honesty matters and interviewers value authenticity. With no perfect example, offer a genuinely similar experience showing transferable skills, or explain thoughtfully how you would approach the situation based on what you do know.</li>
</ul>

### Technical interviews

The focus shifts off your background and onto your competency. You may be asked to complete a coding or design challenge, live or as a take-home, and then to explain how you solved it.

<ul>
<li><strong>Solidify the fundamentals and anticipate the formats.</strong> Review the core concepts the role rests on, and ask your recruiter whether anything specific will be tested, such as SQL, coding or spreadsheet proficiency. Research the business practices and technology the company actually uses. Be ready for problem solving, debugging existing code, and what-if hypotheticals.</li>
<li><strong>Showcase practical experience.</strong> Be ready to talk about the development tools, programming languages and source control systems relevant to the role, with clear examples of applying that knowledge to reach a specific outcome.</li>
<li><strong>Communicate adaptably.</strong> Explain complex concepts and your problem-solving process concisely, and adjust so that technical and non-technical interviewers alike can follow you.</li>
</ul>

### Case study interviews

You are handed a hypothetical business situation with facts and context, and asked to analyse it and propose solutions. The key insight is that **your analytical process usually matters more than your answer**. Interviewers are watching you gather and synthesise information, spot key details, think critically under pressure and cope with unfamiliar ground.

<figure class="sc-fig">
  <div class="sc-flow col">

    <div class="sc-node green">Summarise and recommend<span class="sub">close with a concise summary of the core issues, then one well-reasoned, actionable recommendation supported by your analysis</span></div>

### Panel interviews

The panel might include a hiring committee, potential supervisors and colleagues, HR representatives and other stakeholders. It is often used for final rounds, leadership positions, academic roles, government jobs, and any position needing heavy cross-functional collaboration. Some attendees ask questions, others observe. The organisation's aim is to pool multiple perspectives and raise the odds of hiring the right person.

<ul>
<li><strong>Prepare thoroughly.</strong> If you are given panellist names, research their roles and expertise. Have strong answers ready for the classics, including how a colleague would describe you, why you want to work here, and why they should hire you, since different members will probe different aspects of your qualifications.</li>
<li><strong>Engage professionally.</strong> Note names and job titles as people introduce themselves so you can address them properly. Make initial eye contact with whoever asked the question, then widen it to the rest of the group. Treat every question with equal thought and respect, whoever asks it and whatever their seniority.</li>
<li><strong>Observe the dynamics.</strong> How the panellists interact with each other tells you about the team's communication style and the company culture, which helps you judge the fit from your side.</li>
</ul>

<Aside type="note">
**Universal practices, whatever the format:** dress professionally for that company's culture and the role, be punctual, speak clearly and concisely, and follow up with a thank-you email. If you can find out the format in advance, tailor your preparation to it.
</Aside>

## Behavioural questions and the STAR method

Behavioural questions exist to understand how you responded in past situations, on the assumption that this predicts future performance. STAR is the course's go-to structure for answering all of them: a storytelling framework that makes an experience clear, concise and compelling, so the interviewer understands exactly how you handled the situation.

The worked example answers the question *can you tell me about a time you failed and what you learned from it*.

| Letter | What it does | The worked example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **S - Situation** | Set the scene with the background and context. Vivid detail where possible, but do not go overboard; only what is needed to make the rest land | A kitchen appliance company took a big retailer order, and a shipment of an essential part from the usual supplier was unexpectedly delayed, creating an urgent supply chain problem at the warehouse |
| **T - Task** | Your role and responsibility in it. What were you trying to achieve | I ran ordering and procurement, so the potential shortage was mine to solve, and I needed to secure new units of the part in time for the customer deadline |
| **A - Action** | The specific steps you took, in I statements, focused on your individual contribution. Described as the most crucial part | I researched new suppliers that met our quality standards and found one who could deliver the part immediately |
| **R - Result** | The clear, measurable outcome, quantified with numbers or data where possible, plus what you learned | The part arrived the next day, negotiated at 10 percent below the original supplier's price, and I set up a backup supplier system for every part. The lesson: look for risk even where there has never been a problem, and always have a fallback |

### A hiring manager's example of STAR done well

The module includes a second example from the other side of the table. Asked about a difficult stakeholder, a candidate immediately framed the **situation**, a product launch where the head of sales disagreed with their marketing plan, then the **task** of finding a compromise. The **actions** were what impressed: using data, market research and customer personas both to support their own strategy and to understand the sales team's goals, which turned into productive discussion and a blended approach. The **result** was stated plainly, a successful launch that met everyone's objectives. The structure itself demonstrated problem solving and communication.

### Why STAR is worth the effort

<ul>
<li>It highlights strengths and successes even on questions you already feel confident about.</li>
<li>It keeps answers short and on point, which the course calls a lifesaver when nerves make you ramble.</li>
<li>It fits the recurring question shapes: how you exceeded expectations, how you improved the way a task was normally done, kudos you received from a customer you helped, or an innovative solution you came up with.</li>
</ul>

<Aside type="tip">
**Build a reservoir.** The course recommends preparing at least **five stories or projects** you can tell in STAR form, writing them down, and adding them to your Job Search Portfolio so you always have interview-ready anecdotes. Even experienced interviewers admit to nerves; the framework is what lets you present your best self anyway.
</Aside>

## Questions to ask your interviewer

An interview should not be one-sided. Asking thoughtful, insightful questions gathers information you genuinely need and demonstrates real interest in the company, and it is described as an expected practice that shifts the interview into a more natural, conversational exchange for both sides.

**Prompt shape for the research:** I have an interview coming up for [role] at [company name]. Research the company's mission, values, history, number of employees, products and services, and current priorities. What are some compelling questions I could ask in the interview to showcase my knowledge about and interest in the company beyond what is presented on their website.

**Prompt shape for brainstorming questions afterwards:** I am interviewing for [role] with [company]. What are some questions I might ask my interviewer to learn more about the role, team and company. Include questions to demonstrate that I have researched the company.

Treat the output as a starting point and customise it. The course repeats its running habit of saving prompts you like into your **prompt library** for reuse.

### Five categories of question

| Category | What to probe | Examples given |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Role** | Where the job sits in the company, performance expectations, key deliverables | How does this role contribute to the company's broader goals. If I were in this role for six months, what would I need to accomplish for you to feel you made an excellent hiring decision |
| **Team dynamics** | How the team collaborates and communicates | How does the team view and approach collaboration. How is feedback typically shared and received within the team |
| **Professional development and work environment** | Growth opportunities, work-life balance, how advancement is supported | What opportunities are there for professional development and learning. Beyond formal training, how does the company culture support employee growth and well-being |
| **Company and project challenges** | Past and current difficulties, which shows strategic thinking | Can you share an example of a time the company had to make a difficult decision. When has this team had to shift its projects due to industry changes or challenges |
| **Future plans** | The company's direction, which shows a forward-thinking perspective | What are the company's key priorities and goals for the next one to three years. How does the company invest in innovation and future development |

<Aside type="note">
**Listen actively while you ask.** Maintain good eye contact, ask follow-up questions where they fit, and make sure your questions genuinely align with your own professional goals. An interview is about finding the right match, and that has to work in both directions.
</Aside>

## A recruiter on difficult questions

The first recruiter perspective comes from a recruiting manager at Accenture who builds interview training for everyone involved in hiring there and has run thousands of interviews since the late 1990s. Her core point: any interview can contain difficult questions, and all of them can be prepared for in advance.

| The question type | What the interviewer is actually after | How to handle it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Situational, about things you have done** | Evidence of what you specifically did. They will probe for more and more detail, asking what you were thinking and what you did next | Think in advance of recent situations where you played a substantial role, and practise talking about them knowing detail will be requested layer by layer |
| **Multiple correct answers, or none** | Your thought process and how you evaluate the options in that scenario | Narrate the evaluation rather than hunting for the single right answer |
| **A past failure or mistake** | Everyone has made mistakes and everyone learns from them, so the interest is in the learning | Focus on what you would do differently with hindsight, and have an honest conversation about it |
| **Something you genuinely do not know** | Whether you are straight with them | Say so. Talk about what you have learned that is similar, or say this is something you have been excited to learn, have not had the chance to do yet, and hope to pick up in this role |

<Aside type="caution">
**The biggest mistake is making it up.** It takes no more than a probe or two for an interviewer to see that a candidate has never used the technology or done the thing being asked about. Admitting the gap while showing curiosity signals that you have thought about it, that it is not foreign to you, and that you are willing to learn.
</Aside>

And the upside, in her words: what an interviewer loves is when the exchange turns into a genuinely good conversation. If you are passionate about what you do, it shows.

## Practising out loud with Gemini Live

Landing an interview is hard enough that wasting the opportunity is the real risk, and the module is direct that nailing one is not only about qualifications, but about how you show up as a person and communicate in the moment.

Practising with a friend or family member gives you human connection and different perspectives. **Gemini Live** is offered as the complement to that, not the replacement: it works any time, needs no schedule coordination, and gives expert-style feedback on your responses through a natural real-time spoken conversation.

<Steps>

1. **Get the app.** Gemini Live runs from the Gemini app on your phone. Open it and tap the live icon.

2. **Describe the practice you want, with context.** The demo opens with the situation: an entry-level customer service job with a financial services provider, a first interview with the company, a request for interview questions plus feedback on the answers, and a statement that the answers will use the STAR method.

3. **Answer out loud, as you would in the room.** The demo answer covers an upset customer whose order was in fact delivered two days earlier, with the email notification having failed.

4. **Take the feedback and go again.** Gemini Live replies that it is a good start but asks for more of the situation: the specific reason for the delay and the steps taken to resolve it. Because it was asked to, it is coaching against STAR and steering towards a fuller answer.

</Steps>

<Aside type="tip">
**Context is the lever.** The more detail you give about the job you are interviewing for, the more the questions resemble what a real interviewer will ask. Practising aloud is what makes you comfortable in the real situation, so use all three: ask a neighbour, call a mentor, and use Gemini Live as often as you need.
</Aside>

## What a recruiter listens for on the first call

The second recruiter perspective comes from a senior marketing recruiter at Google who speaks with candidates all week. On a first conversation she is listening for **great energy, preparation and conversation flow**, and for the candidate to connect the dots between the responsibilities of the role and what they have done before, compacted into a good story: what you have done, what your journey has been, why this fits, and why you would fit the organisation.

A phone screen is often **30 minutes or less** and has to cover role specifics, your background, intentional questions about the role, and an education on the hiring process and timeline. That is why **brevity** matters so much to the person on the other end.

### Who to write to, and how

<ul>
<li>Send notes to the hiring manager, the recruiter and anyone else you met with. These can often go through your main point of contact, so you may not need to ask for extra email addresses.</li>
<li>Thank anyone who helped you land or prepare for the interview too.</li>
<li>If writing is not your strength, use Gemini for a first draft: tell it about the role you applied for, who interviewed you and what you discussed, and include relevant links to portfolios or other materials or projects you mentioned, while keeping it brief.</li>
<li><strong>Always edit the output.</strong> A thank-you note has to feel personal.</li>
<li>Follow the company's social media channels to stay connected and show genuine interest. Even if this job does not come through, staying current costs nothing and may matter later.</li>
</ul>

### The polite nudge

If the interviewer never said when you would hear back, send a brief, polite follow-up email **after a week or two**. Include the role you interviewed for and the date of the interview to jog their memory, and a line along the lines of: our conversation during my interview made me even more excited about the role, and my interest in this opportunity remains strong. Gemini can help draft this too if you are unsure what to write.

## Responding to a job offer

The first move is the same whatever you decide: respond **professionally, enthusiastically, promptly and with gratitude**. Taking a short pause to consider is entirely acceptable, but tell the recruiter or hiring manager the timeline you will respond within. That clarity builds trust and avoids any impression of indecision or disinterest.

### Evaluating what is on the table

<Steps>

1. **Review the offer letter thoroughly.** Salary, benefits such as health insurance, retirement plan and paid time off, work arrangements such as in-person, hybrid or remote, job title, responsibilities, start date, and any other perks or conditions. Check each against your expectations.

2. **Research the market value for the role.** This is what makes a negotiation informed rather than hopeful. Search the typical salary range for that job title in your area; check salary data on major job search sites such as Indeed and Glassdoor, filtering by title and location and factoring in your years of experience, education and the specific skills required; explore government resources such as the US Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook for pay and benefits by region, state or city; and look at ranges for similar roles at comparable companies nearby.

3. **Analyse the benefits and perks**, not only the base salary, since they add real value to the total compensation.

4. **Ask clarifying questions.** If anything is unclear, ask for detail, politely and professionally. Every interaction still makes an impression.

5. **Formulate your response** and formally accept or decline, again with professionalism and gratitude.

</Steps>

**Prompt shape for an acceptance email:** Draft a formal email accepting the [job title] position at [company name]. Express sincere gratitude for the offer, confirm acceptance of the [salary amount] salary and the agreed start date of [start date], express enthusiasm about joining the team and contributing to [something specific about the company or role], and include a sentence looking forward to the next steps in onboarding.

## When the answer is no

Not every interview produces an offer, and sometimes there is no reply at all. The course is explicit that this is not necessarily a reflection of your quality as a candidate: hiring processes can be long and complex, and are subject to delays from an expanding candidate pool, hiring freezes or other administrative shifts, while some companies have a policy of only contacting successful candidates.

<ul>
<li><strong>It is not personal.</strong> It is the employer deciding on the best fit for that role at that time, given the candidates who applied.</li>
<li><strong>Reply anyway, politely and professionally.</strong> This keeps the connection positive, and you can ask the recruiter or hiring manager to keep you in mind for future relevant roles. Gemini can help draft this delicate response.</li>
<li><strong>Ask to stay on file</strong> if you are particularly interested in the organisation, so they can come back to you when a suitable role opens.</li>
<li><strong>Focus on what you can control.</strong> Keep refining your search approach, since small adjustments can have a significant impact, and lean on your network for support and guidance if you are struggling to make headway.</li>
<li><strong>Reflect after every interview</strong> and adjust for the next one. Every interview is valuable even when the only outcome is experience and confidence.</li>
<li><strong>Celebrate small successes</strong> to keep confidence and motivation up across a long search.</li>
</ul>

## Revision summary

<Aside type="tip">
**One-minute recap**

- **Three areas decide the interview:** preparation (research the company, the role and the interviewer, then map your skills onto their current needs), practice (say your answers aloud, repeatedly), and communication (be natural, show personality, ask questions back).
- Preparation beats blandness: instead of saying you are passionate about the industry, name the company's current priority and attach a quantified result of your own to it.
- **Four formats.** Behavioural tests competencies through past situations and spans all industries and levels. Technical tests competency, often with a live or take-home challenge. Case study tests analysis, where the process matters more than the answer. Panel is a format, not a question type, common for final rounds, leadership, academic and government roles. Formats are frequently combined.
- **Universal rules:** dress for the company's culture, be punctual, speak clearly and concisely, send a thank-you email, and find out the format beforehand if you can.
- **STAR** = Situation, Task, Action, Result. Set the scene, state your responsibility, detail your specific steps in I statements (the most crucial part), then give a measurable result plus the lesson. It keeps answers tight when nerves push you to ramble. Prepare **at least five stories** and store them in your Job Search Portfolio.
- Never invent an example. If you lack a perfect one, offer a similar experience or explain how you would approach the situation.
- **Gemini Notebook** is an AI research and thinking partner that works only from the sources you upload, which keeps it grounded in your trusted material. Feed it the job description, your resume, company research, your question notes, industry reports and details of your own experience. It summarises, builds custom study aids such as FAQs, study guides and talking-point outlines, and produces audio overviews for review on the go.
- **Gemini Deep Research** is the research specialist mode inside Gemini, producing multi-page reports on the company's projects, the role and industry trends, and it can suggest questions to ask. It is weaker for very small or local businesses. Standard Gemini remains the tool for quick answers, drafting, summarising and brainstorming.
- **Ask questions in five areas:** the role, team dynamics, professional development and work environment, company and project challenges, and future plans. Listen actively, follow up, and check the match against your own goals.
- **A recruiter on hard questions:** situational questions are probed detail by detail, so prepare recent situations where you played a big role; for open questions they want your thought process; for failures they want what you would do differently. If you do not know, say so and show willingness to learn. Making it up is the biggest mistake and is caught within a probe or two.
- **Gemini Live** lets you rehearse aloud in real time on your phone. Give it the role, the company type and the round, ask for questions and feedback, and name the STAR method so it coaches you against it. More context in means more realistic questions out.
- **A recruiter on first calls:** she listens for energy, preparation and conversation flow, with the dots connected between your past and this role. A screen is often 30 minutes or less, so be brief. Answer tell-me-about-you in **three minutes or less**, built from two to three relevant experiences pulled from the job description's responsibilities and framed by your career identity statement. Not an autobiography. The thing to avoid is arriving unprepared.
- **Follow up.** Around **95 percent** of candidates do not, while **68 percent** of hiring managers say a thank-you note makes a positive impact. Email the hiring manager, recruiter and anyone else you met, thank whoever helped you prepare, draft with Gemini but always edit for a personal tone, follow the company's channels, and send a polite nudge after a week or two if no timeline was given.
- **On an offer:** respond promptly and gratefully, state your decision timeline, then review the whole letter (salary, benefits, work arrangement, title, responsibilities, start date, perks), research market value via Indeed, Glassdoor, the Occupational Outlook Handbook and comparable local employers, weigh the benefits alongside base pay, ask clarifying questions, then accept or decline professionally. Gemini can draft the acceptance email.
- **On a no:** it is not personal, silence is often process rather than judgement, reply graciously anyway, ask to be kept on file, keep refining the search, lean on your network, and treat every interview as experience and confidence gained.
