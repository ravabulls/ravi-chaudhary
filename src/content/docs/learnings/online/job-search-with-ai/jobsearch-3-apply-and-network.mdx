With the application pack finished - skills inventory, career identity statement, resume, cover letter and online profile - the question becomes where to send it and who to talk to. This module opens with a statistic that sets the tone: **over half of job seekers say they spend more time looking for a job than looking for love.** The problem is rarely a shortage of listings, it is the sheer volume of them spread across dozens of platforms.

So the module is about focus and follow-through. Focus means directing energy at the platforms and companies most likely to produce results, rather than scrolling every board that exists. Follow-through means two habits that most people skip: keeping an organised record of every application and conversation, and building relationships before you need them.

The second half of the module makes an argument that is easy to nod at and hard to act on. Networking is not a performance at a crowded event, and a large share of jobs are never advertised at all. Both facts point the same way: towards steady, genuine, mutually useful professional relationships.

<Aside type="note">
**Where this sits:** modules 1 and 2 built the raw material - what you can do and how you describe it. This module spends that material. The **application phase is often the longest part of a job search**, so the skills here are less about polish and more about stamina, organisation and reach.
</Aside>

### Four sources of postings

| Source | What it is | Examples named |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **General job boards** | The common starting point for most job seekers, broad across industries | CareerBuilder, Indeed, Glassdoor |
| **Employer consortium postings** | For Google Career Certificate graduates, thousands of postings from consortium member employers specifically seeking people with those skills | The Google employer consortium |
| **Niche job platforms** | Boards dedicated to one industry or type of work | Behance for creative professionals, USAJOBS for government positions |
| **Professional associations** | Industry bodies that post roles and host in-person networking events, worth following on social media too | The National Health Career Association for healthcare |

A fifth source is the companies themselves. Firms often post openings **on their own websites**, so a handful of companies that already interest you can be treated as a personal niche job board.

<Aside type="tip">
**Build a mix, then work it regularly.** As you uncover sources, note them down and consult them on a routine, combining general boards, specialised platforms and professional associations. Coverage plus repetition beats one heroic afternoon of searching.
</Aside>

### Investigating the hiring company

Once a few promising leads appear, research the companies behind them rather than firing off applications blind.

<ul>
<li>Visit the <strong>official company website and social channels</strong> to understand their work and collect potential talking points for interviews.</li>
<li>Read <strong>employee reviews</strong> on sites such as Glassdoor, and see what employees say about the company on social media.</li>
<li>Combining both gives a holistic picture of the organisation and its environment, not just its marketing.</li>
</ul>

## Staying organised through the application phase

The application phase can be the longest stretch of a search, and it usually means submitting a large number of resumes. Struggling to remember the state of even a handful of applications is normal. The fix is a system, and the module's system is a spreadsheet.

A tracker answers the questions that otherwise get lost: **where and when you applied, who you communicated with, what the outcome was, and which platform you used.**

### What belongs in a job search tracker

<div class="sc-quad">
<div class="q amber">
<div class="qh">The basics</div>
<p>Job title, company, the link to the job posting, and the date you applied.</p>
</div>
<div class="q violet">
<div class="qh">Your versions</div>
<p>Which resume and which cover letter you used for that specific application, so tailoring stays traceable.</p>
</div>
<div class="q green">
<div class="qh">Company information</div>
<p>Company name, website and industry. The website saves time when you need to revisit an About Us page, and the industry column reveals patterns you can use to refine focus.</p>
</div>
<div class="q slate">
<div class="qh">Progress</div>
<p>Dates of calls and interviews, plus any offers or rejections, so nothing slips.</p>
</div>
</div>

### The course tracker template, column by column

Building all of this from scratch feels overwhelming, so the course supplies a template to copy and customise. It is a blank sheet with eleven headings, and it is a starting point rather than a finished tool - tailor it the same way you tailor a resume.

| Column | What it is for |
| --- | --- |
| **Application Status** | Where this application currently stands, so one glance shows what is live, closed or waiting |
| **Company Name** | The employer, the key you sort and group the sheet by |
| **Job Title** | The advertised title of the role |
| **Job Description** | A short summary of the posting, two or three sentences, so you can tell similar roles apart |
| **Job Link (URL)** | The link back to the full posting, kept alongside the summary |
| **Date Applied** | When you submitted, which is what tells you when a follow-up is due |
| **Contact Person(s)** | Recruiters, hiring managers or connections tied to this application |
| **Contact Info** | How to reach those people without hunting for the details again |
| **Interview Dates** | Scheduled and completed conversations, the progress record for this row |
| **Company Research** | Your condensed findings on the company, ready to recall before an interview |
| **Notes** | Anything else: referral source, salary information, impressions, next steps |

<Aside type="note">
**Why the summary column earns its place.** A full description lives in the posting, but a short summary identifies each opportunity at a glance and differentiates roles that look identical on paper. If ten applications all carry the title IT support technician, only the summary tells you which is which. Keep **both** the link and the summary.
</Aside>

**Gemini Deep Research** behaves like a research assistant: it analyses a range of sources to pinpoint a company's trends and business drivers, builds a research plan, and produces a detailed report you can save and link from the tracker. An **audio summary** of that report is also available.

<Aside type="caution">
**Its limit:** Deep Research is likely to be **less effective for very small or local businesses** whose online footprint is thin. There simply may not be enough published material to analyse.
</Aside>

Understanding a company matters at two moments: when deciding whether to apply at all, and before an interview, where showing that you know a company's history and priorities demonstrates genuine interest in the role.

<Aside type="tip">
**The tracker is a living document.** Keep adding to it as the search moves, and store it in your **job search portfolio** so everything stays in one place.
</Aside>

## Resilience across a long search

The module includes a practitioner perspective from **A.J., who works for the NFL Player Care Foundation** supporting retired NFL players into careers after football - helping them work out which careers they want, which skills they need to get there, and how to build a resume that puts them on the market.

The challenge he sees most often is **underestimating how hard and how long finding a role can be.** Organisations run different policies, timelines and talent pools, so a search can stretch across many months. His conclusion: **resilience is one of the most important and most underrated skills** for attacking the job market.

### Being programmatic

The advice for keeping morale up is to make the search a discipline rather than a mood, with a concrete daily quota.

<div class="sc-chips">
  <span class="sc-chip amber">Apply to X jobs today</span>
  <span class="sc-chip violet">Reach out to a set number of individuals today</span>
  <span class="sc-chip green">Spend 30 minutes learning a new skill</span>
  <span class="sc-chip slate">Repeat tomorrow</span>
</div>

Motivation comes from **successes**, and the example given is the email three weeks after an application that offers an interview, which injects real momentum. But that email only arrives because the seeds were planted weeks or months earlier, which is the argument for keeping the daily quota running even when nothing seems to be happening.

### Handling rejection

<Aside type="note">
**Do not take it personally.** There is one job and probably **100 to 1,000 applicants**. The strategy is to **reframe negative experiences as learning opportunities** and treat them as part of the course, because they build the skills that eventually land the role. Much of the search is outside your control - the state of the market, a no, someone else getting the job, losing a job - so the discipline is refusing to internalise those losses, getting back up and attacking the next day.
</Aside>

## What networking actually is

The popular image of networking is barrelling into a crowded room, locating the biggest name present, and impressing that CEO, VP or president with a canned pitch. The module rejects this.

### Why it is worth the effort

<ul>
<li>Early in a career, networking surfaces <strong>job opportunities</strong> and introduces people who can support the search.</li>
<li>With experience behind you, contacts offer <strong>guidance on advancing</strong> a career or developing new skills.</li>
<li>Either way it puts you <strong>on the radar</strong> of people and companies hiring now or in the future.</li>
<li>For some roles, building professional relationships is a vital part of the search rather than an optional extra.</li>
</ul>

Doing more than showing up means planning and preparing. That holds for the naturally sociable, and it matters even more for anyone who finds networking uncomfortable, because preparation is what converts into confidence.

### Five elements of successful networking

<Steps>

1. **Identify your ideal audience.** Write down who you want to network with and why: people in roles you aspire to, people working at companies you would like to join, experts whose insight you value. For an internal move, that means colleagues or managers in the relevant departments. Add former managers, peers, old classmates and friends of friends - anyone you have a potential connection with is a lead. Also look for **connectors**, people who can open doors to further contacts, and use professional sites such as LinkedIn to find second and third connections.

2. **Commit yourself, concretely.** Come up with a few specific ways to start rather than a vague intention. Once you have identified someone, look for ways or events where you can connect. Further along, that becomes setting up phone conversations, coffee talks or video meetings with people in your ideal audience.

3. **Organise your connections.** Track current and potential contacts in your job search tracker so everything stays in one place. A spreadsheet gets messy fast, so sort contacts into groups.

4. **Build a networking strategy.** Decide *how* you will network, choosing among the three common modes below.

5. **Make your connections last.** A list of contacts is worth nothing if nobody is willing to help when you need it.

</Steps>

### Three ways to network

| Mode | How it works | How to open it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **One-on-one** | With someone in a company or industry you want to enter, or someone with expertise or connections valuable to you | Check for **mutual connections** who could introduce you. Failing that, send a brief introduction message that is meaningful and specific, respects their time, and states clearly why you want to connect |
| **Social networking** | No face-to-face meeting required, which extends your contact list well beyond the people you meet in person | Join groups such as LinkedIn groups and exchange ideas with people in your target industry |
| **Large group networking** | Conferences, talks, and in-person or virtual events where you meet several people at once | Research relevant events, and if going alone feels daunting, invite a friend, a coworker or an ally from your list |

Before a one-on-one meeting, prepare a few talking points around four questions: **why** you want to talk to this person, what **specific skills or experience** you can learn from them, what you intend to **do with that knowledge**, and what **value or benefit you can offer them** in return.

<Aside type="caution">
**Networking is a mutually beneficial relationship, not a withdrawal service.** Do not only contact people when you want or need something. Reach out occasionally for its own sake, for instance to share an article they would find interesting or useful.
</Aside>

As confidence grows, the presentation details still count: bring positive energy to each meeting, show enthusiasm, stay aware of how you present yourself, and **follow up with a thank you note**.

## The hidden job market

Some openings are **never publicly advertised at all.** Networking is what reaches them, through word of mouth, internal referrals and direct connections. On top of that, a strong referral from a trusted connection carries real weight with hiring managers: candidates who are directly referred can have a **higher likelihood of landing an interview and ultimately an offer**.

That reframes networking from a transactional exchange aimed at getting a job into an ongoing strategy for building relationships and developing a career.

| Benefit | What it gives you |
| --- | --- |
| **Authentic professional relationships** | Go beyond favours: genuine connections built on trust and mutual respect, through active listening, offering insight and support, and taking a real interest in other people's professional journeys. Relationships that are not based only on immediate need are far more likely to produce opportunities later |
| **Insider knowledge** | Job descriptions cover the basics and miss the nuances. A network tells you about company culture, team dynamics, hiring manager preferences and likely challenges, none of which is online, which lets you tailor your application and interview and judge whether the role is genuinely a fit |
| **Staying relevant and informed** | Conversations with people in your field are a real-time learning platform for emerging ideas, industry trends, shifting skill requirements and career paths you had not considered. Engaging regularly also keeps you top of mind when something opens up |
| **Support through the search** | Job searching is challenging and isolating. A network supplies encouragement, a sense of community, and practical help such as resume feedback or interview practice, which sustains motivation and resilience |
| **Sharper communication skills** | Networking events are low-stakes practice for communication, presentation and interpersonal skills in a relaxed setting, and that practice transfers directly into more confident interviews and better workplace interactions |
| **Long-term career growth** | Today's relationships evolve into mentorships, collaborations, partnerships and a continuing source of opportunity across an entire career |

<Aside type="tip">
**The practical next step from the reading:** find a networking event relevant to your field or interests and use it to practise and connect. The possibilities tend to reveal themselves quickly once you are in the room.
</Aside>
