Starting something new is genuinely exciting: a chance to learn, to grow, to take on different problems. The search that gets you there is far less romantic. Writing a resume, firing off applications, rehearsing interviews - it piles up quickly, and the numbers are sobering. The course opens with the figure that an average job search in the US runs to around **20 weeks** and can involve hundreds of rounds of applications.

This short course is an answer to that grind. It is a guide to using **artificial intelligence** as a working partner across the whole search, whether you are changing jobs or chasing your very first one. The format is demo first, practice second: you watch a tool being used, then use it yourself on your own material.

By the end, the promised output is concrete: your **transferable skills** identified, a **career identity statement** and a **job search plan** written, a refined resume tailored to different roles, a curated professional online profile, an application tracker, networking practice, a **20-second professional pitch**, interview preparation, and as a bonus a personal **library of AI prompts** to keep collaborating with AI after the course ends.

<Aside type="note">
**Where this sits in my notes:** this is a separate, much shorter Google course from the Project Management Certificate that lives elsewhere in these online-learning pages. It is not about running projects at all - it is about running a job search, with AI tools doing the heavy lifting on drafting, structuring and exploring. Module 1 is the foundation module: work out what you already have to offer before you start applying anywhere.
</Aside>

## Transferable skills: taking stock of what you already have

Think about what fills an ordinary life. Juggling a child's after-school schedule. Being the person friends call when their laptop misbehaves. Volunteering at an animal rescue. None of that appears on a job description, and all of it builds skills.

Tony's own example: time spent waiting tables sharpened his customer service instincts and taught him to anticipate what someone needed before they asked. It also made him a clear, succinct communicator who could pitch the same message differently to different audiences. Both of those still do real work in his job as a program manager and leader.

<Aside type="note">
**Transferable skills** are abilities learned in one situation that can be applied in a completely different one, including a new job. They travel with you because they belong to you, not to the role you learned them in.
</Aside>

### Why they are worth naming

Knowing your transferable skills changes four things at once: which jobs you decide to chase, how well you can explain the link between scattered life experiences and the role in front of you, how you shape your resume, and how convincingly you talk about yourself in an interview.

There is a market reason too. Many employers now use **skills-based hiring**, which means selecting candidates on the skills needed to succeed in the job rather than on strict education requirements or previous job titles. That is precisely the door transferable skills walk through.

Most people reach for the technical ones first, such as programming or data analysis. The course is clear that **adaptability, communication, problem-solving and teamwork** can matter to employers just as much.

### Why this is hard, and the three steps that fix it

Plenty of people struggle to name the qualities employers want, and the reason is unflattering to nobody: your own skills are second nature to you, so they are invisible from the inside. The fix is to go at it step by step rather than trying to produce a list from memory.

<Steps>

1. **Reflect on your life experiences.** Every aspect of your life, not only the parts you assume are job-related. Have you volunteered? Do you mentor someone? Do you coach a sport? Have you taken a leadership role in a group setting?

2. **Name the skills each experience built.** Interrogate each one. Did the volunteer role involve detail-oriented administrative duties? Did you have to brainstorm fundraising ideas? Were you in charge of running an in-person event? Write the skills down as they surface.

3. **Connect those skills to a potential job.** Work out which roles those skills point towards, which is the step that turns a list into a direction.

</Steps>

### Two worked examples of step three

| Starting point | Skills already in hand | Where they point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Executive assistant** | Managing complex schedules, coordinating meetings, communicating with stakeholders | Project management roles, which often demand exactly that strength in communication and planning |
| **Military service, sports, school or any setting with diverse personalities working to a shared goal** | Being a team player and a strong collaborator | Many roles across many industries, since collaboration is one of the most broadly required abilities there is |

The encouraging conclusion: you are already carrying a decent stock of valuable skills, so you may be further along in your preparation than you assumed.

## The four skills employers value across industries

Mike, a product lead for the Google School for Leaders, builds innovative learning experiences for managers and leaders. He used to be a teacher, and he makes the point bluntly: prioritisation, time management, navigating uncertainty, adapting to a changing situation, communicating with senior leaders and colleagues - all of it started in the classroom and all of it transferred.

His definition is the compact one: transferable skills are abilities learned in one setting that you can take anywhere. He names four that employers value highly across multiple industries.

<div class="sc-quad">
<div class="q amber">
<div class="qh">Navigating ambiguity</div>
<p>Things change constantly and you have to respond constantly. What an employer wants to know is whether you work through change effectively, without a negative effect on the people around you, ideally with a positive one.</p>
</div>
<div class="q violet">
<div class="qh">Emotional regulation</div>
<p>How you show up on the outside is tied to how you feel on the inside. Can you be there for the team and talk people through what is happening? Or recognise that you need a moment and then come back to it? Being able to describe that also demonstrates self-awareness.</p>
</div>
<div class="q green">
<div class="qh">Communication</div>
<p>It shows an employer that you can adapt to whoever you are talking to, adjusting how you say something while still getting it across clearly and concisely.</p>
</div>
<div class="q slate">
<div class="qh">Problem-solving</div>
<p>It sounds simple, and it is still worth saying. Describing how you respond when problems appear gives the employer a more holistic view of you, and a unique contribution is what lifts a team above what it could do collectively before you arrived.</p>
</div>
</div>

## The career identity statement: your north star

If you were lost in the woods, you would navigate out by the north star: knowing where it sits clarifies where you are and gives you a direction to walk in. A **career identity statement** does the same job for a job search. It keeps you pointed at something instead of applying scattershot.

### What it is

It is a few sentences about yourself that go further than a list of qualifications. It gives recruiters, hiring managers and other professionals a quick but insightful picture of what you bring, covering four ingredients.

  <div class="sc-flow">
    <div class="sc-node amber">Key skills<span class="sub">what you can actually do</span></div>
    <span class="arrow">→</span>
    <div class="sc-node violet">Genuine interests<span class="sub">what holds your attention</span></div>
    <span class="arrow">→</span>
    <div class="sc-node green">Career goals<span class="sub">where you are heading</span></div>
    <span class="arrow">→</span>
    <div class="sc-node slate">Passions<span class="sub">what motivates you</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Four ingredients, a few sentences, one direction. Combining them is what lets you present an authentic self to the market rather than a generic one.</figcaption>
</figure>

### The worked example

The course example reads roughly like this: a background in finance and customer service has produced strong analytical skills and a client-focused approach; the speaker excels at using data to solve problems, is committed to clear communication and positive interactions, values teamwork highly, and finds that a passion for basketball keeps reinforcing how much collaboration and communication matter in reaching shared goals.

Why the course says it works:

<ul>
<li>It lays out a valuable and fairly unusual <strong>mix</strong> of skills and experiences rather than one narrow specialism.</li>
<li>Calling out both the analytical side from finance and the focus on customer service immediately signals someone well-rounded.</li>
<li>Naming the specific backgrounds, finance and service, adds weight and paints a clearer picture of what the person can actually do.</li>
<li>Mentioning the passion adds a personal touch and shows what genuinely drives them professionally.</li>
</ul>

### Four things to get right

| Do | Avoid |
| --- | --- |
| Use **specific** skills and examples | Vague phrases and generalities |
| Keep it **focused** on the most important aspects of your professional self | Trying to be everything to everyone |
| Keep the tone **positive and genuine** | Anything that reads as arrogant or negative |
| Aim for **clarity and conciseness**, since a short impactful statement works best | Padding it out into a paragraph nobody finishes |

### Where it gets used

<div class="sc-chips">
  <span class="sc-chip amber">Networking and introducing yourself</span>
  <span class="sc-chip violet">Inside a cover letter or a job inquiry email</span>
  <span class="sc-chip green">As the professional bio on an online profile</span>
</div>

In a few sentences it highlights what makes you distinctive while narrowing your search to jobs that match your goals and values. The image the course leaves you with: the search should start looking less like an insurmountable mountain and more like a gently rolling hill.

## Career Dreamer

**Career Dreamer** is an experimental Google tool that uses AI to make career exploration easier and more personal, working from your own background and experiences rather than from a generic questionnaire. It is reached at grow.google/careerdreamer.

It does two things in sequence. First it pinpoints the tasks and skills you have likely developed in roles you have held, which is to say your transferable skills, and it grounds those on **real job data from trusted sources such as Lightcast** so that they reflect actual job requirements rather than guesswork. Then it uses those skills to draft a **career identity statement** for you, which narrows your search towards jobs aligned with your goals.

### The walkthrough

<Steps>

1. **Enter a current or previous role.** The demo starts with accounting associate, the presenter's first job out of college. An optional industry field follows, in this case finance, and it can be skipped.

2. **Pick your real tasks.** The tool populates a list of tasks someone in that role likely performs. Select the ones you actually did.

3. **Turn tasks into skills.** Career Dreamer draws on real job market data to break those tasks into transferable skills. Hovering over any skill explains why it was included. The demo selects Problem Solving, Financial Analysis, Data Entry and Attention To Detail.

4. **Review the building blocks of your career identity.** The left side holds four categories, **Experiences, Education, Skills and Interests**, with the chosen skills already filled in. The right side already holds a first draft of the career identity statement.

5. **Add education.** More detail produces a fuller view, whether that is an associate's degree, a bachelor's degree or a professional certificate. The demo adds high school, and the tool then asks for a favourite subject, physics, to pin down the skills gained there: Scientific Method, Technical Communication and Teamwork.

6. **Add more roles and your interests.** A bartending job under the service industry surfaces another set of activities and skills. Interests go in too, in this case basketball, along with a few reasons why the sport appeals. As the skill list grows, the statement rewrites itself to match.

7. **Regenerate until it sounds like you.** Clicking Re-generate rephrases the statement, and version history brings back earlier drafts. Copy the version you like into Google Docs to keep editing it, and save it into your job search portfolio.

</Steps>

<Aside type="caution">
The tool drafts, you decide. The statement has to **authentically represent you**, so treat the generated version as a starting point to adapt rather than a finished answer.
</Aside>

### Exploring paths

Clicking **Explore paths** reveals career possibilities tailored to your career identity, where every role shown draws on at least some of the skills you selected. A central filter customises the results: unchecking Experiences and Education, for example, makes the tool prioritise roles based purely on your skills and interests, which surfaces a wider variety of positions.

Opening a single role, Financial Analyst in the demo, gives you the job description, the average salary and the level of degree typically required. Scrolling further shows how the skills you identified overlap with that role, what a day in the life looks like, and the areas you could work on to be a stronger match for that field.

## A career changer's story

Atlantis is a forensic scientist turned project manager, living in Georgia. Her account is the module's reality check on how a real transition feels.

<ul>
<li><strong>The trigger was life, not ambition.</strong> Finding out she was going to be a mother, she wanted the flexibility to work from home and still be there for her son.</li>
<li><strong>The first round failed completely.</strong> She applied and got nothing back, and she describes the repeated rejection as a blow to the ego. She kept going anyway.</li>
<li><strong>The gap was credentials, not capability.</strong> Looking back, she had her science degrees and her work experience, but no certificate and no evidence targeted at the roles she wanted.</li>
<li><strong>An organisation that helps mothers upskill</strong> and return to or change careers pointed her to the Google Career Certificates, and she enrolled in project management.</li>
<li><strong>The second round worked.</strong> Applying much as before, the silence stopped. She landed an interview, and the job, roughly four to five months after finishing the certificate.</li>
<li><strong>A mentor did the reframing.</strong> A second set of eyes on her resume spotted several skills that transferred at a glance. Once she saw them, she could name them herself: analytical, able to problem solve, a researcher by nature and therefore good at document gathering, all of which sit squarely inside project management work.</li>
</ul>

<Aside type="note">
**Her advice to anyone feeling stuck**, returning to work or mid-change: believe in yourself, remember who you are, and take the time to reflect on what you actually want. Have a vision for how you want your life to be, and judge it on your life as a whole rather than on one job or one career you happened to hold, because most people have more to offer than they realise.
</Aside>
