## The case: a quality brand at a crossroads

| | CERMEDES today |
|---|---|
| **Business** | High-quality corded DIY power tools: drills, hammer drills, grinders |
| **Size** | About €30.7M revenue, 85 employees (61 in production), one site in Hamburg producing about 480,000 units a year |
| **Products** | Corded only: no cordless line, no brushless motors, no smart functions, no shared battery platform |
| **Prices** | Mid-premium, €42 to €85 wholesale: above budget imports, slightly below the leading brands |
| **Channels** | 70% of revenue through wholesalers, 30% through specialty retail; no web shop, no marketplace presence, no CRM |
| **Market** | German power tools growing about 3.7% a year to around $3.4 billion by 2031; cordless already about 81% of the market |

**The diagnosis.** CERMEDES' engineering quality and *Made in Germany* reputation are real strengths, but it was losing competitiveness on five fronts: an ageing product line, limited innovation, pricing pressure, untapped trends and dependence on traditional channels. It also overlooked growing segments, such as women DIYers, tech-savvy DIYers, older craftspeople and facility maintenance firms.

## How we worked: the seminar as a consulting engagement

<Steps>

1. **Situation analysis.** Industry overview, trends and the competitive landscape, then SWOT with a TOWS matrix (SO, WO, ST and WT strategies), a 5C analysis and a resource-based (VRIO) view of what CERMEDES can build on.

2. **Customers and value.** Segment identification and selection, personas for the priority segments, a business model canvas, a product life-cycle assessment and a sharper value proposition.

3. **Going international.** A yes-or-no case for internationalisation, a market-selection process and an Uppsala-style rollout, prioritising markets in four waves: Austria and Flemish-speaking Belgium first, then the Netherlands and French-speaking Belgium, then Switzerland and Scandinavia, and France and the UK last.

4. **Pricing and profitability.** A product-by-product contribution analysis, the volume-versus-margin trade-off, what to do with loss-making products, and whether price increases are viable.

5. **The pitch.** Situation, problem and resolution for the CEO, leading with the business problem: declining sales, outdated technology and rising competition.

6. **The final report and the project plan.** The full RE:CHARGE strategy, its financial case and the plan to deliver it: charter, work breakdown structure, RACI, communication plan, Gantt chart, stakeholder matrix and risk register.

</Steps>

### The pricing insight

The contribution analysis showed CERMEDES was **losing money on its cheapest tool**: the 500 W drill sold at €42 against €43.06 of direct costs, a negative margin, while the 2000 W hammer drill earned more than €27 per unit (about 32%). An economic value estimation against a leading competitor's equivalent drill put the value-based price at about €44, and a 5% increase on the top drill (€65 to €68 wholesale) could add about €300,000 a year in profit if volume held. The lesson: stop discounting the brand at the bottom and price the strong products on the value they deliver.

## What we recommended

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Product" icon="puzzle">
    Relaunch a cost-efficient **CoreLine**, introduce the **SmartEco** cordless line through a cross-brand battery alliance, and develop a **Pro Series** of smart tools for professional users. Add ergonomic, lighter designs and phase out overlapping models.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Price" icon="list-format">
    Move from cost-plus to **value-based pricing** with good-better-best tiers (Basic, Advanced, Pro X), psychological price points and bundles, cordless tools about 10% below the market leader, and no reactive price wars.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Place" icon="random">
    Launch a **web shop and Amazon** presence, pilot shop-in-shop displays with large DIY chains, keep pro tools with dealers to avoid channel conflict, and set up a European logistics hub with a fulfilment partner.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Promotion" icon="comment">
    Reposition the brand under **Tools That Last**: durability, inclusivity and repairability, told digital-first through tutorials, influencers and real users, with marketing raised to 8-10% of sales and a 40/30/20/10 media mix.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Enablers" icon="setting">
    A **hybrid manufacturing** model (final assembly and motor quality checks in Hamburg, casings and batteries outsourced, automation at home), a **CRM** for customer data and after-sales, and a **phased EU expansion**.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Why it fits" icon="approve-check-circle">
    It builds on what CERMEDES already does well, quality and German engineering, while closing the gaps that cost it customers: cordless, smart features, direct channels and a clear brand story.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## The investment case

| Pillar | Cost | Expected return | Breakeven | Feasibility |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **CoreLine relaunch** | €250K | 18% over 18 months | Year 2 | Very high |
| **SmartEco cordless line** | €500K | 8-9% over 8 years | Year 8 | High |
| **CERMEDES Pro Series** | €1.8M | 35% by year 5 | Year 4 | Medium |
| **Digital after-sales & CRM** | €400K | 20% plus savings and lifetime value | Year 3 | High |
| **Manufacturing upgrade** | €2.5M | 20% long-term cost savings | Year 5 | Medium |
| **Total** | **under €6M** | | | |

The Pro Series targets a high-margin niche, property maintenance firms in north-western Europe, with co-developed cordless and diagnostic tools and multi-year supply contracts. A separate, more detailed roadmap for that sector to 2030 is costed at €3.6 million, with an average 25-30% return and breakeven in years four to five, aiming to make CERMEDES a top-three brand there. For SmartEco, the unit economics were worked out in detail: about €50.50 production cost against an €80 wholesale price, a 36.9% gross margin, and a breakeven of about 96,000 units a year.

## Going international, step by step

The CEO had ruled out international growth for now, so the plan respects that: a year of internal readiness first, then expansion from late 2026 following the **Uppsala model**, starting in close, low-risk markets and moving outward as knowledge and commitment grow.

| Phase | Markets | How |
|---|---|---|
| **Phase 1 · years 1-2** | Austria, Switzerland, the Netherlands, Belgium | Distributors, multilingual e-commerce, retail alliances |
| **Phase 2 · year 3** | Sweden, Denmark, Norway | Amazon, local online retailers and agents, then distributors |
| **Phase 3 · year 4 and later** | France, the UK, Eastern Europe | Joint ventures or licensing, localised e-stores |

The international programme is planned at €5.6 million over 2026-2030 with an average return of 24-30%, aiming for 25% of sales from outside Germany by year five. New tools launch and prove themselves in Germany first and reach other markets six to twelve months later.

**The literature review** followed a five-step systematic process: keywords, a Boolean search of Web of Science (3,738 articles from leading international business journals, 2014-2025), citation and usage filters (396 articles), relevance scoring down to 30 articles, and a content analysis. The most used theories were the Uppsala model, transaction cost economics, the eclectic (OLI) paradigm and the resource-based view, and one of the clearest research gaps was the limited attention to non-traditional entry modes.

### My part: non-traditional entry modes

I presented the section that picks up that gap, based on Brouthers, Chen, Li and Shaheer (2022) in the *Journal of International Business Studies*. Besides exporting, licensing or building a subsidiary, firms can now enter a market in four less physical ways:

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Capital access" icon="star">
    Entering a market financially rather than physically: listing shares, issuing bonds or raising venture capital abroad. Low cost and low control, with no subsidiary needed.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Innovation outposts" icon="magnifier">
    A small team in a foreign innovation hotspot that scouts ideas and technologies through startups and universities. It enters to import knowledge, not to sell.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Virtual presence" icon="laptop">
    Serving foreign customers purely online, through platforms and cross-border delivery, with no local offices, distributors or partners.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Managed ecosystems" icon="puzzle">
    Extending a multi-sided digital platform into a country so that independent local providers and customers transact on it, with no physical assets.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>
