## The problem: three bottlenecks

At a port, containers selected for non-intrusive inspection pass through a scanner on their truck. The scan image is read for anything abnormal, and only then is the container released. The control itself was sound. The way the work was organised around it was not.

The cost showed up as a long queue of trucks at the scanner: slower cargo release for traders, congestion in the terminal, and constant pressure on officers to hurry a check that matters for enforcement.

## How I approached it

<Steps>

1. **Walk the process and time it.** I followed containers from arrival to release and timed each step. Most of the 6-7 minutes was not scanning or image analysis at all; it was waiting, and paper moving between floors.

2. **Separate value from waste.** The scan and the reading of the image are the control, and they had to stay. Carrying receipts, waiting for them and re-endorsing them added nothing.

3. **Find the real constraint.** The bottleneck was not the scanner but the hand-off between the two officers. Any improvement elsewhere would just move the queue.

4. **Redesign around one window.** I planned the reorientation of the set-up so that both systems, the scan control and the image analysis, sat in a single room, with one point where a container is cleared.

5. **Fix the information upstream.** The unit needed to know what was coming before the trucks arrived, so the list of containers marked for scanning was made available as soon as a vessel came in.

6. **Digitise the control.** Paper registers were replaced by a simple web form, with the results checked automatically against the scan list.

7. **Settle it into routine.** The new way of working was explained to the officers, tried out on real shifts and adjusted until it ran as the normal procedure.

</Steps>

## The solution: three changes

### 1. Know what is coming

    <span class="arrow">→</span>

As soon as a vessel came in, the list of its containers marked for scanning was given to the unit. Officers knew how many containers to expect and which ones, so the right number of officers could be on duty, and the list became the reference for checking that nothing was missed.

### 2. One room, one window

<figure class="sc-fig pj-shot">
  <img src="/projects/pravah/layout.svg" alt="Schematic of the scanning set-up before and after. Before: a paper receipt travelled from the ground-floor scanner control room up to the image analysis room on another floor and back, while trucks queued in the scanner lane. After: scan control and image analysis share one room and the container is cleared at a single window." loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>The set-up before and after the reorganisation. Schematic, not to scale.</figcaption>
</figure>

Both systems were moved into a single room. The officer at the scan control and the officer reading the image now work side by side: the image appears on the next desk the moment the scan is done, and the container is cleared from one window. No receipt has to leave the room, so the round trip, and the wait that came with it, disappeared. The two-officer check stayed exactly as it was; only the walking went.

### 3. A register that checks itself

<figure class="sc-fig pj-shot">
  <img src="/projects/pravah/register.svg" alt="Illustration of the digital register: a web form for the vessel, container number, scanning status and officer, and a sheet that matches every entry against the vessel's scan list, showing how many containers are scanned, referred and still to scan. Container numbers are invented." loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>The digital register: a form for the officers, and a sheet that reconciles every entry with the vessel's scan list. Illustrative, with invented container numbers.</figcaption>
</figure>

Using Microsoft Forms, I set up a web form in which officers simply enter the container number they are scanning and its scanning status. The responses land in an Excel sheet that is connected to the vessel's scan list, so every entry is matched automatically by container number. What has been scanned, what was referred for examination and what is still outstanding shows up straight away, with no registers to go through by hand.

## In operations terms

This was a small project, but it applies classic operations thinking. Each of the old problems maps onto a recognised form of waste in lean management:

| Waste | Where it appeared | What removed it |
|---|---|---|
| **Transport** | Receipts carried between floors | Both systems in one room |
| **Waiting** | Trucks and officers waiting for paperwork | The image reaches officer 2 the moment it is taken |
| **Motion** | Officers and staff walking between floors | One room, one window |
| **Over-processing** | Repeated handwritten endorsements | A single entry in a form |
| **Defects** | Containers missed or wrongly recorded in manual registers | Automatic matching against the scan list |
| **Unused capacity** | Officers rostered without knowing the workload | Advance scan lists for every vessel |

Capacity, not effort, was the real issue. At 6-7 minutes a container, one scanner could clear only about 9 or 10 containers an hour; at under a minute, the same scanner could clear around 60. Once its capacity comfortably exceeded the rate at which trucks arrived, the queue had nothing left to build on.

## Value delivered

- **Faster clearance:** from 6-7 minutes to 45 seconds to a minute per container, about 85% less time.
- **No more queues:** the line of trucks waiting at the scanner cleared, easing congestion and speeding up the release of cargo.
- **Control kept intact:** every image is still read by a second officer before release; the process got faster without weakening enforcement.
- **People matched to the work:** advance scan lists let the unit roster officers to the expected volume.
- **Complete, provable scanning:** for every vessel, it is clear at a glance which marked containers have been scanned, referred or are still outstanding, with a digital record behind each entry.
- **Almost no cost:** a change of layout and procedure plus tools that were already available, Microsoft Forms and Excel; no new system to buy.
