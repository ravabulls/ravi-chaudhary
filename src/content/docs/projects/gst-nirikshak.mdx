## The problem

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Data in many places" icon="document">
    Returns, statements, e-way bills, third-party reports and legal notifications arrive as PDF, Excel, JSON, CSV and text, each in its own layout.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Manual matching" icon="random">
    Comparing sales with tax paid, credit claimed with credit available, or goods billed with goods moved was done by hand in spreadsheets: slow and easy to get wrong.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Hard to explain" icon="magnifier">
    A figure in a report could not easily be traced back to its rows and its legal basis, so results were hard to repeat and harder to defend.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Networks hide in tables" icon="puzzle">
    Fake-invoice networks pass credit through chains and loops of firms. Seeing them needs graph analysis that spreadsheets cannot do.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Confidential by nature" icon="setting">
    Tax data is confidential, so cloud tools were not an option. Whatever helped had to run on the officer's own machine.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## What I did

<Steps>

1. **Understand the work.** I mapped the investigator's workflow from finding a lead to computing a demand, and the questions asked at each step.

2. **Study the data.** I studied the structure of the returns, statements and reports the work depends on: their fields, layouts and how they relate. Structure only; no data was copied.

3. **Specify.** I wrote a detailed build specification (version 3.1): the data model, every check with its legal basis, the screens, the security model and the acceptance tests.

4. **Build with AI assistance.** I planned fifteen milestones and directed AI coding agents (Claude Code) working in parallel under a written contract that fixed the shared files, the privacy rules and the testing standard. I reviewed and merged each milestone and integrated the result.

5. **Prove it.** I built a synthetic data generator that plants known problems, 507 automated tests with expected values, and a demo zone of 20 cases that exercises every module.

</Steps>

## Connecting the data: the heart of the project

The core idea is simple: clean every source into one case store and link it on a small set of shared keys. Once the keys line up, each analysis becomes a join instead of a manual hunt.

| Kind of source | Examples | What it adds |
|---|---|---|
| **Own returns** | Sales return, summary return with tax paid, annual return | What the business declares, month by month and for the year |
| **Supplier statements** | Auto-drafted purchase statements built from suppliers' filings | The credit the business is actually entitled to, invoice by invoice |
| **Third-party reports** | Tax deducted by government buyers, e-commerce collections, income-tax statements | What others report paying the business: an independent check on turnover |
| **Goods movement** | E-way bills, the public PIN code directory | Whether billed goods were plausibly moved |
| **Registration data** | Registration details across the area | The wider population, for screening and peer comparison |
| **Law** | Rates, exemptions, reverse charge, blocked credit, cess, each with its effective date | The correct treatment on any given date, so checks are time-aware |
| **Books and finance** | Trial balance, sales register, bank statements | Turnover in the books against the returns; money flows |
| **Trade** | Shipping bills and bills of entry | Exports and imports against refunds and credit claimed |

Files are recognised by their headings rather than fixed row positions, so the app copes with banner rows, merged headers and small layout changes.

| Key | What it links | The question it makes answerable |
|---|---|---|
| **GSTIN** | The registration number across returns, e-way bills and third-party reports | Does what the business declared match what others reported about it? |
| **PAN** | All registrations of one business, and its income-tax data | Is one business spread across several registrations? |
| **Period** | Months and financial years across returns and books | In which months does a gap appear, and is interest due? |
| **Invoice + supplier** | The purchase register against suppliers' filings | Which credit claims have no matching invoice, or are claimed twice? |
| **Vehicle + time** | E-way bills against movement records | Could the goods on this bill actually have travelled? |
| **PIN code** | Addresses to approximate locations | Is the declared distance plausible for this route? |
| **Product code + date** | Products to the law master and to peers | Was the right rate applied on that date? Is a firm far out of line with its peers? |
| **Name, address, phone** | Fuzzy matching across registrations and past cases | Are supposedly independent firms actually linked? Was this lead already examined? |

## A walkthrough on synthetic data

The app ships with a demonstration story. It shows how each extra source adds a layer of proof. All firms and figures are synthetic.

<Steps>

1. **Spot it.** Population screens rank a group of closely linked new registrations at the top of the worklist, each with a score and the reasons behind it.

2. **Connect it.** Supply links from returns, purchase statements and e-way bills become a network of 18 units. Graph search finds a ring of four firms passing credit in a loop and two firms with credit but no real purchases, all feeding one beneficiary (the picture at the top of this page). The analysis takes about a tenth of a second.

3. **Test it.** The ring's 95 e-way bills are tested for whether the goods could physically have moved: 36 consignments fail, and the tax on them is computed exactly, with a clearly labelled estimate for the bills not tested.

4. **Corroborate it.** Financial records show money moving in a loop between two of the firms, matching the pattern in the network.

5. **Explain it.** Each finding becomes an issue with its legal provision, its method (exact or estimate), its confidence and its source rows. The officer drafts the report with annexures; a supervisor approves it before it is registered.

6. **Act on it.** For a related case, the app computes tax by month, interest day by day, payments already made, penalty options and the statutory deadlines.

</Steps>

## What the application does

Twelve modules follow the investigator's workflow, from deciding whom to look at to computing a demand and reporting on it.

  <Card title="Return analysis" icon="list-format">

## Built to be trusted

In tax enforcement, a number is only useful if it can be defended. The design follows from that.

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Explainable by design" icon="magnifier">
    Every figure traces back to its source rows, the rule that produced it and the legal provision. Each check states its method (exact, proxy or estimate) and its confidence. Estimates print their assumptions, and changing one recomputes the figure. Officers' decisions on findings survive a re-run of the checks.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Private and tamper-evident" icon="setting">
    Fully offline: outside network connections are blocked while the app runs. Every imported file is fingerprinted, the audit log is hash-chained, backups are signed, and case packages are digitally sealed so any change shows. The web layer is hardened, and the app runs without admin rights.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Tested properly" icon="approve-check-circle">
    A synthetic data generator plants known problems, so every check is tested against expected values: 507 automated tests, including property-based tests and performance benchmarks. No real taxpayer data in the code, tests or documentation.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Made to be used" icon="laptop">
    A browser interface with light and dark themes, a guided first-run tour, a "How to" page, a 22-chapter manual built into the app, and a one-click demo zone of 20 synthetic cases for training and presentations.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## Technology and architecture

A local web application: a Python server on the officer's own PC and the normal browser as the interface. No internet, no cloud, no installation rights needed.

| Layer | Tools | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Analytics engine** | DuckDB (embedded SQL), PyArrow | Fast columnar analysis of large extracts on one PC, with no database server |
| **Graph and matching** | Own graph algorithms, RapidFuzz | Trading cycles, clusters and chain levels; fuzzy name and address matching |
| **File handling** | python-calamine, pypdfium2, JSON and CSV readers | Reading returns and reports from Excel, PDF, JSON and CSV |
| **Documents** | python-docx, XlsxWriter, Pillow | Reports, notices, annexures and network pictures in the office's own formats |
| **Web server** | FastAPI, Uvicorn, Jinja2 | A testable server with server-rendered pages |
| **Interface** | HTMX, Alpine.js, Tabulator, Cytoscape.js | Interactive tables and network pictures, all bundled locally |
| **Security** | cryptography (Ed25519), Argon2, SHA-256 | Signatures, sealed packages, passwords and file fingerprints |
| **Quality** | pytest, Hypothesis, pytest-socket | Unit, property-based and offline-enforced tests |
| **Packaging** | PyInstaller, portable build | Runs on locked-down Windows PCs without admin rights |

### How I built it

I treated the project as a product. I started from the officers' workflow and the shape of the data, wrote a specification precise enough to build and test against, and split it into fifteen milestones. The build was AI-assisted: I directed Claude Code agents working on separate branches in parallel, under a written contract that fixed the shared files, the privacy rules and the testing standard, *correct before complete*: every check needs expected values on synthetic data. I reviewed and merged each milestone, then integrated everything into one app with a guided tour, a manual and a demo zone.
